Homes abandoned, harvests failing: Haiti's farmers rebuild amid violence, hunger and El Niño
Years of insecurity, violence perpetrated by murderous armed gangs and economic instability, combined with displacement, poor agricultural performance and climate shocks, have left millions of Haitians struggling to meet their food needs.
It's estimated that 6.35 million people, roughly half the population, will not get enough to eat between March and June 2027.
Almost two million of them will be at emergency levels, where families have little or nothing to eat and may have to sell the things they depend on for a living just to get food.
Combination of shocks
A series of overlapping crises are currently shaping food insecurity.
More than 1.5 million people have been forced to flee their homes due to the threat of gang violence, and many can no longer work or grow food.
Insecurity is also disrupting farming communities and market supply chains.
The El Niño weather phenomenon is now a third threat.
It is bringing droughts and floods that are reducing harvests and affecting farmers' ability to prepare for the next planting season.
The number of people affected by drought conditions is projected to reach four million by December 2026.
What do the farmers say?
"Rain isn't falling," said Dieula Célestin. "You work the soil, but there is no rain. You plant your crops, and you end up with nothing to harvest."
Délivrance Dovily lost her animals: "Because of the country's crisis, we struggle just to eat. We used to raise livestock to help pay for school, the animals used to support us. But because of the crisis and diseases that came through, we lost them."
Less food is being grown in Haiti, and ongoing insecurity means it cannot easily be transported to the people who need it most.
What is the UN doing?
Short term: Keeping people alive.
The World Food Programme (WFP) provides emergency food and cash to vulnerable families, including those displaced by violence.
This year, working alongside the Haitian authorities, it has supported 2.6 million people.
Its programmes also include school meals for more than 620,000 students and malnutrition prevention for young children and support to the government for the establishment of a social safety net system.
In the case of extreme weather, such as Hurricane Melissa which struck the south of the country one year ago, pre-positioned stocks can provide rice, beans and cooking oil to up to 300,000 people for one month.
The UN Humanitarian Air Service (UNHAS) flies aid workers in and out of the capital Port-au-Prince, and to remote locations across the country, enabling humanitarians to reach the most vulnerable Haitians, wherever they are.
The UN's Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) works alongside WFP during emergencies and has provided support including livestock, such as poultry but also goats in order to help farmers immediately generate cash and food to sustain their own family.
"The animals are interesting because we can distribute them at any time. So, we do not have to wait for the planting season and farmers can immediately use them," said FAO country chief, Pierre Vauthier.
Délivrance Dovily agrees: "Receiving these goats helps boost our activities and strengthen our capacity, giving us more resources to support our children with school and meet their needs."
Longer term: Building sustainability.
In partnership with national authorities, WFP, FAO and the International Fund for Agricultural Development (IFAD), another UN agency working on rural poverty, are investing in programmes that help vulnerable communities to withstand shocks and protect livelihoods.
If people are more self-sufficient, they will depend less on humanitarian assistance.
WFP is rehabilitating water-management infrastructure and rural roads. It also provides insurance against crop losses from drought or excessive rain. Some 74 per cent of its school meals use food sourced directly from local farmers.
Humanitarian or development?
Both.
WFP and FAO seek to connect immediate life-saving interventions with responding to the root causes of hunger, such as support for smallholder farmers and resilience.
"As Haiti faces a complex and multifaceted crisis, it's more important than ever for UN agencies to work together with the government, not only to provide life-saving emergency food assistance, but also to scale up long-term investments that help communities build hope and resilience, withstand shocks and protect livelihoods from events like El Niño," said Erwan Rumen, WFP Acting Country Director.
Food aid keeps people alive today. Seeds, animals, roads and insurance make families less dependent on it tomorrow.
Who's who
- WFP: Delivers emergency food and cash, school meals and shared logistics for the entire humanitarian community, while working to build resilient communities.
- FAO: Supports families with seeds, inputs and livestock to quickly produce their own food locally, for their own consumption or to generate income.
- IFAD: Funds programmes that raise smallholder farmers' productivity and incomes.
