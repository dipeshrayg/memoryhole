'Far too much failure': UN deputy chief's final call to end violence against women
UN Deputy Secretary-General Amina Mohammed warned on Tuesday that another woman or girl would be killed by a partner or family member before she finished speaking, as she called for justice for survivors and consequences for perpetrators.
"Have we made progress? Yes. But we also have tolerated far too much failure," said Ms. Mohammed, who was marking the International Day for the Elimination of Violence against Women for the last time as Deputy Secretary-General.
The event on the eve of the official launch of 16 Days of Activism against Gender-Based Violence, heard calls for justice systems that work for survivors, accountability from technology companies and funding for women's rights organizations.
On display at the event were 129 orange flags, one for each woman and girl killed daily by an intimate partner or family member, according to new UN data released on Tuesday.
'Deliver on commitments'
Earlier this year, at the 70th session of the Commission on the Status of Women, Member States agreed on steps to strengthen access to justice - from repealing discriminatory laws to investigating and prosecuting violence, including online - Ms. Mohammed said.
"We must now deliver on those commitments so that women can rebuild their lives in safety and dignity," she continued, adding that governments, the UN and technology companies all share that responsibility - the latter in "answering for the violence on their platforms".
Through the Spotlight Initiative - a partnership between the UN and the European Union to eliminate all forms of violence against women by 2030 - conviction rates for gender-based violence have doubled across 13 countries, she said. Yet more than a third of women's rights organizations surveyed have had to suspend or close programmes because of funding cuts.
She also called on men to challenge the attitudes and social norms that allow violence to continue. "You have influence in ways that others do not. Use it," she said.
Technology must 'not shield abuse'
"This violence is not random. It is not a private matter," General Assembly President Khalilur Rahman said in a video message.
He set out three priorities for Member States:
- Funding justice and protection;
- Making technology "serve justice, not shield abuse";
- Holding institutions accountable.
Governments must ensure the law protects women and girls online as well as offline, and technology companies must make it easy to report abuse, he said.
"Laws matter. Enforcement is the real test."
UN Women Executive Director Sima Bahous warned in her own video message that digital technologies and artificial intelligence (AI) are "enabling abuse faster than laws and justice systems can respond".
Only one in five countries provides legal aid, she said, and 89 per cent of women's rights organisations report reduced access to services because of cuts.
Solutions are working, she added: the number of countries with domestic violence legislation rose from 12 in 1995 to 104 in 2025.
"Women and girls must wait no longer."
'Believe in women'
In a panel discussion, French advocate David Pelicot, who works to engage men in ending violence against women, said justice fails "when a woman doesn't dare to speak out because she fears that she won't be believed".
His mother, Gisèle Pelicot, waived her right to anonymity in 2024 as the survivor in a multiple rape case in which her former husband and 49 co-defendants were convicted.
"Justice starts when we believe in women," he said.
