From a ship frozen into Arctic ice, a warning to world leaders: Trust the science
Drifting through the polar darkness, a team of scientists is documenting a rapidly changing Arctic. As sea ice retreats and oil tankers move in, a conversation with the UN chief reveals why the battle for climate science matters far beyond the North Pole.
Somewhere in the darkness of the Arctic Ocean, 12 people and a dog named Örkki are drifting right now across the frozen sea, with little say over where they are going.
Their home for the winter is Tara Polar Station, a 26-metre-long French research vessel that departed Norway in August with an unusual purpose: to become trapped in ice.
Locked into a floating sheet of sea ice above more than 2,500 metres of water, the station moves wherever the winds and currents carry it. There is no destination to steer towards, and in the middle of the polar night, no sunlight to mark the passing days.
The team is taking part in Tara Polaris I, the first of 10 planned expeditions in a 20-year international programme to study the transformation of the Arctic Ocean. What they are already witnessing has caught the attention of UN Secretary-General António Guterres.
What happens in the Arctic doesn't stay in the Arctic.
Ahead of the COP31 UN climate conference, the UN chief spoke this week by video link to the expedition's chief scientist, Maxime Geoffroy, asking what message he should take to world leaders about an Arctic which is warming three to four times faster than the global average.
"I think that, unfortunately, the political leadership in this world is not yet entirely aware of how serious the situation is," the UN chief said.
Mr. Geoffroy's message was simple: "What happens in the Arctic doesn't stay in the Arctic."
The changes unfolding beneath the expedition's feet could have far-reaching consequences for the world's oceans, climate and biodiversity, he warned.
A disappearing world
The mission draws inspiration from Norwegian explorer Fridtjof Nansen, who famously allowed his vessel to freeze into Arctic sea ice in 1893 and drift with the currents.
More than 130 years later, Mr. Geoffroy and his colleagues are following in his footsteps, with one striking difference: there is considerably less ice in their way.
The expedition spent three weeks navigating along Russia's northern coast before heading into the Arctic ice pack in search of a suitable floe (a large flat piece of floating ice) for its research station.
"We reached 82 degrees north just through leads," Mr. Geoffroy said, referring to channels of open water between the floes.
When Nansen made his voyage, his vessel became trapped in ice hundreds of kilometres farther south. Now, open stretches of water allowed Mr. Geoffroy's team to travel much deeper before becoming icebound.
But the retreating ice is only part of the story.
The planet's 'mirror' is melting
The Secretary-General asked Mr. Geoffroy about one of his greatest fears for the future:
"One of the things that has scared me most was the perspective that in a number of years we might have no ice in the Arctic, during summer."
Arctic sea ice plays a vital role in regulating the Earth's temperature and keeping the planet cool.
"This large ice sheet on which we are drifting, when the sun comes back, acts as a mirror," the scientist explained.
Like a giant reflector, the white ice sends much of the Sun's energy back into space. But as it melts, the darker ocean beneath absorbs that heat instead, warming the water and accelerating the loss of ice.
The consequences are far reaching. Ocean currents carry heat far beyond the North Pole, influencing marine ecosystems and climate patterns across the planet.
As Arctic ice disappears, these circulation systems could be disrupted, with consequences scientists are still struggling to predict.
"We have models, but we don't know when, how, and what will be the exact impacts," Mr. Geoffroy acknowledged.
The central Arctic remains poorly studied. Firsthand observations are crucial to improving climate models and predicting what lies ahead.
From ancient life to an uncertain future
But the team's questions go far beyond climate forecasts, reaching into one of humanity's oldest mysteries: the origins of life.
Could life on Earth have begun in sea ice?
Mr. Geoffroy pointed to organisms that survive extreme cold thanks to antifreeze proteins, and to the intriguing hypothesis that frozen environments may have played a role in life's earliest beginnings.
Other researchers on board are tackling a more practical mystery: how many fish live in the central Arctic Ocean?
Despite growing commercial interest, researchers still don't know whether its waters could sustain fisheries without threatening fragile ecosystems.
"To understand the loss of biodiversity and protection of biodiversity, you need to know what is there," the scientist explained.
The research programme brings together 80 scientists and some 220 research protocols to investigate everything from how marine life responds to warming to the ocean's role in storing carbon.
The Secretary-General, a longstanding advocate for ocean protection, smiled when he heard Mr. Geoffroy describe the international effort as a "miniature United Nations for Arctic science".
But while researchers race to understand the disappearing ice, others are already looking to profit from its retreat.
New shipping routes, new battles
On their way north, the scientists encountered another unmistakable sign of change: oil tankers navigating the Northeast Passage along Russia's Arctic coast.
The retreating ice makes the route more accessible but increases the risks of pollution and accidents.
"I can guarantee you that we saw a lot of oil tankers going through that passage," Mr. Geoffroy told the Secretary-General. "So, it's not just a forecast. It is happening."
An oil spill in these waters could be disastrous. Petroleum can become trapped in the ice for long periods, threatening marine life and making cleanup exceptionally difficult.
Commercial interests are not the only ones turning their attention north. Guterres pointed to tensions surrounding Greenland and the growing strategic importance of the Arctic, warning that countries increasingly viewing the region through a security lens could threaten the very environment scientists are trying to understand.
For Mr. Geoffroy, whose expedition brings together researchers from 12 countries, the question was whether science could help bridge those geopolitical divides.
"I think that is a central battle we have now. The battle for truth and the battle for science," the UN chief replied.
The Secretary-General accused powerful economic interests, particularly those linked to fossil fuels, of seeking to obscure scientific evidence and undermine its role in public debate.
"We went from the climate denial to the climate [...] delay and 'green hushing'," Mr. Geoffroy observed, lamenting how climate action has slipped down political agendas despite decades of scientific warnings.
A sense of urgency, and fury
Before ending their conversation, the UN chief invited Mr. Geoffroy to imagine addressing world leaders gathering at COP31.
The scientist's message was clear:
"The findings of this science [...] need to be trusted, they need to be followed and they need to be supported."
For Guterres, the warning struck a personal chord. He recalled visits to Antarctica and the Himalayas, where he had witnessed glaciers retreating.
Those experiences, combined with the continued refusal of some political leaders to act, had left him with more than concern.
"All these things I think have created in me a sense of urgency and, to be honest, also a sense of fury," he admitted. "It's an existential battle."
In the Arctic, the researchers will continue their slow drift through the polar darkness, gathering evidence from a region changing before their eyes.
Örkki the dog will keep watch for polar bears while the team measures the ice, water and atmosphere. Thousands of kilometres away, world leaders will gather in Antalya, Türkiye at COP31 to negotiate how to confront the climate crisis.
And while the scientists have no control over where the ice carries them, the future they are documenting remains a matter of human choice.
