The Takeaway: UN General Assembly debate Day 4
From the Caribbean to the Pacific, leaders from small island States demanded climate justice, ocean protection and greater solidarity at the UN General Assembly on Friday. They warned that rising seas are already redrawing national boundaries, while the ripple effects of faraway wars are being felt on their shores. This edition of The Takeaway summarizes key points made during the fourth day of the annual high-level debate.
Why it matters: Small island States told the General Assembly that climate change is no longer a distant threat. It is damaging economies, threatening water and food security, and placing communities at risk.
- Tuvalu's Prime Minister Feleti Teo said sea-level rise was "a present reality", pointing to efforts to reclaim elevated, flood-free land on the capital island of Funafuti.
- Bahamas Prime Minister Philip Davis warned: "The sea does not negotiate. It does not read communiqués. And it does not recognize a veto."
- Leaders stressed that countries responsible for only a small share of global emissions are being forced to pay repeatedly for climate adaptation and disaster recovery.
Climate finance is about justice, not charity
The big picture: Leaders argued that the international financial system does not reflect the risks facing small island developing States.
- Saint Lucia's Prime Minister Philip J. Pierre described climate change as a direct threat to national security, economic stability and human survival, declaring: "Climate finance is not charity."
- Samoa's Deputy Prime Minister Mulipola Anarosa Ale Molioo called for climate funding that is accessible, responsive and free from conditions.
- Saint Vincent and the Grenadines Prime Minister Godwin Friday said small States "cannot continue borrowing merely to replace what climate-related disasters repeatedly destroy".
- Speakers urged the full use of the multidimensional vulnerability index so that financing decisions reflect countries' exposure to climate shocks, rather than relying primarily on income per person.
Ocean protection is national protection
What was said: For island and coastal States, the ocean is central to identity, security and economic survival.
- Tuvalu described itself as a "large ocean State", responsible for some 750,000 square kilometres of ocean despite having only around 26 square kilometres of land.
- Mauritius reaffirmed its commitment to the 1982 UN Convention on the Law of the Sea and called for prompt implementation of the High Seas Treaty.
- Solomon Islands Prime Minister Matthew Wale noted that Sustainable Development Goal 14 on life below water remains among the least funded SDGs.
- "The ocean is not simply a resource; it is our identity, our heritage and our future," he said.
Military spending highlights global disparities
Reality check: Speakers contrasted limited development and climate finance with rising global military expenditure.
- The Solomon Islands noted that the world spent more than $2.9 trillion on its militaries last year - 16 times the total amount of official development assistance.
- Mr. Wale said the world spends more on arms in fewer than 18 days than developed countries provide in climate finance over an entire year.
- Guinea-Bissau's Prime Minister Ilídio Vieira Té called for stronger multilateralism and international cooperation, warning that no country can confront today's challenges alone.
Water security is an existential concern
Beyond the islands: Water scarcity and transboundary water governance also emerged as major security issues.
- Egypt's Prime Minister Moustafa Kamal Madbouly described the Nile as his country's lifeline and said water security was "an existential issue that is not open to compromise".
- He argued that shared water resources should promote cooperation rather than conflict, while rejecting unilateral action and "fait accompli policies".
- Kazakhstan proposed creating an international water organization under UN auspices and called for a Member State-led process to strengthen global water governance.
Stirring the waters
The security concern: Island States warned that geopolitical competition and disruption of major waterways can quickly raise the cost of food, fuel and transportation.
- Maldives Vice President Hussain Mohamed Latheef said sovereignty can be weakened when external powers restrict a State's freedom to choose its own path.
- He warned that competition in the Indian Ocean must not constrain smaller countries, insisting that every State must be able to shape its future.
- Bahrain's Foreign Minister Abdullatif bin Rashid Al Zayani described waterways as arteries of development through which food, medicine and energy flow.
- Disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz, the Red Sea, the Gulf of Aden and the Bab Al Mandab Strait can affect families far beyond coastal States, he said.
Pacific nuclear testing impact
The unfinished legacy: Pacific leaders said communities continue to experience displacement, illness, contamination and loss decades after nuclear testing ended.
- Kiribati President Taneti Maamau reaffirmed support for the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons and an international trust fund for victims.
- Marshall Islands President Hilda Heine called on the United Nations to apologize for the harm caused by 67 nuclear weapons tests conducted in her country between 1946 and 1958, when it was a UN Strategic Trust Territory.
- "The lack of acknowledgment remains a moral failure," she said.
