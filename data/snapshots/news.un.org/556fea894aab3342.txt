Funding cuts raise risks of trafficking and exploitation for refugee children
Sharp reductions in humanitarian funding are putting refugees, particularly children, at increased risk of being trafficked, exploited or abused, while the systems that protect them are weakening.
That's according to two new studies published by the UN refugee agency, UNHCR, and Oxford University that highlight the urgent need for action.
They focus on Bangladesh and Ethiopia which each host more than one million refugees and asylum-seekers.
The reports found that when protection services and essential assistance such as shelter and education are slashed simultaneously, critical security measures are weakened, thus exposing already vulnerable refugees to exploitation, hazardous labour and trafficking.
'Easier to prey on'
Elizabeth Tan, UNHCR Director of International Protection and Solutions, noted that protection services, such as legal aid, safe spaces, psychosocial support, gender-based violence prevention and child protection, are among the strongest defences against trafficking and exploitation.
"When they are cut, vulnerabilities deepen, and traffickers find it easier to prey on people already at risk. Investing in protection now prevents future exploitation," she said.
Bangladesh is home to more than 1.1 million Rohingya refugees from Myanmar who live in the world's largest refugee settlement, located near the southeastern city of Cox's Bazar.
Bangladesh: Rise in trafficking
Detected trafficking cases there increased by 28 per cent between 2024 and 2025, with children accounting for 17 per cent of victims, up from 14 per cent.
Between January and September 2026, 411 victims of trafficking were identified and nearly 23 per cent were children.
Risks continue to grow as humanitarian resources shrink and needs rise. Between 2024 and 2025, the Rohingya refugee population in Bangladesh increased by roughly 17 per cent, or more than 170,000 people. At the same time, UNHCR's operational budget fell by more than eight per cent in 2025 and faced a 59 per cent funding gap.
Approximately 88 per cent of Rohingya households reported that their shelters were deteriorating and almost none of the new arrivals could be allocated their own housing, while safe water availability dropped from 33 litres per person per day to 23 litres.
Ethiopia: Reduced protection
In Ethiopia, funding cuts have significantly reduced protection services for refugee children, who account for more than half, or 52 per cent, of the 1.1 million refugees in the country. Most are from South Sudan, Somalia, Eritrea and Sudan, including 31,000 children who have been separated from their parents or guardians.
In 2025, UNHCR's Ethiopia operation was less than one-third funded, while only 18 per cent of planned child protection requirements were covered.
Between 2024 and 2025, assistance for family tracing and reunification support fell by more than half, from 4,071 to 1,887 children supported. Alternative care for unaccompanied children and family-strengthening programmes also declined by around half, while 57 primary schools serving some 110,000 refugee children were affected by funding shortfalls.
Young lives in danger
The report's authors said that recent field observations indicate a worrying rise in child labour among refugees, including in agriculture and informal mining areas. As a result, already vulnerable refugee children - particularly those who fled alone, were separated from their families, or girls - are more exposed to exploitation, sexual violence, child marriage and trafficking for forced labour and sexual exploitation.
"The results are very sobering, and a stark warning to the international community as to what may transpire if vital prevention and protection services for refugees remain under-resourced," said Professor Andrew Thompson of Oxford University.
Although the two studies conclude that sustained, flexible and predictable funding remains essential, UNHCR's global programmes for child protection were only 25 per cent funded as of July 2026, while efforts to prevent and respond to gender-based violence were only 24 per cent funded.
For the agency, the reports underscore that investment in refugee and child protection is not only a humanitarian imperative, but also a critical way of preventing conflict, strengthening resilience and finding durable solutions.
