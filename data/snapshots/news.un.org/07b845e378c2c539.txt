Empowering faith and Indigenous leaders to combat hate
Religious, traditional and Indigenous leaders need to be empowered to act as full partners in preventing conflict, not just praised for their role, speakers said Sunday at the opening of the Muscat Plan of Action Forum in Oman.
Key points
- The Muscat Plan of Action Forum was timed with the International Day for Tolerance, marked annually on 16 November
- Young people from more than 20 countries are taking part in a mediation training programme
- Workshops aim to produce a global framework for putting the plan into practice
UN officials, community leaders, Indigenous representatives and young people from around the world have gathered to find practical local, regional and international steps for carrying out the Muscat Plan of Action, launched last June by Oman, the United Nations and international partners.
The forum's theme, From international commitment to local impact: Prevention and peacebuilding, covers three areas: leadership and prevention, dialogue and cohesion and partnerships and implementation.

'Trust of communities'
Peace does not come simply from ending conflicts or signing agreements, Mohamed bin Said bin Khalfan Al-Maamari, Oman's Minister of Endowments and Religious Affairs, told leaders.
Instead, it rests on trust, shared values and a community's ability to manage its differences without hostility, Mr. Al-Mamari said.
"What we need today is for the legitimacy of institutions to be integrated with the trust of communities and for specialised knowledge to meet inherited wisdom," he said.
Such leaders know their communities and have their trust, he continued, so they can reach people that official channels often cannot.
Prevention before violence
Chaloka Beyani, the UN Special Adviser on the Prevention of Genocide, emphasised that traditional and Indigenous leaders must be involved in countering hate speech and incitement and in preventing mass atrocities.
In the same vein, former Finnish President Tarja Halonen warned that intolerance and hate speech deepen divisions and, in the worst cases, lead to atrocities. Prevention, she said, must begin long before conflict breaks out.
"Hate speech can make discrimination seem more acceptable and gradually turn neighbours into enemies," Ms. Halonen said, urging leaders to defend people targeted for their identity, beliefs or ethnic background.
She also praised the plan's focus on women and youth and called for close cooperation among governments, communities, international institutions, faith leaders and civil society.
'Harming others is harming oneself'
Sonia Astuhuaman Pardave leads Indigenous women from the Quechua people of the Wanka Nation and serves as women's counsellor at the organisation CAOI.
She said Indigenous philosophy rests on mutual giving, gratitude and respect for all life.
"Harming others is harming oneself," she said, adding that Indigenous peoples defend their rights through community organising, spirituality and legal action, not violence.
Youth as partners
Alongside the forum, young people from more than 20 countries are taking part in Oman's Harmonious Connections, a training course in religion and mediation.
Mohamed Elsanousi, Executive Director of the Network for Religious and Traditional Peacemakers, said many of the participants already work as peacebuilders in their own communities.
Florencio, from Colombia, has spent nearly 10 years helping underrepresented groups take part in international processes. He said young people are among those hit hardest by conflict but are also able to find solutions.
Kelly Ketchedjian, a Lebanese student of international relations, said the course covered gender-based violence and how to analyse conflicts.
"We are not just children of Lebanon, we are children of the world," she said.
From dialogue to action
The forum's workshops aim to produce a global implementation framework, along with practical steps at the regional, national and local levels.
The plan treats local communities as key partners in prevention.
It also calls for responses suited to each community's circumstances, with dialogue, mediation and confidence-building used to keep tensions from turning into conflict.
