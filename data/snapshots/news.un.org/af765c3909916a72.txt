'We escaped with only our lives': Fighting to give birth in war-torn Yemen
When fighting suddenly escalated around Taiz, in southwestern Yemen two months ago, midwife Anisa Fadhl had no time to pack a bag before she fled, leaving behind her home and her clinic. Both were later looted and destroyed.
"I would have packed my medical equipment and belongings, but the entire village fled the same day," she explained. "No transportation was available, and we escaped with only our lives."
Talking about her clinic, she says that "everything was looted."
"They stole chairs, mattresses and all electronic medical equipment - looters even carried away the examination beds," the midwife told UNFPA, the UN's sexual and reproductive health agency.
In Yemen, surges in conflict have displaced over 320,000 people since August - and more than 80,000 in October alone - while more than 22 million people were already estimated to need humanitarian assistance this year, according to the UN's migration organisation IOM.
Women most vulnerable
Women have been particularly vulnerable in these surges of fighting and displacement, with widespread reports of sexual violence.
Pregnant women and mothers also face particularly serious challenges.
Amongst those displaced, over 6,400 are mothers-to-be. This month, around 600 women are expected to give birth without basic sanitary or medical infrastructure, UNFPA estimates.
Around 1,500 women may require emergency obstetric and newborn care for potential complications, while fewer than two-thirds of health facilities are fully functional.
Yemen has one of the highest maternal mortality rates in the region, with three women dying every day from preventable pregnancy complications.
A midwife on the move
Despite the heat, lack of transport and shortage of safe drinking water, Ms. Fadhl continued caring for vulnerable people as she fled.
"My priority was providing rest for the children and pregnant women," she recalled. "While walking, a cargo truck passed by; we loaded some pregnant women, elderly women and children onto it."
Just two days after escaping her village and settling down in Aden, she returned to work.
Moving between displacement sites with severe shortages of essential medicines and minimal medical supplies, she helps deliver babies, conducts prenatal and postnatal checkups, and advises mothers on breastfeeding, infant nutrition and hygiene.
She also delivers psychosocial support and family planning services to women and girls whose access to healthcare was severed long before the latest escalation.
"Early marriage is widespread," Ms. Fadhl explained. "Girls aged 20 often already have two children without ever receiving birth spacing counselling or family planning services."
Flour-sack thread
Among those Ms. Fadhl helped was Um Mohammed, who fled the fighting in Taiz while heavily pregnant, walking under the scorching sun with her young children.
"I was terrified of losing my [pregnancy], getting injured by shelling, or having something terrible happen to my children," she told UNFPA.
After reaching safety, the family found shelter with a host family. But just hours later, Ms. Mohammed went into labour.
Ms. Fadhl, who was among the crowd of displaced people sheltering nearby, stepped up.
"She delivered my baby smoothly with zero tools," Ms. Mohammed recalled. "She tied my baby's cord with a flour-sack thread and cut it with a razor blade borrowed from a neighbour."
"It was the hardest delivery of my life," Ms. Mohammed told UNFPA. "Not just because of the physical pain, but because of fear and anxiety."
Critically underfunded
Years prior, UNFPA had trained Ms. Fadhl as a midwife and helped provide the initial medical equipment she needed to establish her practice.
As of 30 October, the agency had reached nearly 180,000 people with emergency reproductive healthcare and protection services, deploying mobile health teams, arranging referrals to specialist facilities and mobilising community midwives.
In 2026, UNFPA is appealing for $71.9 million to maintain its critical support for women and girls, but to date only one-quarter is funded.
