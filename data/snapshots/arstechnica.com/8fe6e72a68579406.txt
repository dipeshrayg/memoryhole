Homo erectus was walking around mainland Asia at least 60,000 years more recently than archaeologists thought. A handful of teeth from two caves in southern China's Guangxi Province just filled in a significant gap in the fossil record of a region that's vital to the story of how our genus, Homo, took over the world.
The chemical makeup of the teeth's enamel suggests that, over the course of their lives, these Homo erectus moved across surprisingly large distances and made their living in dense tropical rainforests-an environment we'd previously assumed that only our species had ever gained a real foothold in. And one of the teeth offers a strong clue that Homo erectus probably shared this vast landscape with the Denisovans.
A handful of teeth with a lot to say
Baxian and Naxian are a pair of limestone caves that lie 15 kilometers apart along a karst valley corridor in southern China. University of Copenhagen paleoanthropologist Frido Welker and his colleagues unearthed a total of nine hominin teeth from among the more than 18,000 bones and fragments from non-hominin animals found there. Macquarie University paleoanthropologist Kira Westaway and her colleagues analyzed proteins and chemical isotopes from the enamel of five of these teeth.
Proteins in teeth are sturdier than DNA, so they're more likely to survive a few hundred thousand years. But like DNA, proteins contain variations that can help identify the species they came from. Four of the five teeth in the study had distinctly Homo erectus proteins in their enamel; the fifth seems to be some other hominin species, most likely a Denisovan (more on that below).
The hominin teeth and the thousands of animal bone fragments had accumulated in a cement-like rock called breccia. Based on luminescence dating (a method that measures how long it's been since mineral grains were last exposed to sunlight) of the cave sediments, along with the ratios of uranium isotopes in the bones themselves, it looks like that breccia formed sometime between 324,000 and 184,000 years ago. That's a wide range, but it's enough to show that Homo erectus lived in what's now southern China at least 60,000 years longer than we've previously realized.
