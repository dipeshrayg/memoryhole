As part of ongoing wrangling over the US budget for the current fiscal year, the White House on Monday night sent a list of budget exceptions to Congress that it seeks in the near term for the federal government (PDF).
The list of so-called "funding anomalies" is dominated largely by appropriations for the Department of Defense, including the Golden Dome missile defense program championed by the Trump administration. However, among the highest non-defense exceptions is $2 billion for NASA's Artemis program for a "higher rate of operations."
The funding represents a significant 25 percent boost for NASA's Artemis program, which received approximately $7.8 billion in fiscal year 2026. Technically, the White House is seeking to increase the annualized funding rate for Artemis to $9.8 billion, rather than immediately providing NASA with an additional $2 billion. It comes at a time when it is increasingly clear that China's lunar ambitions are as expansive as NASA's, and there is a good chance the country reaches the Moon with astronauts before NASA can get back there.
Another continuing resolution?
Fiscal year 2027 began on October 1 of this year, but the US Congress and White House have not agreed upon a budget. The government, including NASA, has been operating on a "continuing resolution" since that time. The existing continuing resolution remains in effect until December 11, by which time the parties need to agree to a final budget or another continuing resolution, or else the government will shut down.
Given the broad disagreement in funding priorities, it seems most likely that Congress will settle on another continuing resolution, punting a budget deal into the new year and a new Democratic-led House and Senate. The release of the White House "anomalies" document signals to Congress which priorities the Trump administration wants to see addressed in a new continuing resolution that would be agreed to in the coming weeks, which the president would have to sign.
The budget plus-up for Artemis appears to be a significant win for NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman, who arrived at the space agency 11 months ago, after the White House had already put together its "President's Budget request" for the fiscal year 2027 process. This is the first time he has had a chance to work with the Trump administration on budget priorities, and in doing so has convinced officials that NASA deserves more funding in its race back to the Moon.
