In August, Amazon announced plans to expand its Prime Air delivery drone program to "nearly 500 cities and towns by the end of 2026." Combined with similar uncrewed aerial delivery programs from DoorDash and Zipline (in partnership with Uber and Walmart), the expansion suggests that flying robots dropping their payloads onto suburban lawns will soon be commonplace.
For many people, delivery drones will be little more than a novelty, occasionally good for a laugh when one does something ridiculous. For others, though, their growing use could be a nightmare. As the instant delivery industry clears the last FAA hurdles to rapid deliveries beyond an operator's line of sight, an increasing number of people worldwide are living with post-traumatic stress disorder or other anxiety-related disorders triggered by drones.
The use of all manner of weaponized drones in the ongoing war in Ukraine and in Israeli attacks on Lebanon and Gaza has already produced an epidemic of what some mental health professionals have dubbed "dronophobia." And while relatively few US troops have been targeted by drones flown by Iran, ISIS, and Iran-backed militias throughout the Middle East, even those who have not directly been attacked may experience anxiety simply because of the ample video footage of attacks circulating in news and social media.
Combined with broader anxiety about surveillance technologies, artificial intelligence, and things generally falling from the sky, the resulting hypervigilance could result in some unintended deliveries from those Prime Air flying robots.
Heralds of destruction
Drones have been a part of the battlefield in some form since the late 1980s, when the Israeli Defense Forces began using them. From the beginning, they have had a psychological impact on both those in the sights of drone operators and the operators themselves.
The first military use of drones was for intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance. I witnessed the development of naval drone operations aboard USS Iowa in 1986 and 1987, and for many on the ship at the time, the drones were a curiosity-or, at worst, an annoyance. Our crews spent hours standing by a boat, ready to retrieve crashed drones from the water. But we were helping develop a capability that would eventually lead to Iraqi troops surrendering to the USS Wisconsin's drone during the Gulf War. Back then, drones were merely heralds of destruction, acting as forward observers for the Air Force ordnance fired or launched from elsewhere.
