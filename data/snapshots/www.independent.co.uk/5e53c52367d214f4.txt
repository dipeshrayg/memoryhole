Scammers are exploiting American technology to target teenagers with sexual blackmail, an abuse that has led in some cases to tragic deaths.
According to a joint investigation by the Associated Press and FRONTLINE, recent changes introduced by major tech firms have failed to stem the rising tide of sextortion, with certain updates leaving young people even more exposed online.
Data from the Virginia-based nonprofit National Center for Missing & Exploited Children (NCMEC) reveals that financially driven sextortion, the act of coercing victims with threats to publish explicit images unless payment is delivered, rose by almost 40% over the past year.
At least 160 minors are affected every day.
The consequences have proven fatal: NCMEC has recorded the suicides of at least 36 teenage boys across the U.S. following sextortion demands since 2021.
In response to growing risks, minimum age limits and compulsory parental controls are among the national social media restrictions enacted across at least seven countries, including Australia.
Furthermore, 15 other jurisdictions, among them Canada, the European Union, and the United Kingdom, have either pledged to implement or are contemplating comparable regulatory frameworks.
Conversely, American policymakers have largely allowed technology firms to regulate their own platforms, prioritizing economic expansion, first amendment protections, and competitive dominance over legislative mandates, leaving civil lawsuits as the primary remedy after harm occurs.
"The ecosystem as a whole is completely broken," said Lauren Coffren, an executive director at NCMEC. "There is not enough being done. And it should have happened 35 suicides ago."
While end-to-end encryption preserves digital privacy by ensuring only communicating parties can view message content, it simultaneously hampers the capacity of platforms to identify and flag predatory behavior.
A former trust and safety staffer at Meta disclosed that the firm was aware its contentious move to enable default end-to-end encryption on Facebook Messenger by 2024 carried child safety implications, yet concluded these hazards could be managed.
In response, Meta stated that its transition aligned with broader tech industry shifts toward encrypted communications.
The corporation noted that child protection was considered, asserting that modifications were implemented only alongside new safety measures, such as enhanced reporting tools, pattern tracking for suspicious activity, and mechanisms to block flagged accounts from discovering or contacting minors.
NCMEC reported that the growth of default encryption coincided with a 19% decline in overall reports of child sexual abuse material during 2024.
Coffren noted that offenders are increasingly channeling young victims into encrypted channels at an accelerated rate, creating severe obstacles for law enforcement agencies building criminal cases.
While American federal law obligates social media networks to report instances of explicit child abuse material and sextortion to NCMEC upon discovery, platforms face no statutory requirement to proactively scan for or detect such activity.
As the parent company of Facebook, Instagram, and WhatsApp, Meta stands as the world's largest social network operator.
Its platforms submitted nearly 11 million referrals to NCMEC in 2025, significantly outpacing any competitor.
Australian authorities identified Telegram and Apple's iMessage - both of which utilize end-to-end encryption - alongside Meta services as hosting the highest volume of sexual extortion reports. Nevertheless, Apple submitted only 296 referrals to NCMEC over the course of last year.
Dubai-based Telegram, which operates outside the jurisdiction of U.S. reporting mandates, did not begin submitting reports to NCMEC until 2026, according to the organization.
While both Telegram and Apple affirmed their dedication to protecting minors, neither directly addressed inquiries regarding their referral numbers to NCMEC. Telegram stated that it continues to invest in moderation resources, deletes illegal material following requests from NCMEC, and releases user information when served with lawful court orders.
"The right to private conversations cannot be surrendered simply because a minuscule number of people may misuse it," Telegram said in an email.
Data from Cybertip.ca, Canada's government-supported reporting hotline, shows that financial sextortion cases on TikTok increased dramatically following the platform's decision to broaden direct messaging features and permit photo sharing.
According to figures provided to the AP by the Canadian Centre for Child Protection (C3P), the nonprofit managing the helpline, TikTok featured in merely 2% of sextortion complaints in 2023. By 2025, that proportion grew to 27%, and it currently leads all platforms this year, appearing in 33% of reported incidents.
TikTok U.S. stated that it maintains protective measures and routinely prompts members to send images solely to trusted contacts, while reminding users that media can be captured or shared onward.
Independent testing conducted this year by The Cybersafety Research Center, a nonpartisan team based at New York University and Northeastern University, determined that approximately 60% of online safety mechanisms aimed at protecting youth proved ineffective.
"They are designed for public relations and theater. They are not designed to effectively reduce the harms we care about," said Arturo Bejar, a former safety executive at Meta who contributed to the analysis. "Until we have independent measures of harm to regulate and control the outcomes of these companies, it's going to be slow progress."
Meta characterized the evaluation, which assessed Instagram, as "fundamentally flawed" and asserted that it "demonstrates a basic misunderstanding of how our tools work."
Several platforms, such as Meta, Apple, and TikTok, have implemented specialized youth accounts featuring heightened security settings, though adolescents are rarely required to verify their age during account setup. Meta and TikTok U.S. indicated that they deploy artificial intelligence alongside user reports to detect underage profile holders. Apple noted that it requests age confirmation where mandated by law, which presently includes Australia, Brazil, Singapore, South Korea, the U.K., and the state of Texas.
Additionally, Apple and Meta have fielded default nudity protection filters on minor accounts, designed to detect potentially explicit photos and urge caution before users view or transmit them. However, consumer advocates argue that these mechanisms could be configured to outright block the transmission of explicit imagery involving minors.
Apple clarified that its nudity filtering technology runs locally on user devices, meaning the firm receives no alerts when sensitive material is flagged and cannot view the underlying images.
Meta explained that it refrains from outright blocking because its filters operate cautiously and might accidentally restrict non-explicit photos. The company reported that during June 2025, more than 40% of blurred photos sent in direct messages remained unopened. It did not disclose figures regarding how many explicit files were prevented from being sent by teenagers.
To combat sextortion tactics, Meta also moved to conceal follower and following rosters on Instagram from prospective bad actors, removing the contacts blackmailers frequently target to exert leverage over victims.
However, cybercrime analyst Paul Raffile demonstrated gaps in the feature this summer after establishing a test account representing a U.S. teenage boy on Instagram. Within two weeks, four separate extortionists targeted the profile, each sending intimidating screenshots listing the account's followers.
"The moment a teen accepts a scammer's friend request on Instagram, their entire social network is compromised," Raffile said. "The new safety feature was not effective in any of our tests."
Raffile noted that while Meta removed the four accounts following his reports, nearly identical profiles featuring matching usernames, pictures, and biographical details rapidly emerged in their place.
Meta countered that its systems restrict unapproved accounts from messaging adolescents, block suspicious profiles from accessing teen follower lists, and alert young users when conversing with contacts abroad.
The company stated that throughout 2025, it stopped over 265 million flagged accounts on Facebook and 135 million on Instagram from discovering or contacting minors, while terminating 1.9 million Facebook accounts and 2.5 million Instagram profiles.
TikTok similarly removes material promoting illicit schemes following public flagging, yet scam-related content continues to spread across the service.
An examination by the AP easily located 12 separate TikTok profiles broadcasting scripts, guides, and software tools tailored for sextortionists and online fraudsters.
In one instance, an offender posted using shorthand for blackmail: "If it's your mother I BM yesterday may her soul rest in peace. I didn't mean to kill them."
A second account published a recording of a video call with a woman while utilizing artificial intelligence filters to impersonate a U.S. military member. The woman, whose full name appears on screen, expresses joy during the interaction.
"At least I know you are real," she said.
Several of these accounts accumulated hundreds of thousands of likes, with individual videos capturing millions of views.
TikTok stated that it erased all accounts highlighted by the AP for breaching platform rules against promoting or instructing fraud, adding that it combines automated systems with human review teams to police prohibited material.
Nevertheless, clips promoting fraudulent methods persisted in appearing on TikTok's main algorithmic feed. The enterprise confirmed it subsequently removed the newly served accounts after the AP alerted them once more.
