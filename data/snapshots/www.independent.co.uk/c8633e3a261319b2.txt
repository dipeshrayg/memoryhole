Russia pounded Kyiv with drone strikes on Wednesday, hitting railway infrastructure, warehouses and fuel stations as Ukrainian president Volodymyr Zelensky prepared to address the UN.
At least two people were killed and 22 injured in the morning attack in one of the heaviest strikes since Russia began attacking Kyiv and nearby areas over the summer with rapid high-flying jet drones.
The strikes came hours after Zelensky met with US president Donald Trump to discuss peace efforts, including hopes of a bilateral ceasefire on energy infrastructure.
On the sidelines of the UN general assembly in New York, Zelensky told the Wall Street Journal on Tuesday that he had asked Trump for a "winter package" of new military equipment and believes the US will push for a deal to stop attacks on energy.
Zelensky said he was ready to meet Russian president Vladimir Putin at a trilateral meeting with president Trump. But the Kremlin signalled on Wednesday its position was unchanged ahead of a meeting between top US and Russian officials in New York.
"For now, there are no prerequisites for moving onto a peaceful negotiating track, although we, as the Russian side, remain open to peace talks," Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov told reporters.

Kremlin accuses Burnham of stoking anti-Russian sentiment, says no plans for peace talks with Ukraine
On the heels of Andy Burnham's comments to the UN general assembly on Tuesday, the Kremlin has accused the British prime minister of stoking 'anti-Russian sentiment'.
The PM accused Russia of exploiting Brexit to sow division in the UK as he announced a new unit to tackle online disinformation.
Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said that Burnham's approach, and that of other European countries, was flawed.
"Well, the British Prime Minister said nothing new. That is the first point. The second is that stoking anti-Russian sentiment and fostering an atmosphere of total Russophobia is what Britain is doing. And now, presumably in unison with Britain, the Germans, the French and a number of other European states are doing the same thing," said Peskov.
"From our point of view, this is an utterly short-sighted policy. It in no way brings us any closer to any steps that might, in the long term, lead to a reduction in tensions and de-escalation on the European continent."
With Volodymyr Zelensky in New York to discuss a potential ceasefire on energy infrastructure with the Trump administration, Peskov also assessed that the groups for wider Ukraine peace talks were still not in place.
He said there were no concrete details on plans to hold any high-level meeting on Ukraine, and that Zelensky would have to come to Moscow for future talks. Zelensky has previously ruled out such a trip.
Senior Nato commander warns Putin is becoming more cavalier and open to risk as Ukraine war drags on
Russian President Vladimir Putin is becoming "increasingly cavalier, dangerous and risk accepting," as his war in Ukraine drags on and as he seeks to divide Western allies, Nato's deputy commander in Europe said Tuesday.
Air Chief Marshal Sir John Stringer spoke at a security conference in Estonia amid warnings from some European leaders that Moscow is planning to test Nato's collective defense pact with bolder attacks that have already included sabotage and espionage.
Stringer warned that Nato members need to be aware of the "febrile environment" and careful in their response because Putin would like to provoke the alliance into an "overreaction."

Azerbaijan pardons Frenchman Martin Ryan after EU lifts sanctions on Russian billionaire Usmanov
Azerbaijani President Ilham Aliyev pardoned a French national imprisoned for espionage on Wednesday, a day after the EU lifted sanctions on Russian billionaire Alisher Usmanov following what diplomats said was pressure from Baku.

Latvian PM says removing sanctions on Russian oligarchs means 'normalising Russia'
Latvia initially blocked an EU deal to remove Russian oligarchs Alisher Usmanov and Mikhail Fridman from the bloc's sanctions list, arguing that delisting individuals in exchange for political or prisoner-release deals could set a dangerous precedent.
Latvian PM Andris Kulbergs told Euronews: "Either it is sports, what we've seen, either culture, and now one by one cherry-picking out of sanctions, that is not the way to go.
"That is normalising Russia."
"I'm worried about the playbook which it leaves," he said.
"I don't think this is a very good example, because that gives a message. 'Oh, we arrest some politicians somewhere, and then we can ask for a sanctions package.' This is not a good playbook, and that's exactly why we froze it for 36 months. That's it. No discussion."
Latvia initially opposed the move but agreed to abstain after securing concessions from France, including talks on French nuclear deterrence cooperation and a stronger French military presence in Latvia.
"It's important for the eastern flank, for us, to have the coverage of their security," he said.
Russia is using Ukrainian POWs to fuel its propaganda - report
Russia has coerced thousands of Ukrainian prisoners of war (POWs) into appearing in propaganda videos, often making them criticise Ukraine or say they do not want to return home, an investigation by NBC has found.
Former POWs told the American broadcaster they were beaten, tortured or threatened, while some cooperated simply to prove they were alive.
The investigation focuses on Ukrainian soldier Volodymyr Srebrodolskyy, whose 2024 propaganda video led people in his home village to believe he had betrayed Ukraine.
He later died in Russian captivity, and some villagers opposed burying him locally. His cousin said: "At least they could have buried him near his mother."
Former POW Yevhen Shybalov described the propaganda operation as an organised system. "Russian propaganda - it's an industry."
The UN, which interviewed more than 850 Ukrainian POWs, found 96 per cent reported torture or mistreatment, including beatings, electric shocks, sleep deprivation and mock executions.
