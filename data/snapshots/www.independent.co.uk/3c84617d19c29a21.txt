Campaigners fear children with special educational needs (SEND) could be abandoned under Alan Milburn's proposals to fix Britain's Neets crisis because councils are failing to give them the support they need.
The former health secretary has published a major report, outlining recommendations to curb the growing number of under-25s who are not in employment, education or training (Neets).
In the long-awaited review, released on Tuesday, Mr Milburn said it is an "obligation" for a young person to "put in the effort" to get a job, education or training.
"The deal's a very straightforward one," he wrote. "If there are more opportunities for young people, there have got to be more obligations on their part to take up that opportunity. What we can't have is a generation living on benefits. It's a disaster for them and it's a disaster for the taxpayer."
But campaigners have raised concerns over figures that show SEND children are 80 per cent more likely to be classified as a Neet, in part due to failures by authorities to provide support to those who need it.
Jenny Haines, speaking on behalf of accountability SEND campaign group Measure what Matters, highlighted the case of Ella Sanwalka from Surrey, as being typical of many hundreds of SEND children let down by the system who she fears are in danger of being abandoned by the Milburn reforms.
Ella first attempted to take her own life aged just 11 and now, aged 18, has had little formal education because she has been denied the support she required from Surrey County Council.
Despite an education plan being agreed for her two years ago, she is still waiting for the results of a tribunal to force the council to implement the plan it has already agreed.
Ms Haines said: "A child who nearly died trying to access some form of education is now being told that opportunity comes to 'those who try.' There is no polite word for that."
The Measure what Matters campaign, along with Lib Dem MP Chris Coghlan, has collated 1,253 testimonies of families of SEND children covering 134 different councils who claim to have been failed by the authorities amid growing concerns over the government's plans to reform special educational needs.
A third of another 1,706 family accounts analysed by Measure what Matters described months - and, at worst, years - of severely disrupted education. A total of 354 explicitly refer to periods where they had no school place, and no educational provision at all.
Ella's mother Becky told The Independent that the stress and lack of sleep of the last seven years has taken a physical toll on her as a parent as well and left her needing to use a wheelchair.
She said: "It has been so traumatic. I have had many years of not being able to sleep because of the fear of what would happen to Ella and if she would try to take her own life again.
"Ella is 18 now and she has had almost no formal education. She has said to me 'if the schools don't want me, what is the point of me being alive.' The system has completely failed Ella and the girl she was has gone."
Ella was aged about seven when her mother first raised concerns but was told her daughter was "just fine". By the time she was 11, the primary school admitted she was "probably dyslexic."
Entering secondary school without an education, health and care plan (EHCP), Ella attempted to take her life after two months. Despite this, and other suicide attempts, she was refused an EHCP by the council.
Eventually the council agreed to an EHCP in late 2021 but named the mainstream school that had already said it could not meet her needs, forcing the family to appeal again.
Five years later, Ella still has not had the specialist educational support she needs and has not attended school.
Lib Dem MP Chris Coghlan and Measure what Matters are pushing for parents of failed children to report local authorities to the police over misconduct in public office.
Mr Coghlan said: "It is staggering that policymakers are failing to make the connection between funding the 1.8m SEND children properly and addressing the routine lawbreaking by public officials to solving the Neets crisis.
"This could save money and lives. Public officials who have deliberately broken the law against children should be prosecuted."
Mr Milburn addressed the claims with a section in his report, suggesting his reforms will help SEND children in particular.
He said: "We expect those with conditions such as anxiety, depression, ADHD and autism - who currently form the majority of new PIP claimants - should be particular beneficiaries. In the current system, young people with these conditions often end up trapped on benefits, when under the new system they would be both expected and enabled to be in work."
But he admitted: "These changes will not be easy to make. Nor will they be pain-free. They could mean some people get less financial support at the beginning of their benefit claim than under the current system. But the approach I have taken is to try to define what would give young people the best chance of building a good life, not just over a year or two but over the course of their lifetimes."
Responding to the allegations regarding support for Ella, Helyn Clack, Surrey County Council cabinet member for children, families and lifelong learning, said: "While we cannot comment on individual children or families, we believe every child and young person with special educational needs and disabilities (SEND) should receive the right support, at the right time.
"We acknowledge that some families have not always experienced the service they should expect, and there remains more work to do. However, there are encouraging signs of progress across Surrey's Additional Needs and Disabilities Partnership, which brings together education, health, and social care services. The most recent review of Surrey's SEND strategic plan by the Department for Education and NHS England concluded that "Surrey has demonstrated continued commitment and progress", while recognising that further improvement is still required.
"We take the findings of tribunals, complaints, and feedback from families seriously and use these insights to strengthen services and improve outcomes for children and young people. Our focus remains on reducing delays, expanding specialist provision, strengthening accountability, and improving families' experiences, so that every child and young person with SEND can access the support they need to thrive."
The Independent has contacted the government for comment.
