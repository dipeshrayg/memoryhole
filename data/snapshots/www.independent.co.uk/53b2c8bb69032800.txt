President Donald Trump said Monday that he would welcome the return of Jensen Huang as chief executive of Nvidia - days after the chipmaker's co-founder was forced out over an extramarital affair with a Chinese businesswoman - and insisted that no one had proven the woman was a spy.
"There's a big group of people that would like him to return," Trump told reporters as he left the White House to travel to Asia ahead of his meeting with Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin later this week.
"I think having Jensen go back, a lot of people are asking Jensen. They think Jensen should go back. That certainly isn't a bad idea. I recommended Tim Cook, who, as you know, built Apple, I recommended Tim Cook to go and do a job," Trump continued.
Huang resigned last week after acknowledging an affair with Alice Huang - no relation - a businesswoman that U.S. officials have described as a suspected asset of China's Ministry of State Security.
The New York Times reported that U.S. intelligence determined she had been recruited by the spy agency and tasked with influencing Huang to push Nvidia toward selling Beijing more of its advanced chips. The Times also reported Jensen Huang's second affair, with a Nvidia subordinate, moved the company's board to force him out last week.
In the days since, allies of the former Nvidia boss have been attempting to rally support for his return with an online petition that has thus far garnered more than 100,000 signatures, including from Nvidia shareholders and employees.
Tesla and SpaceX chief executive Elon Musk and Tim Sweeney, the founder and chief executive of the video game publisher Epic Games, were among major industry figures supporting an open letter calling for his return.
But Nvidia sources told CNBC on Monday that the company's board is not considering bringing him back and is instead focused on a probe into whether his actions harmed American national security.
Pressed repeatedly on whether he accepted the U.S. intelligence on Alice Huang, Trump questioned it.
"I don't know that she's a spy at all. Nobody's proven that," he said. "He probably shouldn't have been doing, on a personal basis, what he did. You know, from - who knows? That's a different story. But nobody's proven that she's a spy. She may not be a spy."
Trump earlier offered a broader version of the same claim. "Usually what happens with that is that any woman coming to the country that's a beautiful woman that comes from China, they say she's a spy," Trump said. "I don't know that she was a spy at all. I mean, I really don't."
Asked whether he trusted the U.S. intelligence that the woman was a Chinese agent, and whether Secretary of State Marco Rubio had told him so, Trump said he had not heard it.
"Who told me that? ... I haven't heard that. I haven't heard that," he said. "No, I don't know that she is a spy."
The president's claims do not square exactly with what is publicly known about U.S. officials' concerns over the affair.
According to The New York Times, Rubio was so alarmed by what intelligence officials were learning about Alice Huang through electronic surveillance that he drafted a classified memorandum on her alleged recruitment and briefed Trump on it aboard Air Force One in May, en route to China.
But Trump brushed aside warnings from Rubio and nonetheless pressed ahead with a relationship that saw Huang become a frequent White House visitor after donating $25 million to the president's ballroom project.
The president defended the decision to keep selling chips to China and brushed aside the suggestion that his closeness to Huang had endangered national security. "It was not put at risk," he said Monday, before pivoting to a claim that the country "is now stronger than ever before."
Trump also dismissed statements from Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, who told CNBC last week that Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick resisted putting Megaspeed - a China-linked company run by Alice Huang and thought to be a pass-through to help Beijing evade export controls - on his department's "Entity List" of firms not allowed to purchase American products.
Asked about that, Trump said Lutnick "might have delayed it, but he was doing that because ... you could have a blowup with China."
"One wants to be friendly with China, and one wants to have a blowup with China. Different views," Trump said, declining to say whether he would replace Lutnick, who is traveling with him to China aboard Air Force One despite the scandal surrounding him and is scheduled to testify on the Nvidia affair before Congress in the next few weeks.
Huang was barred from selling Nvidia's most advanced processors, including its Blackwell line, to China, a restriction Trump defended while playing down its stakes.
"The only thing he was able to sell to China were chips that were sort of obsolete, a little bit like our avionics," Trump said. "Selling the other chips is a good thing, not a bad thing."
The president said he might raise the alleged spying operation with Chinese President Xi Jinping when the two leaders meet on Thursday but would not commit to doing so when pressed by reporters.
"I'll ask him what I want to ask him," he said.
