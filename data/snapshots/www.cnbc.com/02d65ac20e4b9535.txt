The Trump administration has unveiled a fresh tariff salvo on more than a dozen trading partners, with China, Vietnam, and India facing some of the steepest potential rates and mounting pressure to conclude trade deals with Washington.
The proposed tariffs, framed as curbing excess industrial capacity, start at 15% for the European Union, South Korea, Switzerland and Taiwan and rise to as much as 35% for Vietnam.
The U.S. Trade Representative's Office said it intends to cap the new duties at lower levels for countries that have completed trade deals or are still negotiating with Washington.
The overall impact on the region is likely to be limited, said Mark Williams, chief Asia economist at Capital Economics. The proposal "puts most of the targeted countries back where they were earlier in the year," he said, after the Supreme Court struck down Trump's emergency tariffs in February.
Still, the proposal could give some countries an incentive to speed up trade talks in order to lock in lower tariff rates, analysts said.
On Wednesday, U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said that the new policy likely will not take effect for months, with a senior U.S. official saying the duties could take effect as early as January.
China faces a capped rate of 20%, on top of an existing 25% tariff imposed during President Donald Trump's first term. The rate could rise to 40% if the trade truce reached in South Korea last October is not extended beyond January, the USTR said.
Beijing has rejected Washington's claim of excess capacity while vowing to take measures necessary to protect its rights.
On Thursday, Chinese Commerce Ministry spokesperson He Yadong called on Washington to "rectify its wrongdoing and return to the right track of resolving issues through dialogue."
He was responding to Washington's plan to raise tariffs following its Section 301 investigations, while the ministry also urged the U.S. to implement $30 billion of reciprocal tariff cuts agreed by the two sides in late September.
The renewed tensions come despite several conciliatory meetings between Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping this year, as well as what Williams described as Trump's "apparent lack of concern" about allegations that Chinese technology firms have circumvented U.S. chip export controls.
The two leaders are expected to meet for a fourth time this year at the Group of 20 leaders summit in Miami in December.
"While not happy, Beijing seems prepared to live with the new rates provided that they don't exceed 20%," said Wendy Cutler, senior vice president at the Asia Society Policy Institute.
By retaining the option to raise levies if bilateral talks deteriorate, Washington is using the tariffs as leverage to "secure expected shipments of critical minerals and rare earths, and further purchases of U.S. agricultural goods," Cutler said.
Washington has warned that tariffs on Chinese goods could double to 40% if the USTR finds that Beijing is not engaging. "If the trade war did start up again, today's measures provide the U.S. with some ammunition," Williams said.
Even the sweeping actions against the other 13 trading partners appear aimed squarely at China, said Williams, who tallied that the word "China" appears 253 times in the USTR's report on Vietnam, and 315 times in the document on the European Union.
"Foremost among the 'unreasonable acts, policies, and practices' that these countries are criticised for is being slow to restrict imports and FDI from China," Williams said.
While the proposed tariffs generally lift average rates only modestly, India and Vietnam stand out because of the wider gaps between the rates they would face with and without a deal, analysts said.
India and Vietnam face proposed tariffs of 25% and 35%, respectively, but those rates could fall sharply to 17% and 20% if ongoing talks with the U.S. produce reciprocal trade agreements.
The two countries have "a strong incentive" to conclude deals with Washington to secure the lower rates, Cutler said.
Vietnam's trade ministry said Thursday that it was working with the U.S. to complete a trade deal as soon as possible, according to Reuters. India could also gain an edge over some Asian rivals through comparatively lower levies if New Delhi concludes its long-pending trade deal with Washington.
Singapore faces a similar challenge. The proposal sets a maximum potential rate of 20% on its exports, net of existing Section 301 tariffs, which could fall to 10% if it reaches an agreement on reciprocal trade with the U.S. or concludes other negotiations addressing the USTR's findings, according to Singapore's Ministry of Energy, Trade and Industry.
The ministry said it disagreed with the findings and was "very disappointed" with the proposed action, arguing that it failed to account for the structure of Singapore's economy.
Speaking to reporters, Deputy Prime Minister Gan Kim Yong said that about 33%, or 9.4 billion Singapore dollars ($7.2 billion) of direct exports will be affected, and the effective tariff rate on affected Singapore exports will rise to 10.3% from the current 7.8%, should Washington impose the highest levy of 20%.
"I'm also very surprised that this has happened because this is not something that you would expect from an important major trading partner," Gan added, saying "We have been working together for many years, and we have an FTA with the U.S.," Gan said, referring to the U.S.-Singapore Free Trade Agreement that was in force since 2004.
Under the FTA, Singapore imposes zero tariffs on U.S. goods, while the U.S. has run a trade surplus with the city-state for more than 20 years, the ministry said. That surplus grew to $33 billion in 2025 from $27 billion in 2024.
Singapore said it would submit further comments during the public consultation period and urge the USTR to reconsider.
Exemptions for semiconductors and other electronics should cushion the impact on South Korea and Taiwan, whose economies are heavily concentrated in those sectors, analysts said.
The USTR singled out South Korea for its concentration in memory-chip production and what it described as outsized government support. Yet it excluded the country's chips from the proposed excess-capacity tariffs.
Taiwan, which faces an excess-capacity tariff cap of 15%, also benefits from exemptions for other electronics, including mobile phones and networking equipment. Its effective tariff rate would rise to about 9% from 6%, Williams estimated.
Switzerland was hit with a shock 39% tariff rate back in 2025, targeting significant exports, from chemicals and pharmaceuticals to gold, watches and chocolates. In November 2025, it negotiated a deal that slashed U.S. duties to 15% in return for a commitment to invest billions and increase manufacturing Stateside.
In March 2026, the U.S. launched further Section 301 investigations into Switzerland over "unfair or discriminatory trade practices," landing on a recommendation to impose an additional 15% tariff.
Switzerland's Economy Ministry told CNBC that the country rejected the allegations raised during the investigations and said it had already presented its arguments during bilateral consultations with the U.S. Treasury.
The ministry added that it assumed the latest recommendation was consistent with the deal struck in November 2025 and said it was in close contact with U.S. authorities as it reviews next steps.
Officials for both Switzerland and the European Union said during trade discussions last year that while they had a trade surplus with Washington in goods, the relationship was balanced when accounting for both trade and services, where the U.S. dominates in areas such as technology and finance.
Brussels said it was assessing the U.S. report but rejected its finding that Europe was a source of industrial overcapacity.
"To the contrary, Europe is among those most exposed to overcapacity," European Commission Spokesperson Olof Gill said in a statement.
"The EU and the U.S. share a real concern about global overcapacity, much of it state-driven and concentrated in third countries. We regret the lack of joint action," Gill said.
The EU expects any measure taken from the investigation to respect the existing joint agreement of a 15% all-inclusive ceiling on EU goods, Gill added.
EU trade ministers are due to speak with U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer on Monday.
Citi analysts noted they expected a hit on European retailers if the measures stay as announced, with the likes of Adidas, H&M, Puma and Primark potentially seeing downgrades on full-year 2027 earnings.
Inga Fechner, senior economist for global trade at ING, said in a Wednesday note that the U.S. "tariff wall keeps changing, but it isn't coming down," adding that "U.S. protectionism is becoming increasingly difficult to unwind."
While the newly Democrat-controlled Congress could try to restrict presidential tariff authority, remove individual duties or apply greater scrutiny via hearings and investigations, actually reversing the measures will be difficult while Trump retains veto power, she said.
"We don't think the blue wave will mark a return to free trade. The Biden years already showed that tariffs in strategically important sectors enjoy support across political camps."
Protectionist moves become more targeted, she added, adding to a complex system of country- and sector-specific duties based on different legal authorities.
