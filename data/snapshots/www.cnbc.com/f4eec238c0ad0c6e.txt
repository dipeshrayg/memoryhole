European Central Bank President Christine Lagarde will step down on Feb. 7 next year, she announced Thursday, formally kicking off a succession race that will be shaped by political bargaining and France's looming fiscal crisis.
Lagarde has led the central bank since July 2019, steering its responses to the Covid-19 pandemic, the Russia-Ukraine war and a series of energy price shocks that sent inflation in the European Union above 11% in 2022 and spurred the end of the post-Financial Crisis era of negative interest rates.
Lagarde will depart nearly nine months before her term was due to expire in October 2027, while the process to appoint her successor will begin immediately.
In a press conference Thursday following the ECB's October interest rate decision, Lagarde confirmed media reports that she was moving her departure forward because she wanted more freedom to express political opinions. Her departure will come ahead of the French presidential election next spring.
"The reason I'm anticipating the end of my term by leaving in February 2027 instead of October is, as I have said to colleagues, members of staff and governors, is because I want to have the freedom of speech, the freedom of expression and the freedom to engage on matters that are not monetary policy matters," she said.
As ECB president, Lagarde was under the "constraint" of an "obligation of neutrality," she added.
"We don't get out of that mandate, because that's the price we pay for being independent. At this time, in relation to many corners of the world, not just European corners... I think that my voice can help, my actions can help in relation to principles that are dear to my heart."
"One of those principles... I don't think that I can remain neutral; I don't think that I can be comfortable in that neutrality, at the present time. I'm interested in the truth, and I see the truth being very often damaged, contradicted by misrepresentation, and from the position that I have had for the last 20-plus years of my life... there are things that I can say. Whether people will listen or not is another story, but I feel that I should say those things. Voilà."
Market watchers noted that the timing moves her departure before the French presidential election, in which polling suggests far-right candidate Marine Le Pen is the frontrunner.
Lagarde has repeatedly downplayed any suggestion she intends to run herself. If she did run, Lagarde would face an extremely tight timeframe with the first round vote in April.
An early exit means the new French president "will have no role in choosing the next ECB president - particularly significant given the political and fiscal uncertainty surrounding France," Felix Feather, Economist at Aberdeen Investments, said in emailed comments.
Senior ECB figures are already in talks about filling other senior roles, with chief economist Philip Lane and influential executive board member Isabel Schnabel, responsible for market operations, also due to step down next year.
The ECB is institutionally independent, and its Governing Council comprises the six-member Executive Board and the governors of the 21 euro area national central banks.
But appointments to its top jobs are made by the European Council following consultations with the European Parliament and euro area member states, with negotiations generally reflecting political bargaining over national representation and influence.
Spain's Pablo Hernández de Cos and the Netherlands' Klaas Knot, who both led their respective central banks for recent stints, are widely regarded as top contenders to replace Lagarde.
De Cos served from 2018 to 2024 and is now general manager of the Bank for International Settlements. Knot was Dutch central bank chief from 2011 until 2025 and is a distinguished visiting fellow at the Peterson Institute for International Economics.
The appointment of a new president is seen as crucial amid market debate over whether the ECB could use one of its intervention mechanisms as France faces a mounting sovereign debt crisis.
France's presidential election is injecting further uncertainty as the country's bond yields spike and its spread over euro area benchmark Germany's rises to the highest level since the bloc's Sovereign Debt Crisis of the early 2010s.

Berenberg Chief Economist Holger Schmieding said that as a result, the ECB would need to consider who was best placed to deal with potential financial turbulence.
"As a former chair of the G20's Financial Stability Board (FSB), Knot may have an edge in this respect," Schmieding said in a note.
"The European Union is trying to guard itself at least somewhat against the risk that right-wing Marine Le Pen may win the French presidential election."
"However, the ECB has no mandate and no appetite to fund French fiscal follies. In our view, the ECB would only support France if Paris returns to more sustainable policies," he added.
Knot is widely seen as the more hawkish of the pair, previously warning that the ECB risked doing too little during the inflation surge of recent years and suggesting the possibility of large rate increases.
His appointment "would likely be read as hawkish and could support the euro, though a tougher stance could also add pressure to French and peripheral bond spreads," Pierre Roke, global capital markets associate at Validus Risk Management, said in a note.
"De Cos would signal more continuity, with a stronger focus on financial stability. With French debt under pressure and inflation still above target, rate markets would likely take more comfort in a De Cos appointment."
Nationality will be another factor, ING's global head of macro, Carsten Brzeski told CNBC by email on Thursday.
"While Knot might find more support among Northern European governments, the question is whether De Cos will be supported by the current Spanish government."
"In any case, both would bring a high level of monetary policy expertise to the table. Something that Christine Lagarde had to pick up on when already in office."
Lagarde was given a standing ovation by the Governing Council of the central bank on Thursday.
"It has been a source of great pride to serve this institution. I thank all of those who have supported me on this journey over the past seven years," Lagarde said.
European Council President Antonio Costa said she had provided a "steady and determined stewardship, helping to safeguard stability and the resilience of the euro area.
Lagarde, born in Paris in 1956, first worked as a high-ranking lawyer before joining the French government. In 2007, she became the first female Finance Minister of a G7 country. She was appointed managing director of the International Monetary Fund in 2011, the first woman to hold the post, before joining the ECB.
She faced a blow to her leadership of the central bank in 2024, when a staff survey gave a heavily critical verdict on her tenure. The survey suggested many felt she spent too much time discussing topics unrelated to monetary policy.
Lagarde is viewed as a top candidate to become the next chair of the World Economic Forum, according to Financial Times reporting.
