France's economy is flashing fresh recession warnings as businesses delay decisions ahead of the 2027 election - but resilient industries, progress on the budget and lower energy costs could help keep growth positive.
Business activity declined to a six-month low in November, with manufacturing performing worse than the services sector, according to the S&P Global Purchasing Managers' Index published on Monday.
The French private sector is at its most pessimistic about the coming year since late 2012, excluding the months at the start of the pandemic, according to the survey, with job losses recorded for a seventh straight month.
Among the biggest challenges cited by companies were economic conditions, uncertainty, competition and cost pressures, while frail domestic demand is weighing on sales.
France is heading toward a closely watched presidential election in April 2027, with polling suggesting voters may favor candidates offering a break from the policies associated with incumbent President Emmanuel Macron.
Politico polling this year has consistently indicated the front-runner is far-right candidate Marine Le Pen, who has recently made efforts to appease financial market jitters over France's fiscal sustainability, but has failed to convince many analysts that she can stabilize the country's debt trajectory.
The latest PMI figures add to a run of economic woes for France this year, including a dramatic rise in government borrowing costs, a surprise outlook downgrade by ratings agency Fitch, and a tumbling stock market which has underperformed its European peers.
This has compounded a sense of crisis after hundreds of thousands of people took to the streets in October and November to protest underfunding in education, the cost of living and the state of public services and housing.
The latest PMI survey has meanwhile raised the odds of France entering a technical recession - two consecutive prints of negative GDP growth - in the fourth quarter, according to Joe Hayes, senior principal economist at S&P Global Market Intelligence. The next print will follow a 0.1% contraction in the third quarter, though a revised Q3 reading is due on Friday.
"The eurozone's second-largest economy is struggling with weak domestic demand, in part due to hesitancy from clients to commit to projects ahead of a highly uncertain election period next year," Hayes said.
"We've subsequently seen a rapid deterioration in business confidence these past few months as other long-standing issues, such as concerns about France's fiscal position, resurface as the election draws closer."
"It's also notable that output price inflation has slowed sharply in November. This suggests that company pricing power has diminished and further highlights the fragility of the French economy," Hayes added.
Sharon Bell, senior European equity strategist at Goldman Sachs, told CNBC's "Squawk Box Europe" on Monday that most people had expected a bounce-back in France's PMI figures in Monday's release "on the basis of getting through the worst period of protests."
France's worst rioting in years in October not only racked up millions of dollars in insured losses but damaged the country's standing among some international investors, contributing to a sense of instability and an inability to pass reforms.
Bell said the gloomy PMI figures showed the weakness of French growth and its additional vulnerability alongside its election risk premium and large deficit.
A crucial factor for the French economy, according to Bell, will be whether a recent spike in European energy prices will begin to ease.
Sovereign risk has already started to become less of a distraction from solid European corporate fundamentals as energy pressures have started to let up over the last month, and will continue to do so if that continues, she told CNBC.
"If energy prices do stay high, then obviously that means higher inflation, higher rates, and more of a problem for the more vulnerable sovereigns. And France would definitely be in that group, probably the most important member of that group at the moment," she said.
Higher French government bond yields - stoked by a mix of its failure to pass a budget that will reduce the deficit, political paralysis and weak economic growth - have piled pressure on businesses as high rates spill over into corporate borrowing costs, making it more expensive to refinance debt or fund new projects.
French homebuilder Nexity said in an October trading update that a 10-year government bond yield near 5% had "reduced household borrowing capacity" as it recorded an 18% fall in sales to individual homebuyers in the first nine months.

Also in October, office landlord Gecina said it would be "very careful" about new capital spending amid pressure on its future financing costs, while bank Societe Generale said that the widening gap in French borrowing costs with Germany "could adversely affect the funding conditions of the Group and its clients."
Uncertainty around the outcome of the election, in which the far-left Jean-Luc Mélenchon is another popular candidate, is also frequently cited by analysts as deterring French business activity and investment.
Helene Gans, president and co-founder of investment fund Renaissance Capital, said in an opinion piece for Economie Matin earlier in November that companies are postponing decision-making, delaying near-finalized commitments and dragging out negotiations with a "worrying intensity" - attributing the fall in confidence to the run-up to the 2027 election.
However, Bank of France Governor Emmanuel Moulin told CNBC's Charlotte Reed in an exclusive interview last week that he does not forecast a recession, even if the central bank's current projection for 0.4% growth in the fourth quarter "might be a little bit too high."
"We don't see a recession coming, we see positive growth in Q4," Moulin told CNBC.
"We have a survey every month of 8,500 companies, and we ask them whether their activity will grow in the next month, and there is a resilience of industry and of services in France, and in particular for industries and sectors that are doing well, like electricity, defense, aeronautics. These sectors are showing resilience and weathering the global shocks that we are seeing," he said.
Moulin nonetheless cited a lack of confidence and political uncertainty as key factors holding back economic performance this year.
"We are seeing ... worries about the situation of public finance, and this triggers high saving rates, worries about the future of pensions, and so all this tends to lower consumption, tends to be, you know, reducing investment," he said.
On borrowing costs, Moulin said spreads with nations such as Germany were "very high, and too high."
"They are too high compared with the situation of the French economy, its trends, but also its fiscal situation, which is not good, which needs to be dealt with, but which has not changed materially from, you know, June to September," he added.
"So there is some kind of, I think, overreaction of the markets. But I think it's important for the government to give signs that they are serious about reducing the deficit."
Moulin said that he was confident that the government's current budget proposal, designed to narrow the country's deficit from 5.4% of GDP to 5% of GDP, will be passed, even if it requires an executive decree or another measure if it fails to pass through the divided parliament.
French finance minister Roland Lescure said on Monday that the government's 2027 economic growth forecast of 1% was at risk of being downgraded, as he also backed the prospect of a budgetary "golden rule" - a reform that would impose stricter controls on public spending and would look to stop governments from running excessive deficits.
Le Pen proposed a golden rule in a recent speech, a concept that has also been backed by centrist candidate Edouard Philippe.
Moulin told CNBC last week that such a move would "enshrine the fiscal discipline in our institutions."
"Most of the [presidential] candidates have, you know, been quite affirmative in their resolution to reduce the deficit. All of them are making pleas... advocating that they will reduce the deficit down to 3%," he said.
"The consensus over reducing the deficit is broadening, but we don't need only words. We need acts, and the first act, I think, in order to show that the government is serious about fiscal discipline, is to have a budget."
