Number of unaccompanied minors entering Germany drops by nearly half
We are taking a brief pause from our coverage of the Saturday attack to bring you another story making headlines in Germany.
A new report indicates a sharp reduction in the number of unaccompanied children and minors crossing Germany's borders.
According to the Federal Statistics Office, the number of minors taken into care after entering Germany dropped from 30.800 to 16.500 in a single year.
This change is also reflected in the overall number of children taken into state care in Germany - the authorities placed around 56,900 children into a foster family or a state facility last year, which is a drop of around 18% compared to 2024. The 2024 numbers had already shown a 7% reduction.

German conservatives urge clampdown on potential terrorists in wake of Berlin ramming
Senior politicians from the CDU/CSU conservative block are calling for harsher penalties, intelligence sharing and other reforms to deal with violent extremists following the latest attack in Berlin.
Police have identified the attacker as Abdul B., a German national with Lebanese roots, who had spent months in detention after trying to join the "Islamic State" in Syria.
Marc Henrichmann, chairman of the Bundestag's Intelligence Oversight Committee, described the van ramming as a "declaration of war against our free democracy" that should not be treated "with kid gloves." He called for seamless data sharing between different intelligence agencies, as well as "appropriate data retention" to track suspected terrorists and identify their allies.
Meanwhile, Bavarian Interior Minister Joachim Herrmann decried the fact that the suspected Berlin attacker received only a suspended sentence after returning to Germany. Herrmann described it as a "disaster" and said the judges passing the verdict needed to be aware of "the responsibility they have, including for human lives."
The interior minister of Saxony-Anhalt, Sven Schulze, also urged a tougher course on extremism, saying that people should not be treated mildly just because they are German nationals.
"We must not allow people who do not accept our culture to live here," he told the Deutschlandfunk radio network.
What do we know about the suspected attacker?
Authorities named the attacker as 21-year-old Abul B., born in Berlin. The German national was known to law enforcement for being radicalized. Last year, he traveled to Lebanon in a failed attempt to join the "Islamic State" militia, was arrested and spent three months in a Lebanese prison before he was allowed to return to Germany.
Upon flying back to Berlin in November 2025, he was detained by German authorities on charges including preparing a serious violent act against the state. After spending months in pre-trial detention, a juvenile court sentenced him to a year and ten months in May. However, his sentence was suspended and he was released.
The suspect was expected to take part in a de-radicalization course.
The prosecutors then appealed the court's decision, demanding a stricter punishment.
On Saturday, Abdul B. drove a van through the crowd celebrating Christopher Street Day in Berlin and fled the scene. He was eventually tracked down and shot by German police.
