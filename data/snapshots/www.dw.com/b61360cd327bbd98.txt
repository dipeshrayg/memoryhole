The report spans more than 150 pages, but its conclusion can be summed up in a single sentence: "The global situation regarding freedom of religion and belief deteriorated during the reporting period," said Thomas Rachel, the German government's commissioner for freedom of religion and belief, and a member of parliament for the center-right Christian Democratic Union (CDU). Recently adopted by the Cabinet, the government's fourth report on the subject covers developments from 2023 to 2025.
The government has issued a dedicated report on freedom of religion and belief around the world. According to the report, this freedom is facing "increasing pressure" globally. The focus is not primarily on the suppression of religious institutions or structures, but on the situation of individuals who hold a religious belief, or none at all.
Authoritarian regimes increase pressure on religious communities
The importance of religious freedom is reflected in the way the government commissioner describes the issue: "Religion and faith are of great importance to four out of five people around the world and are central to their identity. People wake up with God and go to sleep with God. It is part of their everyday lives." Research by the Pew Research Center suggests that about 84% of the world's population identifies with a certain religion.
At a time when authoritarian regimes are gaining ground and expanding their surveillance capabilities, the report paints a bleak picture. The growing number of violent conflicts, the rise of authoritarian rule, and the spread of populism have led to "painful restrictions on freedom of religion and belief around the world."
The German government report does not attempt to provide a statistical tally of attacks or the number of people facing persecution. Instead, it examines 30 countries as case studies where, in the federal government's view, freedom of religion and belief is being restricted. These include about six countries each from Africa, Asia, and the Arab world. In Europe, the report focuses on Belarus, Russia, Ukraine, and Turkey, while in Latin America it highlights Mexico, Nicaragua, and Cuba. It also identifies shortcomings in five of the world's six most populous countries: China, India, Indonesia, Pakistan, and Nigeria.
Regarding India, the report refers to "Hindu nationalist efforts" that threaten the equal treatment of all officially recognized religions. It states that the generally peaceful coexistence of different faiths is "repeatedly overshadowed by tensions and, at times, violence." Both Muslim and Christian communities report growing intolerance and hate speech. According to the report, violent Hindu extremist groups have carried out a "large number of physical attacks" against Muslims, while the police and government authorities often respond inadequately.
In China, the German government identifies a "significant tightening of the legal framework of state control at the expense of freedom of religion and belief." According to the report, this particularly affects Uyghurs, Tibetans, and churches that lack official state approval and are subject to harsh measures by the authorities. The law on the "Promotion of Ethnic Unity and Progress," which came into force on July 1, 2026, subordinates religion to "the formation of national identity" and patriotic education.
One of the countries where the situation has deteriorated most dramatically is Nicaragua, which has severely restricted freedom of religion and belief since 2018. The repression has primarily targeted the Catholic Church. According to the report, "intimidation, persecution, imprisonment, and expulsion" have forced around 130 priests and nuns to leave the country.
The reports reserves some of its sharpest criticism for Kirill, the patriarch of the Russian Orthodox Church. Under his leadership, the church has strongly supported Russia's war against Ukraine and portrayed it as a "holy war." According to the report, "weapons are blessed, Russian soldiers are absolved of their sins, and donations are collected for the war." It is perhaps the clearest example in the report of how religion can be used to justify oppression and violence.
Intimidation reaches beyond borders
The report explicitly addresses the threat posed by "transnational repression" (TNR), which it describes as a "growing global problem" that also affects Germany. The term refers to the persecution or intimidation of individuals by their country of origin beyond its own borders.
Ultimately, it argues, this amounts to a "continuation of domestic repression." Such "cross-border repression" has become considerably easier through the growing availability of digital means of repression, including smear campaigns on social media and spyware.
These forms of pressure range from physical attacks on individuals to digital assaults such as online intimidation and defamation. In some cases, repression is also carried out by threatening family members who remain in the country of origin. Russia and Iran, for example, are identified as the "main perpetrators of transnational repression" in Germany.
China is a particularly prominent example of religious and ideological tensions. Repression by Beijing in Germany primarily targets members of groups that the country's Communist Party classifies as "hostile to the system." These include Uyghurs, Tibetans, and members of the Falun Gong movement. According to the report, they are "also subject to surveillance and repressive measures in Germany," while relatives living in China are pressured.
Thomas Rachel hopes the newly published report will be "discussed in as many Bundestag committees as possible" and that members of parliament will "engage with the issue accordingly." Previous government reports on the subject have been debated in the Bundestag plenary at least once and, in some cases, twice.
Religion: A matter of German foreign policy
The issue's place within the German government has also changed during the current legislative term. Commissioner Thomas Rachel is now based at the Foreign Ministry, while both of his predecessors since 2018 were attached to the Ministry for Economic Cooperation and Development. Today, Rachel says, the importance of religion is being "taken into account" in German foreign policy. He also points to his involvement in the Foreign Ministry's diplomatic training programs.
In doing so, the government led by Chancellor Friedrich Merz (CDU) reversed a structural decision made by the previous center-left coalition, which was led from 2021 to 2025 by Chancellor Olaf Scholz of the Social Democratic Party (SPD). The Scholz government had reduced the issue's prominence by assigning it to the "Religion and Foreign Policy" division. Religion and foreign policy have now regained a more prominent place within government.
One example is the current foreign minister, Johann Wadephul, who has already visited the Vatican more often than his two predecessors combined. In addition, alongside Rachel, the ministry has for some time maintained a dedicated unit headed by an ambassador that deals with, among other things, "fundamental issues relating to religion and foreign policy."
