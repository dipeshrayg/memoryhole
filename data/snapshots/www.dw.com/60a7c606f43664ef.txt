High-speed train crash in Spain: What do we know so far?
At least 68 people have died after a high-speed publicly-run Renfe train collided into the back of a privately-run Iryo train that was stopped farther along the tracks on the line between Madrid and the southern city of Seville.
The trains crashed just after 9 a.m. on Wednesday, between the towns of Mora and Orgaz, near Toledo. About 540 people were on the two trains, local officials said.
More than 100 remain injured.
Spain's train accident is the deadliest in Europe since 2013
Wednesday's accident was the second train disaster to have taken place in Spain this year and the deadliest train crash in Europe since 2013.
The train crash earlier this year in Spain also took place on the same line, with the collision near Cordoba having killed 46 people in January.
Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez said in a televised address from near the accident site that "all of Spain" was in mourning.
He said the "terrible tragedy" would be investigated and vowed to address any failures that came to light.
Spain's Transportation Minister Oscar Puente said the crash resulted from an "inexplicable" decision by operators to have the high-speed train continue on its journey after a computer error had triggered a red signal closing down the stretch of track.
All major parties, apart from the far-right Vox party, cancelled their campaigning on Wednesday out of respect for the victims. Spaniards are set to head to the polls on November 29.
Spain's 4 deadly train disasters this year: What to know
The deadly train collision on Wednesday was the second high-speed rail crash this year. Spain's high-speed rail network, the largest in Europe, had gone without any fatal crashes since it opened in 1992. But there have also been fatal collisions on other parts of the network this year:
January 18: Derailment and collision near Cordoba kill 46
An Iryo train traveling from Malaga to Madrid derailed near Adamuz, in southern Spain, and collided with an oncoming Renfe train heading to Huelva.
46 people were killed and more than 100 injured. Investigators' preliminary findings indicated that a fracture in the rail appeared to predate the derailment.
January 20: Train strikes fallen wall near Barcelona
A commuter train crashed near Gelida, outside Barcelona, after a retaining wall collapsed onto the tracks during heavy rain.
One person was killed and 41 were injured, according to the Spanish government.
February 12: Motorist killed at a railroad crossing
A passenger train traveling from Madrid to Badajoz struck a car at a railroad crossing near Talavera de la Reina, in Toledo province.
The car's driver was killed. None of the 206 passengers aboard the train were injured, according to regional emergency services.
October 28: High-speed trains collide on line between Madrid and Seville
Two high-speed trains, both traveling from Madrid to Seville, collided near Urda, in Toledo province, killing at least 68 people and injuring dozens more, regional authorities said.

Spain to observe 3 days of mourning after rail collision
Spain will observe three days of mourning after the death of at least 68 people in a high-speed train collision on Wednesday, Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez has announced.
"All of Spain is in mourning," he said from the scene of the crash in the central region of Castilla-La Mancha, adding that it was "not acceptable for a country like Spain" to suffer two such disasters in the same year.
Renfe train was told to continue journey despite red signal, transport minister says
The Renfe high-speed train that crashed into another stationary train had been told to continue its journey "as normal" despite the fact that all trains on the section of track in Toledo province had been told to stop, Spain's Transport Minister Oscar Puente has said.
Puente told a press conference at the ADIF control center in Madrid that the control system at the train station in Urda had failed due to a computer error.
That failure meant that a 63-kilometer section of track had no control system, which triggered an automatic stop, or "red signal," for all rail traffic on the parts of the track affected.
Operators at the control center ADIF, the company that is responsible for managing and maintaining Spain's railway infrastructure, took over communications.
"At 8:36 a.m. the Renfe train was told to resume its journey 'with normality'," Puente explained, a decision he described as "inexplicable."
He said that between that time and the moment of impact at 8:53, the Renfe train had traveled about 25 kilometers (15.5 miles), likely reaching a speed of over 100 kilometers (60 miles) per hour.
During the press conference, Puente refused to respond to questions about whether he would resign in the wake of the crash, saying his attention was on clarifying the circumstances that led to the crash and supporting the victims.

Do we know what caused the accident?
A train operated by the private Iryo consortium was reportedly told to stop by rail infrastructure manager ADIF due to a faulty signal, a person briefed on the disaster told the Reuters news agency.
Government officials have not confirmed what caused the train crash, and neither Renfe nor Iryo have commented on the cause.
DW has spoken to Nick Kingsley, the executive editor of the Railway Gazette International, about what might have caused the crash, the second fatal accident to hit Spain's high-speed rail network this year.
Kingsley described it as "a dark day for the Spanish rail industry" and said there would be "profound questions asked" as to how this accident occurred.
While he cautioned that it was still too early to speculate, Kingsley said: "The key point with high-speed railway is you design safety systems and especially signalling and train control systems such that the trains do not crash. This should never happen."
He said investigators would be looking into whether there are any similarities with the fatal accident in January.
"The fact that these two accidents happened in the same year when we had 30+ years without a single fatal accident on a high-speed rail network anywhere in the world, to now have this happen twice in one year in the same country is quite shocking," Kingsley told DW.
What are investigators going to be looking at?
"If reports that one crashed into the back of the other are confirmed, that will raise questions about the protection of those sections of track," Kingsley said.
He explained that the "fundamental principle of railway operation is that you have a signalling system that is designed to prevent two trains from occupying the same section of track. This is a fundamental tenet of railway operation."
Kingsley added that it will take a long time, possibly several weeks or even months, "to establish a definitive sequence of events."
