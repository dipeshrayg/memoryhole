Climate negotiators have removed any mention of fossil fuels - the main source of greenhouse gas emissions heating the planet - in a new draft deal published by COP31 summit co-host Australia.
The latest text released Thursday at the UN climate conference in Turkey stripped out two references to a transition away from coal, oil and gas and a call for an accelerated phaseout that had been in an earlier draft.
This slimmed down version follows days of tough negotiations with representatives from nearly 200 countries on issues such as the future of fossil fuels and climate funding for developing countries.
The text also includes a call to increase the global share of final energy demand met by electricity to 35% by 2035 - up from around 20% today - a proposal that had been pushed by host Turkey.
It did not specify whether that additional electricity needs to come from clean sources, a point that has drawn criticism from some countries. But it did link the target to efforts to triple renewable energy capacity globally alongside other energy efficiency measures.
'Little clarity' on climate financing
Another hotly contested issue at the talks has been financial support to help developing countries deal with climate change. Divisions largely revolve around who should bear the costs and whether the funding should come in the form of grants or loans.
The second draft called for further discussions on a tripling of funding for climate adaptation measures in developing countries to at least $120 billion (€109 billion). It also noted "with concern the gap between climate finance flows and needs," with the adaptation costs in developing countries estimated at $305 billion annually up to 2035.
Sandra Guzman, director of the regional network Climate Finance Group for Latin America and the Caribbean (GFLAC), welcomed the reference to tripling adaptation finance for developing countries, but said there was "little clarity in its implementation. Unfortunately, we are running out of time."
The text prompted criticism from Meenakshi Raman, coordinator of the climate change program at the Third World Network, who had been "hoping to see much more ambition on the side of finance from the developed world."
"Coming here really confirmed the worst fears that developed countries are not only backsliding but putting nothing in terms of real meaning on the table," said Raman.
What else is in the draft text?
The draft text also expressed "deep concern" that global warming is projected to exceed 1.5 degrees Celsius (2.7 degrees Fahrenheit) above pre-industrial levels - the threshold outlined in the 2015 Paris Agreement to avoid runaway climate change.
It acknowledged that every increment of warming above current levels would "result in increasingly adverse, and in some cases irreversible, impacts and risks to human and natural systems."
Australian Climate Change and Energy Minister Chris Bowen said the text aimed to communicate that "1.5 degrees of warming is not acceptable and will not be allowed to stand."
The draft also recognized the need for "deep, rapid and sustained" cuts to greenhouse gas emissions and called for efforts to ramp up action targeting the potent greenhouse gas methane. The greenhouse gas emissions heating the planet are largely caused by burning fossil fuels.
The text additionally calls for the deployment of technologies that can remove carbon from the atmosphere to be sped up - those technologies are as yet largely unproven at scale.
Harjeet Singh of the Fill the Fund campaign to boost climate finance for developing countries said the second draft "trims away inconvenient mandates and accountability while leaving behind lofty rhetoric that won't protect a single vulnerable community."
"It is actively more regressive, stripping out hard-won accountability, timelines, and legal anchors just as frontline communities in the Global South are paying the steepest price for climate breakdown," said Singh in a statement.
Talks in crunch time
The climate conference officially ends on Friday, but it's not uncommon for the annual talks to run overtime. The latest text is expected to face further revisions before it can be adopted.
Decisions on the language need approval from all countries, meaning an objection from one party is enough to scupper a deal.
On Thursday, Panama said it would block any agreement that does not meet its demand for a cap on oil production and steps to eliminate the potent greenhouse gas methane.
The country's environment minister, Juan Carlos Navarro, said Panama "not lend its name" to "complacent or ineffective language" in the final pact.
Bowen said he expected countries to bring forward their concerns, feedback and questions in the coming hours and overnight.
"We won't be leaving the rooms of Antalya until we have that consensus, until we have a plan we can put to the parties on behalf of the countries and governments they represent, and they can say, yes, we can do this: we can keep the world to 1.5 degrees of warming."
The stakes are high for this edition of the COP summit, which comes in a year of major climate-related disasters, from wildfires and deadly heat waves in Europe to a glacier collapse in Nepal that killed more than 1,400 people.
It's also taking place against the backdrop of an intensifying "super" El Nino weather pattern, and geopolitical shocks that have sent energy prices rising globally.
It's the first conference taking place with the acknowledgement by the UN that keeping the average global temperature under 1.5C is no longer realistic. Instead, scientists are talking about limiting the time spent above this threshold and bringing temperatures back down rapidly by the end of the century.
