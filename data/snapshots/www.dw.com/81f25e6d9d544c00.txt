It's been quite a while since the European Union's last enlargement, when Croatia joined the bloc back in 2013.
If everything goes according to plan, another might soon be on the cards: Montenegro, with a population of just 620,000, could complete accession negotiations in the coming months and join the EU in early 2028.
But no sooner had EU Enlargement Commissioner Marta Kos made this announcement in October than news emerged that Croatia might veto Montenegro's accession unless the country meets a series of Croatian demands. EU politicians and MEPs were dismayed.
This is not the first time something like this has happened: The veto rights that member states have on many EU matters have increasingly been used by individual countries in recent years as a means of leverage or obstruction, paralyzing the EU in the process.
DW takes a look at some of the most significant cases, the background to them and what are - in some instances - the serious political consequences of them.
Montenegro: Croatia's eight demands
Montenegro is the most advanced EU candidate country, with a potential accession date of 2028. It has already provisionally closed several of the EU's 33 negotiating chapters.
As of November 23, however, Croatia is blocking the closure of three chapters that have already been negotiated.
Zagreb is demanding, among other things, compensation for former prisoners held in Montenegrin camps during the Yugoslav wars of the 1990s, clarification of the fate of Croatians who have been missing since that time, and more rigorous prosecution of war crimes.
There are also disputes over the precise maritime boundary between the two countries. In addition, Croatia is demanding payment of about €2.4 billion (about $2.6 billion) for assets that belonged to the Yugoslav People's Army, the army of the former Yugoslavia to which both countries belonged before its breakup in the early 1990s.
Montenegro has already responded to some of Croatia's demands, including those concerning compensation for former prisoners. Others, such as the one relating to the assets of the Yugoslav People's Army, are disputed.
It is clear that Croatia's list of demands is also driven by domestic politics. No government in Zagreb can afford to appear compliant on issues relating to the Croatian War of Independence (1991-95).
Moreover, moderate conservative Prime Minister Andrej Plenkovic, who is regularly accused in Croatia of being too pro-EU, can use the dispute to portray himself as a staunch defender of national interests.
Ukraine: Hungarian domestic politics in the name of a minority
After Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine, the EU in June 2022 granted Ukraine and the Republic of Moldova EU candidate status through an accelerated procedure as a gesture of solidarity.
Many EU member states have reservations about Ukraine joining the bloc quickly. Nevertheless, only Hungary, under former Prime Minister Viktor Orban, adopted an obstructive stance.
Ostensibly, Hungary's obstruction was about getting Ukraine to improve the rights of the Hungarian minority in Transcarpathia in western Ukraine. But Orban used the matter for domestic political purposes and as an election campaign issue, portraying Ukraine as a "mafia state."
In an effort to appeal to widespread anti-Ukrainian sentiment in Hungary, the country's new prime minister, Peter Magyar, has also adopted a tough stance on Ukraine.
Nevertheless, Magyar's government lifted several temporary blocks on the opening of new negotiating chapters after the Ukrainian parliament passed a law in early October strengthening the educational rights of national minorities.
North Macedonia: The never-ending blockade
No EU candidate country has suffered as much or as long from veto politics as North Macedonia: It was granted EU candidate status in 2005 but only began accession negotiations in July 2022.
Greece blocked the start of negotiations for 13 years because it objected to the name "Republic of Macedonia," as the country was previously known, and accused its neighbor of laying territorial claims to the region of Macedonia in northern Greece.
The dispute was settled in 2018 through a compromise that saw the country change its name to the Republic of North Macedonia the following year.
But just as the path to accession negotiations seemed clear, Bulgaria blocked the opening of talks in November 2020.
Bulgaria regards the Macedonian language as a dialect of Bulgarian and disputes the existence of a distinct Macedonian nation.
Here, too, domestic Bulgarian politics is ultimately a factor.
Former Bulgarian President and current Prime Minister Rumen Radev, for example, fueled the escalation with North Macedonia to enhance his political profile.
Serbia: Croatia is not the only country obstructing progress
Although Serbia has been negotiating EU accession since 2014, little progress has been made.
In July, when the country was still led by President Aleksandar Vucic, no less than eight EU member states - including Croatia - opposed opening further negotiating chapters with Serbia.
While the main reservations relate to democratic shortcomings, deficiencies in the rule of law and corruption in Serbia, Croatia also has its own disputes with its eastern neighbor.
These concern the 1991-95 war, including missing persons, war crimes, minority rights and differing interpretations of history. Relations are also strained by nationalist rhetoric on both sides.
Whether the election victory of the Student List in Belgrade in late October , after almost two years of anti-government and anti-corruption protests, will change this situation is impossible to say.
Albania: Greece threatens to use minority rights as leverage
The EU opened accession negotiations with Albania, which has been an EU candidate since 2014, in July 2022.
Albania could become the bloc's next new member after Montenegro. However, Greece has repeatedly threatened (most recently in June) to block the accession process unless the rights of its Greek minority, particularly their property rights, are adequately protected.
An end to the veto system?
In light of all this, Brussels has long been debating a reform of the accession process and the EU veto system.
In early October, the European Commission proposed that decisions to open negotiations with candidate countries be made by qualified majority rather than unanimously. It also proposed that new EU members would be required to give up the right to veto decisions in certain policy areas altogether for a specified period.
The problem with these proposed reforms is that they would themselves have to be adopted unanimously. In other words, any member state could veto the reform.
