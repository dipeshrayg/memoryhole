Minimum wage violations cost Germany billions, union says
Illegal practices used to evade Germany's minimum wage have cost workers, social security funds and the state as much as €64 billion over a decade, according to calculations by the German Trade Union Confederation (DGB).
The DGB estimates that an average of around two million workers a year have received less than the legal minimum since it was introduced in 2015.
The shortfall amounted to roughly €1.70 to €2.40 per hour, based on survey data on working hours and earnings.
For the period from 2015 to 2024, the union calculated that workers missed out on around €35 billion in pay.
A further €22 billion was lost to social security funds and about €7 billion in income tax revenue.
Germany's main employers' association said minimum wage violations were unacceptable and should be investigated and prosecuted, but urged caution over the DGB figures.
It said survey data could be imprecise, particularly when working hours were not recorded accurately.
Germany's statutory minimum wage currently stands at €13.90 an hour and applies to around six million workers, especially in sectors such as hospitality, retail and agriculture.
Germany to speed up drone defenses at airports
Germany has promised to accelerate upgrades to drone detection and defense systems at its airports following last week's explosive-drone incident at Leipzig/Halle.
"Reliable detection is absolutely crucial," Transport Minister Steffen Bilger told the news website Politico on Monday.
Bilger said Germany's air navigation service provider is responsible for safety in the airspace around airports and that early detection therefore plays a key role.
He said Frankfurt and Munich are already better equipped, but warned that other airports need to catch up quickly.
"We have no time to lose," Bilger said. "I want to accelerate further upgrades wherever possible."
The push follows the discovery last week of a drone carrying an explosive device in a secure cargo area at Leipzig/Halle Airport.
A federal police bomb disposal team defused the device.
Several Ukrainian cargo aircraft were nearby at the time. The airport is used as a hub for military aid shipments to Ukraine.
Man shot dead near Berlin's Zoo station
A 30-year-old man has been shot dead following a dispute involving several people near Berlin's busy Zoologischer Garten station.
Paramedics tried to resuscitate the victim, but he died at the scene, police said.
The suspected attackers fled and remain at large.
Police did not say how many shots were fired shortly before midnight, and the circumstances surrounding the dispute remain unclear.
A homicide unit has taken over the investigation.
The shooting took place at one of western Berlin's busiest transport hubs, close to the Kurfürstendamm shopping boulevard and the Kaiser Wilhelm Memorial Church.
The case comes amid a marked rise in firearms incidents in the German capital.
Berlin police recorded 1,119 cases involving firearms in 2025, up 68% from 2024. Shots were actually fired in more than 500 of those cases.
Police chief Barbara Slowik Meisel said earlier this year that disputes once settled with fists were now increasingly involving guns.
Other recent cases include shots fired at a restaurant window in Neukölln early on Sunday and the shooting of a 38-year-old man in the city's Schöneberg district the previous weekend.
Rhine could become impassable at key shipping point
The Rhine River could soon become effectively split in two for commercial shipping as water levels continue to fall amid persistent dry weather, Germany's inland shipping association has warned.
Jens Schwanen, managing director of the Federal Association of German Inland Navigation, said the gauge at Kaub, a crucial point for freight traffic, was forecast to fall into single digits for the first time this week.
"Commercial shipping will no longer be able to transport goods in the region at such low water levels," Schwanen told the Rheinische Post newspaper.
He said excursion boats and river cruises would also be affected.
"In principle, the Rhine would then no longer be continuously navigable and would be divided into two," Schwanen said.
Shipping would still be possible between the ports of Amsterdam, Rotterdam and Antwerp and parts of North Rhine-Westphalia, roughly as far as Cologne.
Further south, traffic could continue on tributaries and connected waterways including the Neckar, Moselle, Main and Main-Danube Canal.
Historic low-water levels were recorded at several points on the Rhine in North Rhine-Westphalia over the weekend after weeks of dry weather and too little rainfall across the river's catchment area.
The falling river is already forcing freight vessels to carry much lighter loads, pushing up transport costs for industries that depend on bulk cargo.
