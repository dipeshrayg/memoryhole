A group of five EU member states, including Germany, agreed on a new scheme to deport rejected asylum seekers to Rwanda, on Monday.
A plan to set up so-called return hubs in a non-EU country had been announced by the group - which also includes Austria, the Netherlands, Greece and Denmark - back in September.
Monday's announcement comes after the EU introduced new regulations to speed up deportations and allow the establishment of hubs outside of the bloc.
EU policymakers have shifted focus toward improving their broken repatriation system, which currently sees less than 30 percent of people ordered to leave the bloc returned to their country of origin.
Speaking after Monday's meeting in Vienna, Austrian Chancellor Christian Stocker, who called the decision "a truly historic paradigm shift in migration policy," summed up the plan by saying, "If returning [migrants] to their home country is not possible, then in the future people without a right of residence will be sent back to a third country such as Rwanda."
Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen lauded the project as a "milestone" that will send a "signal... that you can no longer enter the European Union illegally and think that you can just stay around."
How are both sides spinning the deportation plan?
The five-nation group hopes to have the operation up and running in 2027, with sources calling it a pilot program that will house about 20 people from each of the countries at Monday's meeting.
Rwandan Interior Minister Vincent Biruta, who appeared alongside Stocker after the talks, put a positive spin on the plan, saying it would offer, "a second chance to people who no longer have the right to remain in the partner country."
Biruta said the site will not be a detention facility but rather a "welcome center" housing migrants who can live and work in the country.
He also brushed off harsh criticism of Rwanda's human rights record as "cliched."
That criticism came hard and fast at news of the scheme, with some 45 NGOs from 15 countries blasting it as "a new phase of harmful externalization policies" and the group Human Rights Watch pointing to Rwanda's "well-established record of repression of dissent, arbitrary detention, ill-treatment in detention."
What do we know about the EU deportation hub scheme?
Rwanda had not been officially named as the chosen country prior to Monday, but the central African country had acknowledged being in "preliminary discussions" with the group of states.
Rwanda had also been the intended destination for a similar plan set up under the UK's former Conservative government that subsequently collapsed in UK courts.
However, the new EU scheme differs from the British one in that it is only intended for migrants who have exhausted all other legal avenues within the European Union.
Despite the collapse of the UK deal, Rwanda is one of several countries taking migrants that President Donald Trump's administration has booted out of the US.
Despite declining numbers of asylum seeker arrivals due to deals with source countries in Africa and the Middle East, European governments see migration hubs as a means to visibly show that they are taking action on migration amid surging anti-migrant sentiment and rising support for the far right.
What are the criticisms of the deportation hub plans?
European leaders have said the scheme will comply with EU and national laws but that has not shielded them from criticism, especially over the plan to send migrants to a country with which they have no connection.
"These policies shift people, and responsibility for their safety and rights, out of public sight and beyond scrutiny," read Monday's NGO statement.
The Council of Europe, a pan-European human rights and rule of law body, said in July that return hubs posed "considerable human rights risks."
Claims that the scheme is meant to dissuade migrants from traveling to Europe have been rejected by researchers who say there is no evidence that deterrence works.
"Enforcement changes how people travel, not whether they travel. Some pause or change route, and smugglers adapt and raise their prices," said Roberto Forin of the Mixed Migration Centre, a research institute that collects data along migration routes.
The choice of Rwanda has also been controversial, with the government of President Paul Kagame in particular being accused of suppressing dissent and supporting the M23 rebel group in the neighboring Democratic Republic of Congo.
"Paying Kigali [Rwanda] to host a deportation program would not only cause suffering and harm to those deported, it would finance and provide political leverage to a government responsible for serious human rights abuses at home and abroad," said Human Rights Watch's senior Africa researcher, Clementine de Montjoye.
