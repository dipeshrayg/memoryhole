Welfare groups, unions urge Berlin to stop social spending cuts
A coalition of welfare groups and unions has urged the German government not to go through with planned cuts to social spending, particularly to the elderly care system.
"Social cohesion is a prerequisite for democratic stability," the coalition of 14 bodies said.
The German Cabinet, which consists of ministers belonging to Chancellor Friedrich Merz's center-right CDU, the Bavarian sister party CSU, and the center-left SPD, approved a bill late last month to reform the care system. The sector has come under increasing pressure due to the aging population.
The reform means that childless employees will pay higher surcharges for long-term care insurance and high-income earners will also have to pay more.
There will also be a reassessment of different care levels, which correspond to the benefits and services people receive. Care workers will also be burdened with additional tasks.
The reform, which aims to plug a budget gap of around €8 billion ($9 billion) in the coming year, still needs to undergo further steps in the legislative process, such as getting passed in the Bundestag.
The coalition, which includes the Verdi union and social welfare organizations such as Caritas, said it was essential that adequate care was provided alongside safe staffing levels.
The alliance said it was concerned the government was also planning cuts to other areas, including housing and youth support.
It also called for additional wealth and inheritance taxes.
Germany's environment minister calls for water policy overhaul
German Environment Minister Carsten Schneider said Germany needs to formulate a new approach to water policy after a summer of heat waves and low river levels.
"The days when we in Germany didn't have to worry about water are definitely over. Germany needs a different approach to water," Schneider told the Cologne-based Kölner Stadt-Anzeiger newspaper.
The minister cited low water levels on the Rhine River in western Germany and Lake Constance in the south as a problem for both the natural environment and the economy.
Schneider is expected to take part in a meeting on Monday in Berlin along with EU Environment Commissoner Jessika Roswall and representatives from the public, private and non-profit sectors. At the meeting, they will discuss what a new approach to water policy will look like.
The minister said technical water infrastructure must be upgraded to retain water in the landscape.
"If the mountains and their glaciers fail to supply water, rivers, floodplains, forest and soils must serve as water reservoirs," Schneider said.
The German government has provided €100 billion ($114 billion) to state and municipal authorities as part of a special fund to upgrade water infrastructure.
Deutsche Bahn to offer 'Family Ticket' permanently
Germany's rail operator Deutsche Bahn intends to offer its "Family Ticket" (Familienticket) deal permanently from November.
The "Family Ticket" is a €99.99 roundtrip to and from a destination for two adults and several kids up to a total of five people. The "Family Ticket" includes seat reservations on the high-speed Intercity Express (ICE) train.
The deal was first offered this summer and led to a significant rise in Deutsche Bahn ridership.
"We were able to welcome 100,000 more families on board our trains than last year," Deutsche Bahn CEO Evelyn Palla told the Bild am Sonntag newspaper.
The "Family Ticket" will be available for purchase starting on October 26.
Although families may be able to get a nice deal on a ticket, they may also still have to reckon with delays and cancellations that have plagued Deutsche Bahn in recent years.
"A large part of our infrastructure is too old, too prone to malfunctions, and breaks down too often," Palla said. "We can only renew the system bit by bit and cannot make up in one or two years what has been neglected for many years:"
The Deutsche Bahn CEO said said renovations on the rail network will take at least 10 years and improvements will only be noticed by passengers once a large part of the rail equipment has been replaced.

Nearly half of Germans think Merkel better suited than Merz to tackle Germany's challenges
A new survey from YouGov which was commissioned by German news agency DPA found that 48% of Germans believe former Chancellor Angela Merkel is better suited to tackle Germany's challenges than current Chancellor Friedrich Merz.
The poll also found that 29% of Germans believe the two leaders would be equally capable of confronting Germany's problems.
Only 8% viewed Merkel less favorably than Merz; 15% didn't answer the survey or said they didn't know how to respond.
Both Merkel and Merz are members of the center-right Christian Democratic Union, but have long been political rivals.
Merkel served as chancellor for a period of 16 years from 2005 to 2021.
Merz, meanwhile, faces record low approval ratings since becoming chancellor in May last year.
Germany's problems include a stagnant economy, rising energy prices, an aging population with fewer working to support social programs, illegal immigration and foreign policy challenges such as the Russian invasion of Ukraine, among other issues.

Thousands attend anti-war demonstrations in Berlin, Stuttgart
Thousands of people protested against war, military buildup and possible conscription in Berlin and Stuttgart on Saturday.
Among other things, the protesters called for a end to all economic sanctions and arms shipments, calling instead for increased social spending.
Protesters in Berlin marched from the Brandenburg Gate through the city center. Police estimated there were 5,800 attendees while organizers put the turnout at 20,000 people.
The organizers in Stuttgart estimated a turnout of around 15,000 people. However, dpa journalists who were present at the demonstration estimated the turnout to be in the low to mid thousands.
After Bremen, Düsseldorf next up to host German Unity Day
Düsseldorf has been announced as the host city for next year's official commemoration of German Unity Day.
The annual event is hosted by whichever state holds the rotating presidency of the upper house of Germany's parliament, known as the Bundesrat. In 2027, it will be held by Germany's most populous state, North Rhine-Westphalia.
Düsseldorf is the second-largest city in North-Rhine Westphalia, after Cologne. The state is also home to the industrial cities of the Ruhr Valley including Dortmund, Essen, Duisburg and Bochum.
The mayor of Bremen, Andreas Bovenschulte, symbolically handed the baton to North Rhine-Westphalia's premier Hendrik Wüst, who will take up his post as president of the Bundesrat on January 1.
"Togetherness, connection, and unity are never a finished task," said Wüst.
"Continuing to work on this, maintaining one's interest, and keeping the conversation going - even with people whose outlook might be completely different from one's own," he added.
East German economy still trails west 36 years after reunification
More than three decades after German reunification, the economy in the eastern states still lags beind the western states.
The five eastern German states reached just under 79% of western Germany's economic level in 2025, according to an analysis by the German Economic Institute (IW) released on German Unity Day.
The figure is based on an index designed to track eastern Germany's economic convergence with the west. It incorporates economic output per capita, productivity, investment, research and development, employment, unemployment and self-employment.
Although the east has made substantial progress since 1991, when the index stood at 51% of the west's economic development, this convergence has stalled over the past five years, hovering between between 78% and 79%.
The researchers pointed to large shortcomings in research and development (R&D) in particular. They found that the number of research and development personnel in the east was equivalent to just over 46% of the same workforce in the west.
The researchers also noted that demographic change could affect economic convergence between the east and the west.
Without immigration, eastern Germany's population will shrink by more than a fifth by 2045. This would make it particularly dependent on skilled migration.
But the German Economic Institute noted that support for the far-right, anti-immigration Alternative for Germany party (AfD) in recent state elections has called into question the region's openness to migration.
Oktoberfest crowds surge toward 7 million mark
Munich's Oktoberfest is on course for one of its busiest years, with 5.2 million visitors recorded through Wednesday.
That was around 700,000 more than at the same point last year, according to anonymized mobile phone data analyzed for the dpa news agency.
If the strong attendance continues through the final weekend, visitor numbers could approach 7 million. The record stands at 7.2 million in 2023, although that year's Oktoberfest ran for two days longer.
Good weather is thought to have helped boost attendance this year.
International visitors now account for around 31% of the crowd, six percentage points more than two years ago. Americans are the largest group of foreign visitors, followed by Italians.
Around 58,000 Italians attended during the middle weekend alone, traditionally known in Munich as "Italian weekend."
Visitors are also getting older. The average age this year is 46, with people aged 50 to 59 making up the largest group at around 23%. The share of 18- to 29-year-olds has fallen by 5.1 percentage points compared with two years ago to 21.5%.
Visitors spend an average of just over five and a half hours at the festival.

While some feel eastern Germans remain disadvantaged, others say their generation shares the same concerns about jobs, identity and the future.

Berlin parties move closer to coalition talks
Berlin's Left Party, Greens and Social Democrats (SPD) have agreed on a "joint path forward" toward potentially forming a new state government.
The three parties issued a brief statement after their second round of preliminary talks, saying they would bring the current discussions "to a conclusion" next week. They gave no further details.
The socialist Left Party emerged as the strongest party in Berlin's September 20 state election and is seeking to form a government with the Greens and the center-left SPD.
The Greens and SPD have demanded agreement on issues including antisemitism, racism and organized crime before they are prepared to begin formal exploratory coalition talks. Concerns over alleged antisemitism within parts of the Berlin Left have been a particular point of contention.
The parties have not said when they will meet again or whether the preliminary discussions will lead to formal exploratory talks.
Such talks would still fall short of formal coalition negotiations and are intended to establish whether the parties have enough common ground to negotiate a government agreement.
German asylum applications rise but remain sharply lower
The number of people filing first-time asylum applications in Germany rose in September compared with the previous month, but remained well below levels seen in recent years.
The Federal Office for Migration and Refugees (BAMF) received 5,276 first-time applications, according to the Interior Ministry.
That was 40% fewer than in September 2025 and 70% fewer than in September 2024.
First-time applications had fallen below 4,000 in both July and August, after around 5,550 were recorded in May.
Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt, of Bavaria's conservative Christian Social Union, said the longer-term decline showed that measures taken by Germany and the European Union were working.
"The significant decline in asylum numbers shows that our measures at national and European level are having an effect," Dobrindt said.
The number of subsequent asylum applications has meanwhile risen in recent months, partly following a 2024 European Court of Justice ruling concerning Afghan women.
The court found that the discriminatory measures imposed on women by Afghanistan's Taliban rulers could in themselves constitute persecution and justify refugee status.
