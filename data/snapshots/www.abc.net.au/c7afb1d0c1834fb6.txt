What's next?
The Reserve Bank will meet to decide interest rates at its final meeting of the year, concluding on December 8.
Housing affordability has improved only slightly despite rapidly falling home values, with higher borrowing costs offsetting much of the benefit to new buyers, according to a new report.
A household on the median Australian income that bought a median-priced home at the end of September would still find its mortgage payments eating up 49.5 per cent of its pre-tax income, the figures from property data and research firm Cotality indicate.
That calculation assumes the household had a 20 per cent deposit and took out a 30-year loan at the average discounted variable interest rate for owner-occupiers.

A common definition of "mortgage stress" is when repayments eat up more than 30 per cent of a household's pre-tax income.
Home values would need to fall by a further $345,000 to make housing "affordable" on this yardstick, Cotality analysis finds.
Cotality's head of research, Gerard Burg, said higher interest rates meant softer home prices had not necessarily translated into greater purchasing power for buyers.
"Higher mortgage rates have offset much of the benefit of falling property prices,"
Mr Burg said.
No escape to the country
Meanwhile, a surge in regional home prices in recent years is posing a challenge for people looking to flee expensive cities.
Adelaide has also topped Sydney in this latest report as Australia's least affordable capital city relative to local incomes.
The median Adelaide home price of $928,560 - including units and freestanding houses - at the end of September was 9.5 times median household incomes there.
"Regional housing can still represent good value for someone moving out of a capital city who can retain their existing income, but it's a very different proposition for someone earning a local wage,"
Mr Burg said.
Overall, regional homes across Australia are now worth 8.4 times local household incomes, compared to an average of 8 times in capital cities.
Prices still elevated over past five years
Nationally, falling home values have caused a slight fall in the value of homes relative to incomes.
The median home price is now 8.2 times the median annual household income as at the end of September, down from a record high of 8.7 times in March this year.
This income data is based on modelling by the ANU Centre for Social Policy and Research and assumes a median household income before tax of $110,037 in September, up 21.6 per cent over the previous five years.
This has been dwarfed, however, by a 33 per cent rise in the median home price over the same period to $899,236 at the end of September.
Rent hikes a challenge for home savers
Rental price pain has been even more pronounced, with rents rising by 49.4 per cent over the half-decade.
The median rental payment now absorbs 33.7 per cent of the median annual household income.
This rental squeeze made it hard for aspiring home buyers to save a deposit, Mr Burg said.
"For renters whose housing costs have risen much faster than their incomes, falling property prices may bring down the deposit they need, but that doesn't necessarily mean they're able to save it any faster."
The report said it would take the typical aspiring buyer 10.9 years to save a 20 per cent deposit, assuming they could save 15 per cent of their annual income towards a deposit.
This is slightly reduced from a peak of 11.6 years back in March before home prices began to fall, but remains the fifth-highest level recorded in the Cotality quarterly report.
Challenges to affordability remain
The figures do not include the impact of the Reserve Bank's latest interest rate hike earlier this month.
Mr Burg said the latest rate rise would continue to weigh on affordability, even if prices continue to fall.
"Lasting improvement in affordability will depend on building enough homes where people want to live as well as household incomes growing faster than housing costs over time,"
he said.
