In short:
Australia wants to shape the world order on AI and needs to move quickly in response to the rapidly emerging technology, Foreign Minister Penny Wong says.
It comes amid fears that the construction of AI data centres in Australia could be vulnerable to foreign actors.
What's next?
The major foreign policy speech will be delivered in parliament on Tuesday.
Artificial intelligence will be elevated as a "major foreign policy and national security challenge" this week, with Foreign Minister Penny Wong warning that the emerging technology is giving foreign actors new tools to disrupt democracies.
Senator Wong will say that AI is transforming military power and reordering our lives and economies "in ways we cannot fully foresee" in a major foreign policy speech to be delivered to parliament on Tuesday.
"We have long known actors beyond our shores seek to interfere in Australia's democracy and in the lives of those who call Australia home," she will say.
"These tools make that interference faster, cheaper and harder to recognise."
She will refer to reports of disinformation, including an Iranian operation to use fake journalist personas to publish articles in a bid to sway opinion on the US-Iran conflict, and Russian covert operations to harness AI-generated deepfakes to influence US elections.
Senator Wong's speech comes amid warnings from Australia's spy chief about foreign interference in the construction of AI data centres.
ASIO director general Mike Burgess, in an interview with 60 Minutes, warned that foreign adversaries could seek some form of control over AI infrastructure covertly and "for nefarious reasons".
It also comes amid continued fallout over reports that Jensen Huang, head of powerful AI chip company Nvidia, was having an affair with a suspected Chinese spy.
Mr Burgess's remarks follow US President Donald Trump warning of "tremendous" consequences for American tech companies building AI data centres overseas.
Home Affairs Minister Tony Burke said there was a national security interest in building data centres in Australia, acknowledging Mr Burgess's remarks that with the AI boom came hype, opportunity and threat.
"Whenever you get that sort of capability onshore, it also becomes a magnet for people who would want to conduct foreign interference," Mr Burke told News24 on Sunday.
"So, we need to make sure that we are alert and that we are being very careful in the ownership structures that happen in Australia for data centres."
Senator Wong will say that Australia is strengthening critical technology and cyber resilience, as well as legislating mandatory AI safety standards.
Australia wants to shape world in relation to AI
Senator Wong will say that Australia cannot wait for international treaties and must move swiftly in the face of AI's rapid development.
"We must do what we can to ensure that the race to develop frontier AI, concentrated in a handful of companies and countries, does not reward speed over prudence," she will say.
"Australia is working with partners on AI safety, making it a shared, global endeavour."
She will say that Australia has helped to shape the international order on nuclear proliferation and by taking a lead in online safety.
"With artificial intelligence, we intend to do it again."
The sentiments echo those made by Prime Minister Anthony Albanese at the UN in September, where he called for global cooperation on AI and argued for safety guardrails on the technology.
Over the weekend, Australia and China agreed to hold talks on AI, following Mr Albanese's visit to China for the APEC summit.
Senator Wong's speech, titled Securing Australia's future in a changing world, effectively replaces the concept of the foreign policy white paper.
The last foreign policy white paper was published in 2017.
In June, Senator Wong told Senate Estimates that such documents quickly become outdated, and she instead preferred to outline the government's foreign policy through major speeches and statements to parliament.
