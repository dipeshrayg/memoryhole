The COP31 UN climate summit has ended without a deal after debate broke down over a draft which failed to mention fossil fuels.
The main deal was rejected on Sunday afternoon, local time, by a host of nations, 44 hours after the summit's official close and following two weeks of negotiations. It marked the longest UN climate summit ever.
In a chaotic finale to the talks in the Turkish city of Antalaya, debate continued in overtime until COP31 presidents Australia and Türkiye confirmed they would not put forward another draft of the deal.
Several countries and the 27-nation European Union said they could not accept the final proposal from the joint Turkish-Australian leadership that did not mention fossil fuels, slamming it as too weak.
"We will not endorse a cover decision ... that does not even mention fossil fuels, which is the key driver of climate change," said Ireland's climate minister, Darragh O'Brien, whose country holds the rotating EU presidency.
Developing countries also criticised the deal for not providing sufficient climate finance to help them adapt to a warming planet. The deal had called for a tripling of such finance, but did not specify from what baseline.
A significant number of nations supported the proposal, even reluctantly, saying that consensus was more important. Any pact approved at the talks requires a near-unanimous consensus.
Russia and Saudi Arabia, however, supported the decision to leave the explicit mention of fossil fuels out of the final deal.
Australian minister for climate and energy Chris Bowen, who headed the summit alongside Türkiye's Murat Kurum, said the outcome was "very disappointing".
"Ultimately looking back many parties who are people of good faith had very strong red lines on a couple of key issues and despite many many attempts on reflection those red lines were not able to be met," he said.
"Australia left nothing on the field."
While a blow to efforts to ratchet up international action, the failure to agree a political declaration in Antalya does not erase the obligations agreed by previous UN conferences or remove the technical and legal frameworks governing multilateral action against climate change.

Expectations had already been low heading into the two-week negotiations but discussions about explicitly including fossil fuels - the chief cause of global warming - and other issues including money proved irreconcilable.
This year's COP is the first since the UN's environmental body in October confirmed the rise in global temperature was set to exceed 1.5 degrees Celsius, a threshold countries agreed at Paris climate talks over a decade ago to try not to exceed and beyond which extreme weather impacts intensify.
Confusion ran rife over the final draft of the deal, as Mr Kurum gave negotiators only about three hours to make objections or request changes before the draft was to be presented to the summit's final meeting.
Negotiators began to leave Antalaya as the talks entered double overtime, raising concerns over whether enough would remain to pass the draft.
After announcing EU states would object to the deal, Mr O'Brien said the talks had been "hijacked by some oil states".
The latest text introduced an oblique reference to a previous global deal in 2023 to shift the world away from fossil fuels but stops short of naming them explicitly.
That 2023 deal marks one of only a few global climate deals which names fossil fuels, with previous COP deals from the past 25 years also leaving the term out.
"Fossil fuels - we tried to strike a balance," Mr Bowen said.
"In the end it wasn't enough ... I would have been happy with the transition away from fossil fuels being in the text, but that was not going to get support from parties.
"Given it's the existing position of [previous] COPs ... parties couldn't live with that."
United Nations Secretary General Antonio Guterres said he shared a "profound frustration" with those "who expected governments to rise to the moment the climate crisis demands".
"Those who profit from the destruction of our planet must never dictate the terms of the fight to save it. Fossil fuels are driving the climate crisis."
The failed deal had included a goal put forward by Türkiye to expand electricity's share of total final world energy consumption to 35 per cent by 2035, from around 23 per cent now.
It also included a specific goal for supporting technology that captures carbon pollution and stores it, which is hotly debated and backed by the oil industry.
The last time UN climate talks failed to get a deal adopted by all was in Copenhagen in 2009.
