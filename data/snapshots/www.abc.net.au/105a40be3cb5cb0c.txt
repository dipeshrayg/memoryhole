In short:
The amount older Australians contribute to Support at Home services should be reviewed, a parliamentary committee has found.
A year after Labor's sweeping aged care changes took effect, the Senate committee into the Support at Home program has made 19 recommendations, including allowing clinicians to override algorithm outcomes.
The Senate inquiry has been extended for another 12 months.
Co-payments elderly Australians make towards the subsidised care they receive at home should be reviewed and clinicians should be able to override controversial assessment algorithms, a parliamentary committee has found.
A year on from the federal government's aged care overhaul, a Senate committee has recommended the Health Department review the amount elderly Australians pay towards their Support at Home services, such as cleaning, food preparation or gardening.
Senators from across the parliament also called on the government to "immediately" change the law to allow for humans to override "at the point of assessment" the algorithms used in the assessment process.
The Coalition and crossbenchers, including the Greens, have been highly critical of the government's Support at Home program and the two algorithms used to determine the level of funding someone requires and how quickly they should be given that money.
Controversially, the algorithms cannot be overridden by human clinicians, prompting the government to establish an "escalation pathway" so people in "exceptional circumstances" can have their outcomes reviewed.
After staunchly defending the algorithms as a way to improve consistency, the government also announced in October it would review them.
Under the changes that took effect in November last year, older people are required to contribute more to the non-clinical care they receive at home.
Recipients cover between 5 and 80 per cent of the service, depending on the type of help and whether or not they are a full or part pensioner.
Services such as cleaning and meal preparation demand a higher contribution, compared to remedial or respite care.
But the committee recommended the Department of Health, Disability and Ageing review this model to assess the affects on older people with varying incomes and needs, and to determine if co-payments "discourage timely access to care".
The committee was supposed to deliver a final report, but has instead made an interim report and extended the inquiry until November next year.
On that basis, Labor senators said they did not intend to comment on the content and recommendations of the interim findings.
"[We] instead reserve our consideration of the work of the committee until the time it presents its final report to the Senate," Labor senators said in additional comments.
Coalition and Greens unite in criticising program
Inquiry chair and Greens spokesperson for older people Senator Penny Allman-Payne said Labor had "botched" the Support at Home program and added "baffling layers of bureaucracy and complexity".
"Not only are providers jacking up their prices, the system rations and denies care, systematically underassessing need and priority and creating artificial scarcity by withholding packages," she said.
Shadow Aged Care Minister Anne Ruston said many of the problems were either predictable or known to the government before the changes began.
"Labor dismissed them and now a Senate committee has confirmed every word," she said.
"The committee has backed what the Coalition has said from day one: an algorithm should never have the final say over an older person's care," Senator Ruston said.
"At a bare minimum, Labor must restore human override now, and stop short-changing older Australians with 60 per cent packages while hundreds of millions of dollars sit unspent," she said.
Government faces criticism for aged care underspend
During Question Time on Thursday, Centre Alliance MP Rebekha Sharkie echoed criticism from Liberal senator Anne Ruston earlier in the week about the government not spending its full aged care budget.
Ms Sharkie asked Aged Care Minister Sam Rae why Labor underspent by $1.4 billion last financial year.
"Ageing Australia stated they had capacity to deliver Support at Home to 30,000 additional older Australians," Ms Sharkie said.
Mr Rae said that was a "modest" part of the overall $44 billion budget, but nonetheless it had not been spent because providers were responding slower than anticipated to the need for more at-home packages and new residential beds.
"Since the Labor government was elected in 2022, we've effectively doubled the size of the aged care budget," Mr Rae said.
As of June 30, Mr Rae said more than 390,000 people were on ongoing packages, while more than 106,000 were waiting to receive their approved level of help.
According to data from earlier this year, more than 6,400 older Australians died while in the queue for their Support at Home funding.
Despite not securing the full $3 billion of savings the government was hoping to make from private health insurance rebate changes this week, Health Minister Mark Butler said Labor would still invest that full amount into aged care.
