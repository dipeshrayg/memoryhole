Election day has finally arrived in Victoria after a campaign marked by Labor's plummeting polling and a controversial last-minute policy dump from the Coalition.
Around half the state's 4.7 million eligible voters have already cast their ballots via postal votes and at early voting centres.
The remainder will head to polling booths today, in an election widely expected to bring about a change of government after 12 years of Labor.
The Coalition's campaign has not been without controversy, with Liberal leader Jess Wilson on the defensive after a last-minute reveal of planned privatisation and kinder cuts.
The Coalition's path to victory, which relies on picking up more than a dozen new seats, could also be complicated by the rise in popularity of One Nation.
The past four years have involved difficult chapters for both Labor and the Liberals, with three leadership changes each since Daniel Andrews and Matthew Guy led their parties to the 2022 election.
Andrews resigned from politics in 2023, handing Labor leadership and premier's job to Jacinta Allan.
Then as Labor's popularity plummeted amid Big Build corruption allegations and a controversial change to the emergency services levy, Ms Allan was backed into resigning this July.
After earlier denying he would challenge Allan, then-deputy premier Ben Carroll became the leader after what has been described as a chaotic party room meeting.
The Liberal Party has rolled out three leaders since the last election - Matthew Guy, Brad Battin and finally Jess Wilson.
But the Liberals' leadership challenges faded firmly into the past as the party surged ahead in the polls on the back of the spate of home invasions, car thefts, machete attacks and firebombings.
The Wilson-led opposition continued on a platform of law and order throughout the year, which played out as an extended election campaign, well in excess of the traditional six-week timeframes of the past.
Also straying from tradition, there was no leaders debate.
However Carroll, Wilson, One Nation's Warren Pickering and the Greens' Ellen Sandell did front a televised leaders forum, discussing questions from the audience.
One Nation and Greens expected to perform well
Labor is bracing for a devastating hit to the party's primary vote.
Despite calling a royal commission into the Big Build allegations, Carroll has also been weighed down by Victoria's soaring debt and two controversial leaders during Labor's years in government.
And while polling continues to suggest a Wilson-led Coalition will win today's election, the results are not expected to be all rosy.
They may enter government with an historically low primary vote.
Earlier predictions of One Nation's success have been tempered by more recent opinion polls that now have Pauline Hanson's party trailing the Coalition by some way.
It has been a bumpy campaign for the party with one candidate dumped over social media posts that praised Nazi dictator Adolf Hitler.
Another stepped down after anti-Muslim rhetoric and for making highly sexualised remarks including that seeking consent was "cringe".
Anti-violence campaigners accused yet another of making misogynistic comments on a video featuring former AFL player Dustin Martin.
Even party leader Warren Pickering had to front the media in September, admitting to a history of drug use and drink driving when he was younger, after allegations were aired by a former partner.
Still, One Nation is expected to attract more than 20 per cent of the primary vote.
Progressive voters are looking elsewhere, which has created hope among the Victorian Greens.
The party is looking to retain its seats in Melbourne, Richmond and Brunswick, and is optimistic about taking back Northcote and Prahran.
The Greens also have their eyes on Footscray, Pascoe Vale, and possibly Preston and Albert Park.
But Victorian Greens leader Ellen Sandell said the party was facing some tough hurdles.
"We know that Greens seats are actually always incredibly close," she said.
"This time, in particular, in order for the Greens to win a seat, we essentially have to get more votes than Labor, Liberal, One Nation combined because of the preference deals [they] have done with each other."
