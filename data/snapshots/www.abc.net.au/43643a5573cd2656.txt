In short:
Mary Peltola has defeated two-term Republican incumbent Dan S Sullivan in Alaska's US Senate race.
Alaska was the final Senate race to be called, giving the Democrats 56 seats, the GOP holding 44.
What's next?
The 120th Congress will swear in new members on January 3, 2027.
Mary Peltola has defeated two-term Republican Dan S Sullivan in Alaska to complete the best Senate performance in a US midterm election by the Democratic Party since 1958.
The Alaska race was the last Senate contest to be resolved and gave the Democrats a 56-44 majority after they flipped nine GOP-held seats to retake the chamber.
Due to the logistical challenges of collecting ballots from across the sparsely populated state, it took two weeks of counting to call an Alaska winner after the November 3 poll.
With 99 per cent of ballots counted, Ms Peltola had slightly more than a 50 per cent share, while Mr Sullivan trailed with less than 46 per cent.
The party also won campaigns in other conservative states, including South Carolina, Texas, Kansas and Mississippi.
Ms Peltola's win overcame allegations that members of her campaign helped recruit a candidate who shares the Republican incumbent's name and completes a political comeback in a state US President Donald Trump won by wide margins in 2024.
Independent Daniel J Sullivan Jr won 1.65 per cent of the vote. But even with his votes combined with those for the GOP's Mr Sullivan, Ms Peltola still eked out a narrow victory.
A spokesperson for Dan S Sullivan did not respond to a request for comment, but said on Monday that the campaign would let the process "run its course until every legal vote is counted and certified".
Dan J Sullivan has denied any contact with the Peltola campaign or running at the Democrats' behest, but the US Department of Justice filed a lawsuit pending an investigation into possible voter confusion.
A jubilant Ms Peltola said voters sent a clear message that "the status quo in DC is not working for Alaska".
"Over the course of this campaign, I've spoken with elders in tears about empty fish racks; families struggling to find housing they can afford; and parents in the bush faced with the impossible choice of heating their homes or putting food on the table," she said.
"Today we take a monumental step forward in addressing those issues and putting Alaska first."
Ms Peltola, a 53-year-old mother of seven, including three stepchildren, played up her rural roots during the campaign.
She tapped into Alaskans' frustrations with high fuel prices and other costs. Those concerns hit especially hard in remote communities that rely on diesel for electricity and heat, and are generally only accessible by air or water.
Her campaign peppered social media with photos of her travels around the state as she sought to separate herself from national Democrats and build a coalition of support around a platform of "Alaska first".
"Alaskans who know me know that I'm not liberal or radical,"
Ms Peltola said.
"I am only interested in working for Alaska and working for the best interest of Alaskans, in as nonpartisan of a way as I possibly can."
The strategy resonated with voters who felt they could relate to Ms Peltola, who is Yup'ik and often spoke about growing up in western Alaska.
Winning Alaska was pivotal to Democrats' plans to retake the Senate.
The party's flip of nine Senate seats represents the most since the 1958 midterms, when they wrested away 13 Republican-held seats.
The incoming 56-seat Democratic caucus is the most Senate seats held by Democrats since January 3, 2011, at the conclusion of the 111th Congress.
