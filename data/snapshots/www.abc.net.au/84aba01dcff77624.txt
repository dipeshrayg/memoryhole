Jon Henricks, a record-breaking Australian swimmer who won two gold medals at the 1956 Melbourne Olympics, has died aged 91.
Henricks, who first came to prominence in distance events, will be remembered as one of Australia's greatest sprint swimmers.
At the 1956 Olympics, he won gold in the 100 metres freestyle and with Australia's 4x200 metres freestyle relay team, following three gold medals at the 1954 Commonwealth Games in Vancouver.
Henricks' death came on the 70th anniversary of the 1956 Melbourne Olympics, the Australian Olympic Committee (AOC) said.
Ian Chesterman, president of the AOC, paid tribute to the swimming star in a statement.
"Jon Henricks was a giant of the Melbourne 1956 Games and part of a remarkable generation of Australian Olympians alongside Murray Rose, John Devitt, longtime friend Dawn Fraser and many others," he said.
"In front of a home crowd, he won Australia's first swimming gold medal, then anchored the 4x200m relay team to a world record.
"That week helped establish Australia's reputation as a swimming nation, and it still inspires our athletes today.
"While he made his life in the United States, he remained a proud Australian Olympian. On behalf of the Australian Olympic family, I extend our deepest condolences to Jon's family, friends and the Australian swimming community."
Emerging as a teen sensation in the early 1950s, Sydneysider Henricks held multiple world records and, with greater luck, could have won more Olympic medals.
It was not until months after the 1952 Helsinki Games that he realised he was a sprinter, and at the 1960 Games in Rome - where he was the favourite - Henricks was struck down by a gastrointestinal bug and missed the finals.
Henricks was also involved in some of the sport's most enduring innovations.
Thanks to the proto-sports science of Sydney University physiology professor Frank Cotton, working under the supervision of Henricks's coach Harry Gallagher, Henricks was the first prominent swimmer to use "tapering off" training before major events.
At the urging of his father, Clyde, he was also reputedly the first swimmer to shave his body to reduce drag.
Henricks was named the world's (amateur) athlete of the year for 1954 by the Helms Athletic Foundation, and his victory in the 100 metres final in Melbourne put him at the centre of an iconic Australian sporting moment, with his countrymen John Devitt and Gary Chapman taking silver and bronze in a clean sweep.
"After I touched the wall, I looked for the timekeepers," Henricks told The Age in 1999.
"They were all smiling at me, and I knew. As I scanned the lanes, I could see Gary Chapman and John Devitt being congratulated. We had made history.
"Everyone knew that this was something special."
'One of the greatest swims of all time'
Born John Malcolm Henricks on June 6, 1935, 'Jon' Henricks was the son of Bessie Patersen and Malcolm 'Clyde' Henricks, a company director in Sydney.
By the age of 11, Henricks was winning Cub Scout races at the Balmain baths.
He came to wider attention during his time as a student at Fort Street Boys' High School in the Sydney suburb of Petersham.
There, Henricks dominated the field in inter-school competitions and, while still a schoolboy, became a national champion and holder of the Australian record for the 110-yard and 220-yard freestyle events.
In 1952, Henricks was a medal-winner in three freestyle events at the national championships - bronze in the 1500 yards, silver in the 800 yards and gold in the 400 yards. But an ear infection prevented his selection for the 1952 Helsinki Olympics.
Later that year, "by accident" but with the endorsement of Cotton, Henricks realised he was more suited to sprinting events and convinced Gallagher to allow him to focus solely on them.
At the New South Wales state titles in January 1953, the 17-year-old announced himself as a sensation when he shaved 1.2 seconds off the national record for the 110 yards freestyle.
That swim would have won him silver in Helsinki, and was described by race announcer Sid Grange as "one of the greatest swims of all time".
The next morning, an article in Sydney's Sunday Herald was even less subtle, dubbing Henricks "the best sprint swimmer Australia has ever produced".
Within a month, he had added the national 55-yard freestyle record to his collection, and even his school swimming carnivals received breathless media coverage.
Due to parental pressure to focus on obtaining his leaving certificate and becoming a doctor, in 1953, Henricks declined US college scholarship offers, including from Harvard.
Regardless, his subsequent selection in Australian squads for the Japanese Nationals of 1953 and the 1954 Commonwealth Games in Vancouver meant his studies were put on hold for the better part of two years.
In Japan, Henricks was disappointed with his times yet still claimed gold medals, but his increasing travel commitments had an unforgiving press claiming that he was "gadding about".
In reality, training alongside Dawn Fraser in Gallagher's squad, there was no respite from the hard work.
In Vancouver, Henricks removed any doubts about his focus with gold medals in three freestyle events - the 110 yards, the 3x110 yard relay and the 4x220 yard relay.
The press now lauded him as "the fastest swimmer the world has ever seen" and a "human torpedo".
'The look that every son yearns for'
At the Melbourne Games, Henricks's performance in his pet event, the 100 metres freestyle, fulfilled half a decade of promise and belied the pressure that came with his raging favouritism for the gold.
At just 21, Henricks was also the senior member of the 4x200 metre freestyle team that included teenagers Devitt, Kevin O'Halloran and Murray Rose.
In winning gold, they chopped 0.9 of a second off the world record, never surrendering O'Halloran's healthy lead in the first leg.
But that arrival at the summit of the sport was a bittersweet moment for Henricks.
"That [Olympic gold] had been the focus of my life up until that point," he later admitted.
"And I thought, 'Gosh darn it, what am I going to do now?' It's a feeling that comes over you that you just don't know. It's not sadness ... impending doom I guess ... my life's over.
"It really impacted me."
Indeed, Henricks's immediate reward for his Olympic feats was to complete the national service he had deferred to compete in the Games.
In 1958, he finally accepted a college offer in the US, becoming a member of the University of Southern California (USC) team alongside his countryman Murray Rose.
The team became a "machine", winning four consecutive national collegiate titles.
At USC, Henricks also met his future wife Bonnie Wilkie, sister of his teammate Mike Wilkie.
Though it would eventually pale into insignificance, Henricks's Olympic title defence of 1960 in Rome was ill-starred.
Three days before the 100 metres final, he contracted an especially bad case of what the press dubbed "Rome belly" - sensing immediately he would be weakened too badly to repeat his 1956 heroics.
Summoning himself from his sick bed, Henricks won his first heat, and at the 35-yard mark of his semi-final had raised hopes of a miracle.
But with his tearful fiancée Wilkie urging him on, Henricks faded badly to finish with the 12th-best time and miss the final.
"I was so weak I just could not do it," Henricks told the waiting press pack.
"When I saw the times go up on the board, I felt like crying for having let the team and my coach, Harry Gallagher, down so badly."
In reality, the question about the Henricks-Gallagher alliance that would linger in the following decades was whether the let-down went the other way.
By the 1970s, Gallagher's Cerutty-inspired methods of training his swimmers to exhaustion were suspected to have ruined more careers than they had advanced.
Soon after Rome, Henricks married Wilkie, and they made a life together in Chicago, then Santa Barbara, California. Until 2018, they also maintained a home in the Sydney suburb of Mosman.
Inducted into the International Swimming Hall of Fame in 1973 and the Sports Australia Hall of Fame in 1986, Henricks was awarded the Australian Sports Medal in 2000.
In the 2018 Australia Day honours list, he was awarded the Medal of the Order of Australia (OAM) for service to swimming at the elite level and his work as a mentor.
Three decades after his 100 metres triumph in Melbourne, the aftermath of his most famous race remained clear in Henricks's mind.
"I searched the crowd frantically for my parents," Henricks recalled.
"In the pandemonium that was going on, there was no way I could pick them out. It would have to wait till much later for me to see that look of pride in my father's face. The look that every son yearns for.
"Truly no words are ever necessary. A look that says it all."
