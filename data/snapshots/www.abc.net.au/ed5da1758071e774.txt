New data suggests Australia's rental affordability squeeze is deepening, spreading geographically and starting to affect people on higher incomes.
Rental affordability has worsened or remained at crisis levels across most of Australia, in both cities and the regions, according to new data analysis.
However, average affordability varies a lot depending on where you live - and one capital city is bucking the trend.
"What we've seen over the last year is overall for capital cities the deterioration in rental affordability has slowed down," SGS Economics principal Ellen Witte said.
"But for the overall picture, we still see a few cities standing out where rental affordability continues to get worse and worse."
Here's a chart of rental affordability for every capital city, except Darwin, since 2013.
It uses a metric created by SGS Economics and Planning, calculated from median rental bonds data and household income.
To start with, we've highlighted the ACT and Perth, the highest and lowest on the scale.
But you might be wondering: what exactly do these numbers mean?
To put it simply, if the line is under 100, rentals are considered "unaffordable" for households earning an average income.
This means that typical rents take up more than 30 per cent of their income.
The chart shows Canberra and surrounds is the most affordable capital city rental market in Australia, while Perth is the least affordable.
Canberra has a few things going for it.
Average incomes are higher than other cities, which contributes to better affordability. More housing supply and policies to stop big rental rises have also helped, Ms Witte said.
"ACT is really bucking the trend. The overall income levels are very strong for average income households in the ACT and there's a strong public sector.
"But we also see that ACT is the only state that put in guardrails around rent increases."
Even so, this result hides how hard it is for lower-income households, like students and minimum-wage couples, to rent in Canberra.
Perth rentals are at the other end of the spectrum.
Affordability improved in the 2010s as Western Australia's mining boom wound down, which meant there were more empty rentals available. But rents have soared since the pandemic, and affordability has tanked.
That means the average household is paying more than 30 per cent of its income on rent.
"When there's growth, incomes pick up. But they haven't increased as much as rent, especially not from the lower-income households," Ms Witte said.
Other capital cities sit somewhere in the middle.
Melbourne is the next most affordable behind the ACT.
"There's been quite a few policy changes in the housing markets in Victoria and Greater Melbourne and the economy has been developing weaker, so that is an impact as well," Ms Witte said.
"There's also been quite a push to build more housing."
Brisbane and Adelaide have followed matching trajectories, both becoming less affordable since the COVID pandemic.
Hobart has had the steepest year-on-year fall. Households there are now, on average, paying more than 30 per cent of income on rent.
This is because more people are coming into Hobart than moving to the mainland Ms Witte said.
"It's really that change in population growth together with, of course, a housing market where material costs are very high," she said. "So supply hasn't really been responding as well."
Sydney also has low affordability.
But as mentioned before, these lines are based on averages and hide a lot of the detail.
Most households are not 'average'. So how does affordability look for different groups of people?
To find out, let's take a look at affordability in Sydney from a slightly different angle.
This is the exact same data, but we have tweaked the scale of the chart a little.
And now we can compare the Sydney average to a few different types of households...
A two-income couple with children fares the best, despite affordability declining in recent years.
On average, these couples spend 18 per cent of their income on rent, which is "affordable" according to the index.
But it looks a lot more grim for some other households.
A household where the only earner is a single parent is below average.
And rent for a single-income couple with children sinks further into unaffordability.
Next are couples where both partners earn the minimum wage, student share houses, hospitality workers and childcare workers.
We're getting into severe unaffordability levels here.
Nearly half of a childcare worker's wage could go to rent, according to the analysis.
"In the past, rental affordability was really an issue for lower-income households, people who may have no jobs or part-time jobs," Ms Witte said.
"But increasingly what we see is that it's people with full-time jobs."
Things are even less affordable for renting pensioners and a single part-time working parent on government benefits.
Median rents in Sydney take up 84 per cent of a single pensioner's income.
And a single person on benefits in Sydney ranks the lowest, where typical rent would cost more than their entire income.
Jackson Hills, who runs the organisation that commissioned the research, National Shelter, said it was concerning that rental affordability was gradually impacting more and more groups of people.
"What we're seeing now is that where rental affordability was a challenge for the bottom two income quintiles pretty much across the country, it's now creeping well and truly into higher income groups as well and it's not just metropolitan locations," he said.
"I think there's some new categories emerging like childcare workers for example, and other low to moderate income earners where a single worker may have historically been able to find a modest rental close to where they work and are now weighing up if they can afford to live further away to balance their weekly budget."
"We still would have the most concern for people on the lowest incomes that are just outside of social housing eligibility because that means they will need to find a property in the private market where they are unlikely to find something suitable and the risk of becoming homeless is quite high," Mr Hills added.
As affordability in capital cities has deteriorated over time, more people are moving into regional housing markets, which in turn creates a squeeze there.
The data shows affordability in regional parts of Australia has declined, particularly in South Australia and Tasmania.
Regional Queensland is the least affordable, with rents consuming more than 32 per cent of the average renting household's income.
Alistair Sisson, a research fellow at the UNSW City Futures Research Centre, says multiple solutions should be considered, including capping rent increases, increasing income support payments and investing in public housing.
"Australia has comparatively very, very low levels of public housing compared to similar kinds of advanced economies," he said.
"There's been a little bit of movement on that in recent years, but not enough really to make a change in that total proportion."
Mr Hills said there were no overnight fixes.
"It's going to take a sustained investment really over the next 20 years to get there."
About the data
- The SGS Economics Rental Affordability Index is based on a widely used standard where a low-income household is in "rental stress" if payments cost at least 30 per cent of gross income, known as the 30/40 rule. The index considers households at all income levels, not just those within the lowest 40th percentile.
- It's calculated using localised household income estimates from ABS census data and average weekly earnings, and the median rental price of dwellings where bonds were lodged.
- The analysis uses rental bonds data, which means it does not track changes in existing rental agreements.
- The index for household profiles is calculated using estimated income and rentals with a suitable number of bedrooms.
- The Northern Territory is not included as rental bond data is not available.
- More information on the methodology can be found at the SGS Economics and Planning website.
