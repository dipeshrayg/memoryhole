In short:
Grass-fed cattle producers have voted 54 per cent in favour of raising the cattle transaction levy from $5 per animal sold to $6.
It's expected to deliver about $30 million in extra funds to Meat and Livestock Australia for marketing and research.
What's next?
The new levy is expected to start in July 2028.
Cattle producers have narrowly voted to pay more for marketing and research, but the peak industry body says the close result is a "wake-up call" on transparency.
Cattle Australia's recommendation that the fee graziers pay should rise from $5 to $6 per grass-fed animal sold was approved, with 54 per cent in favour.
Chair Garry Edwards said while it was a positive result, it highlighted a lack of understanding about how the money was spent.
"Some levy payers present were saying ... I voted 'no' a couple of weeks ago but now I understand the levy more, I wish I could change my vote,"
he said.
"It's a really interesting response about the communication that's required ... in trying to explain the levy."
"This is not about just giving more money to Meat and Livestock Australia (MLA); it's about investing in our future and really redirecting where that funding is going."
The increase is expected to deliver about $30 million in extra funds to MLA, which invests more than $370 million in research, development and marketing on behalf of cattle, sheep, and goat producers.
Although the increase is unlikely to directly affect retail beef prices, Mr Edwards said consumers were the ultimate beneficiaries of the work funded through the levy.
"This is a wake-up call that we need to take greater steps in making sure that we are communicating our story to those consumers,"
he said.
"They don't necessarily understand what happens on our farms, our cattle stations and within our businesses.
"Our job is to tell those stories and much of today was about embracing that, and we look forward to the challenge."
MLA managing director Michael Crowley said it is the first time the levy has increased in 20 years, with the additional funds to be invested in programs to reduce costs and increase productivity.
"The operating environment continues to change a lot,"
he said.
"We're looking at on-farm objective measurement technologies, data systems that get feedback going back to not just the last owner but to the breeder.
"It's a really important job for us ... to be really clear on the benefits to levy payers and the value that they get from the investments that we make on their behalf."
Cattle Australia chief executive Will Evans said the board would now work towards implementing the reforms ahead of an anticipated introduction of the new levy in July 2028.
"I think we always knew coming into this, no matter what the outcome was, this is a starting point," he said.
"We're confident in our analysis, we're confident in the vision that we've put down for the industry.
"It's about proving that the people who've backed us today were correct in their assessment, and the people who still have questions have more opportunities to have conversations with us.
Cattle producer Don Heatley from the Burdekin region in north Queensland was MLA chair when the levy last changed in 2006.
He said support was stronger then.
"Fifty-four to 46 is not an overly decisive outcome," he said.
"The reality was it was a different set of circumstances [in 2006]."
He said the last rise set a clear priority to fund marketing to overseas markets and as a result, global demand for Australian beef rose by more than 60 per cent.
"There was extra money required because we're in a very competitive world," he said.
"I think the dilemma is going to be figuring out the break-up of the dollar to the components needing to receive extra funding."
