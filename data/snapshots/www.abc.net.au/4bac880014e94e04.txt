In short:
At least 68 people have died in a crash between two trains in central Spain, officials say.
The high-speed trains collided in the Toledo region on Wednesday morning.
Australia's Department of Foreign Affairs says it is not aware of any Australians affected by the collision at this stage.
At least 68 people are dead, and dozens more are injured after a crash involving two high-speed trains in central Spain.
Local officials say the crash was caused by a failure of rail security systems.
The regional government of Castilla La Mancha confirmed the death toll and said another 58 people were receiving medical treatment at hospitals.
Rescue workers were searching the wreckage for survivors and bodies on Wednesday, local time.
Spain's rail system operator, ADIF, said the crash occurred in the central province of Toledo and involved two high-speed trains carrying hundreds of passengers, heading south from Madrid to Seville.
A train operated by the state-owned RENFE company struck the rear of another train operated by private company IRYO that was stopped.
The collision derailed two carriages on the tail of the Iryo train and the front of the RENFE train.
The collision is the deadliest rail disaster in Europe since a 2013 crash in Spain's Santiago de Compostela left 80 people dead.
It also came only nine months after a deadly crash in Spain's south involving high-speed trains operated by the same two companies.
"Today all of Spain is grieving," Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez said from the crash site.
Later, protesters, including far-right groups, gathered outside Spain's Ministry of Transportation in Madrid to demand the government's resignation.
Hundreds took to the streets to call for accountability.
Some demonstrators clashed with police, throwing bottles and flares.
Officers responded by using tear gas.
Australian Education Minister Jason Clare said the Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade (DFAT) was not aware of any Australians who had been affected by the crash.
"As you'd expect, we've got consular officials in Spain who stand ready to help any Australians or any Australian families if they are affected," he told the ABC.
"I think the whole country is in shock at the moment, seeing these images on our screens. [They're] just horrific."
In a statement, DFAT offered its condolences to victims' families and other people affected by the collision.
Mr Sánchez said his government would declare three days of national mourning and called for increased safety measures to restore confidence in the high-speed rail system.
"It is unacceptable for a country like Spain to suffer two high-speed rail accidents in the same year," he said.
The crash, which occurred on a flat stretch surrounded by olive fields between the towns of Urda and Los Yébenes, shredded carriages into bits of metal and other material. Debris was scattered down a gravel slope.
Samy Paul, a British tourist, described the shock of the impact to The Associated Press after being rescued from the RENFE train.
"Suddenly, everything started falling apart. Bang! We fell down," he said.
"We realised carriage two, carriage one, all gone, all smashed. There was blood all over."
David Gómez, a construction worker, was working nearby when he heard the crash and ran to help, he told the newspaper El País.
"People were trying to get out through the windows ... We cut through some fences to access the area and treated people with the first-aid kits we had brought until emergency services arrived," he said.
"As I was trying to pull a young woman out, there was a piece of a leg."
Passenger Ignacio Relaño was also travelling on the RENFE train. He told state broadcaster TVE that the train stopped momentarily on the line, then started again and crashed into the Iryo train.
"We all felt the train abruptly slowing down and then the impact," he said.
"The car leaned to one side like it was about to tip over. I hit my nose, everything went flying, luggage, cell phones. Everything shook."
Authorities said 397 passengers were in the RENFE train and 147 passengers in the Iryo train.
Ambulances and medical helicopters made trips to and from the site, while survivors who were unharmed were put on buses. A military emergency brigade was deployed to help.
Spain's King Felipe VI also visited the crash site.
"We share in the grief of the families and loved ones of those who lost their lives, and we send our heartfelt support to the injured and all those affected," Spain's royals said in a statement.
'Inexplicable' command centre order before crash
Spanish Transport Minister Óscar Puente said the crash was caused by what he called an "inexplicable" order from a central command centre for Spain's trains after a security system failed.
He said the train engineers were not to blame.
Mr Puente said that at 8:30am, local time, the security system on a 63-kilometre stretch stopped working because of a computer malfunction, causing all trains on that stretch to stop.
Central command told the RENFE train to start running again without any speed restrictions, and it collided with the stopped IRYO train at 8:53am.
Mr Puente said the train should never have been told to start moving again because a "stop signal" was still active on that line.
If it had resumed moving, he said, it should at least have been told to go at reduced speed.
Authorities were still reviewing information to determine how fast the train was going, but Mr Puente said it could have been travelling at 200km/h on that stretch of track.
"I am not attributing responsibility to anyone, I am just stating the facts," he said.
Earlier, Spanish Health Minister Mónica García told TVE that emergency workers were focusing on rescuing survivors and bodies from the two most damaged cars, and that injured passengers were being sent to different hospitals in the area.
"It was a high-speed impact, so we are expecting this to be a tragedy," she said.
