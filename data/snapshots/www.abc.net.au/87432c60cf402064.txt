In short:
An inquest is looking into the neonatal deaths of four babies born by freebirth or homebirth in NSW.
Deputy State Coroner Joan Baptie will hear evidence about the advice and treatment given to the babies' mothers before, during and after the births.
What's next?
The inquiry will run for two weeks.
The mother of a baby who died in northern New South Wales four years ago was afraid she would be forced to have a caesarean if she went to hospital, an inquest has heard.
What drove her decision to continue with plans for a freebirth, despite medical advice that her baby was in distress, will be examined by the New South Wales Coroners Court in Lismore.
The inquest will investigate the neonatal deaths of two other babies born by freebirth in northern New South Wales, and a fourth born by homebirth in the Hunter Valley, between 2022 and 2024.
A freebirth means a baby was born without medical assistance, often supported by family, friends, a doula or "birth keeper".
Homebirths were deliveries supported by a registered midwife outside a clinical setting.
During the two-week inquest, Deputy State Coroner Joan Baptie will hear evidence about the advice and treatment given to the babies' mothers, before, during, and after the births, and how it influenced their birthing decisions.
The counsel assisting the coroner said the inquiry did not seek to place blame on the parents for their decisions, but rather sought to understand the barriers preventing parents engaging with health services.
On the first day of the inquest, the court heard the mother of Baby R, who cannot be named for legal reasons, was about three weeks past her due date when she had an ultrasound that revealed the baby was in distress.
Despite advice from the sonographer to go to Tweed Valley Hospital immediately, the court heard the woman still wanted a homebirth.
Counsel Assisting the Coroner, Tracey Stevens, said she consulted her birth keeper, Sita Kali, for advice, despite knowing she had no medical training.
Ms Stevens said the birth keeper noted the woman's anxiety, and advised her to go home, rest and calm down before making a decision.
Within hours, the woman went into labour. The baby was born with the cord around its neck, was pale, had faeces on its face, and had breathing difficulties.
Twelve hours later, emergency services were called, but the baby died.
In a statement after the baby died, the mother said: "I was afraid to go to hospital and be forced into a caesarean. I still thought the baby was ok."
Expert midwives giving evidence at the inquest said this mistrust of clinicians was driving some women to choose freebirths outside the medical system.
Professor Hannah Dahlen and Dr Alison Teate said while doulas and birth keepers did not have the same clinical expertise as midwives, they played an important role in supporting childbirth.
Professor Dahlen supported women being able to make an informed choice about how their babies were born.
She said that included letting doulas and birth keepers be involved in the birth process.
"I'd like to see us work together rather than this great divide," Professor Dahlen said.
She said trust was key to the safe delivery of babies.
