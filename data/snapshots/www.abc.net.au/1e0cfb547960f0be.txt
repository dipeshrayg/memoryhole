In short:
An investigative computer analyst from Queensland Police has given evidence at the trial of Robert Crawford.
Prosecutors have alleged Mr Crawford's phone was unlocked multiple times during hours when he initially told police he was asleep.
What's next?
The prosecution has now closed its case and the defence will call expert witnesses in when the trial continues next week.
A former Queensland RAAF pilot accused of killing his wife, allegedly "interacted" with his phone during the time he told investigators he was sleeping and she accidentally died, a jury has heard.
Robert Crawford is on trial in Brisbane charged over the murder of 49-year-old Frances Crawford in Upper Lockyer in July 2024.
The 49-year-old has pleaded not guilty, and maintains his wife died in a tragic accident.
A Supreme Court jury has already heard Mr Crawford called Triple Zero (000) in the early hours to report he found Ms Crawford trapped underneath their ride on lawnmower at the bottom of a retaining wall.
He told officers he had been asleep at the time when he believed his wife was fatally injured trying to move the machine away from sprinklers, the jury has heard.
On the final day of prosecution evidence, an investigative computer analyst from the Queensland Police Service took the witness stand.
Sean Dalton detailed to the jury how he analysed data extracted from both phones and explained the tests he carried out.
For Mr Crawford's phone, he told the jury he was asked to analyse usage between 1:09am to 3:20am.
"I developed a hypothesis being that the mobile phone was manually interacted with during the time periods," he said.
The jury heard according to Mr Dalton's analysis, Mr Crawford's phone was unlocked at 1:09am, 1:50am, twice at 2:35am and finally at 3:20am.
His analysis found all five times the phone was unlocked, the Signal messenger application was open, the jury heard.
The jury heard the independent testing could not conclude what was being done within the application.
However the jury heard the analysis did show Mr Crawford's phone keyboard was last accessed at 12:35am.
Mr Dalton said there were limitations to his findings.
"My testing is incredibly clinical, very structured," he said.
The jury heard the analysis of Ms Crawford's phone showed an exchange of messages between her and one of her sons from 6:31pm until 7:44pm.
At 9:23pm, a message was received on Ms Crawford's phone from her other son, whose conversation had been previously muted, the jury heard.
The analysis the jury heard showed that message was not opened until the following morning.
At 11:20pm, the jury heard Ms Crawford's phone was unlocked, and one minute later a signal message was sent to Mr Crawford's phone.
The jury has already heard that message said "hey are you going to put mower away soon?".
At 11:22pm, the jury heard Mr Crawford made a call to a work colleague which ended at 11:25pm.
Between 11:24 and 11:26pm, the jury has already heard Ms Crawford's phone received three messages from Mr Crawford's phone.
In them he explained he was dealing with a work issue, asked her to "wait a sec", and suggested she turn the sprinklers off, the jury heard.
At 11:26pm, the jury heard the analysis showed Ms Crawford's phone was locked, and was not accessed again until 4:45am, when the screen's back light was activated.
The jury has already heard the final messages from Mr Crawford were sent to his wife's phone about an hour later, in which he asked her to come to bed, and the analysis showed they were not opened until the following morning.
Prosecutors allege the messages sent and received after 11:21pm between both phones were authored by Mr Crawford, in a bid to set the scene that his wife died in an accident.
Mr Crawford has denied using his phone after sending his last message at 12:35pm.
The prosecution closed has now closed its case, and the defence has opened its.
They are expected to call three expert witnesses when the trial continues next week.
