What are 'overhang' seats?
We've mentioned "overhang" seats quite a lot this evening.
They are the result of a political party winning more electorates than the seats they would be eligible for according to their general party vote, and are added to the total number of seats in parliament.
For example, if there were five overhang seats, the total number in parliament would be increased to 125 seats.
They are a quirk of the New Zealand political system that some parties have been seeking to take advantage of this election through the controversial strategy of encouraging supporters to "split" their vote.
It could see a bloc get more seats than it would be eligible for according to their party vote.
Both major party leaders criticised the strategy during the campaign, with National's Christopher Luxon saying it could confuse people and lead to a Labour win, while Labour's Chris Hipkins said he didn't agree with "people trying to manipulate the electoral system".
Left-bloc member Te Pāti Māori told supporters to give them their electorate vote in the Māori seats but give their party vote to other left-wing parties.
Meanwhile, the two right minor parties ACT and NZ First on the right, approved messaging telling supporters voting for National in their electorates not to give their bloc ally their party vote. National publicly opposed the strategy.
Some estimates indicated the push could see National get more than any party has had before.
And with National's party vote falling substantially while maintaining about the same number of electorate seats tonight, it looks like they are going to get quite a few overhangs.

Who are the potential 'kingmakers' this election?
If it turns out neither National nor Labour wins enough seats to form government by themselves, as polls have indicated, then it will be smaller parties that emerge as the potential "kingmakers", giving them the numbers in parliament.
NZ First leader Winston Peters has played such a role before, when he made a coalition with Jacinda Ardern's Labour, putting them both in government in 2017. More recently, NZ First has been in coalition with the National-led government.
This year, it could fall to the Opportunity party and its leader, Qiulae Wong, to decide the next government. If Opportunity wins seats, making good on some recent polls, Wong may hold the keys to the top office for National's Christopher Luxon and Labour's Chris Hipkins.
While Luxon has said he won't do a deal with Opportunity to secure power, few political observers take that promise seriously.

How do NZ's Māori electorates work?
Overlaying New Zealand's map are seven Māori electorates.
The Māori electorates have been part of New Zealand's electoral system since 1867 and are meant to ensure representation for the country's indigenous people in parliament.
Māori New Zealanders can choose whether they want to vote in the general electorate where they live or the Māori electorate.
Te Pati Māori won six of the seven at the 2023 election, but ended up with having four following internal ructions during the term.
This time around, they have announced a controversial "split voting" strategy.

Chris v Chris
As the Washington Post put it in a headline this week, "A man named Chris will be New Zealand's next prime minister, whichever party wins the election".
Yes, the leaders of the two largest parties share a name and the election has been dubbed by some "the battle of the Chrises".
Maybe it is not surprising. "Christopher" was one of New Zealand's most popular boy names in the years both National's Christopher Luxon (1970) and Labour's Chris Hipkins (1978) were born, according to the NZ government's Smart Start website.
They have also each served as prime minister of New Zealand.
But there, the similarities seemingly end. Luxon, New Zealand's prime minister, is a former Air New Zealand chief executive. He was elected to parliament in 2020 and became National Party leader the year after. Luxon became prime minister in 2023, after forming coalition agreements with NZ First and ACT NZ following the election that year.
Hipkins is Labour leader and succeeded Jacinda Ardern as prime minister in 2023. He led the government for about 10 months before it lost government at the election that year, but he has stayed on as party leader and is hoping to return to the top office.
And despite the headlines, polls indicate that smaller parties will once again play a major role in this election, not just the "Chrises".
What is the Opportunity party and why could it be a 'kingmaker'?
Opportunity, formerly the Opportunities Party, is a self-described centrist party that has been around for about a decade but has never won a seat.
However, under new leader Qiulae Wong, they've been tipped as possible "kingmakers".
Pitched as a pragmatic, non-ideological political force, the party's headline policy is a new land tax, with the proceeds going towards a $NZ370 ($297) per week universal basic income.
The party also wants New Zealand to cut emissions more sharply and step up its transition to a low-carbon economy.
The polls are indicating a tight contest.
The latest Roy Morgan poll suggests Opportunity could get 8 per cent of the party vote, although a recent 1News-Verian survey has estimated its vote may be closer to 6 per cent.
If Opportunity gets over the 5 per cent wall and wins seven or even 10 seats, they could have the power to decide who gets to form government.
Labour leader Chris Hipkins has said he's open to working with Opportunity (though would not support the new tax), while National leader Christopher Luxon has ruled it out.
You can read more about the party and Wong in this profile by foreign affairs reporter Stephen Dziedzic.

Who's who in NZ's parliament?
For those not intimately familiar with New Zealand's political scene, here is a quick introduction.
The country has a single-chamber parliament, so its national vote is called a general election, not a federal election.
The National Party, led by Prime Minister Christopher Luxon, are New Zealand's major centre-right party and is seeking a second term in government.
They are the incumbents in coalition with minor parties ACT New Zealand (economically focused libertarians) and New Zealand First (more socially conservative and led by the outspoken Winston Peters).
The Labour Party (spelled with a "u" in New Zealand) share a similar space on the political spectrum to their namesake in Australia. They are led by Chris Hipkins, who was briefly prime minister after former leader Jacinda Ardern resigned and before leading the party to defeat in 2023.
They make up an opposition bloc with Te Pāti Māori (the left-wing Māori Party) and the Green Party (a left-leaning environmental party).
There are about 10 other minor parties contesting the election; however, the main one you need to know about is the Opportunity party. More on them later.
Why we're only just starting our coverage now
You might be wondering why we've only just started our election day coverage now.
Under New Zealand's electoral rules, the media is not allowed to publish stories or any form of advertising that is likely to influence voters from midnight on the day before until after voting closes.
As many Kiwi expats were voting in Australia, the ABC has made the decision to honour those rules.
That's why we've just started the blog after voting closed at 7pm (NZ time), which is 5pm AEDT.

How does NZ's electoral system work?
New Zealand's political system uses Mixed Member Proportional (MMP) representation.
It's a bit confusing to outsiders, so bear with us.
When New Zealanders go into the voting booth, they cast two votes: one vote for a specific candidate to represent their electorate and one party vote.
The party vote determines what proportion of the nominally 120 seats in the parliament each party gets.
Those seats are then allocated to each party first with the electorate seats won and then topped up with "list" seats according to each party's ranked list.
A party can also get list seats without winning any electorates, but they must get a minimum threshold of 5 per cent of the party vote.
If a party gets more electorate seats than their share of the party vote would indicate, those seats are added to the total seats in Parliament and called "overhang" seats.

The results to keep an eye on tonight
Wondering which results will be critical to the overall outcome this evening?
Experts say a few could indicate which side of politics will emerge strongest.
One is turnout. A higher turnout is likely to favour left-wing parties like Labour and the Green party, said Tom Jamieson, a senior lecturer in political science at Victoria University of Wellington.
Second, experts will watch to see whether the Opportunity party either wins an electorate or receives more than 5 per cent of the vote and enter parliament.
"Polls suggest a left-leaning coalition is likely dependent on them making it to parliament (and joining that coalition), so if they do not, it is much more likely the incumbent government will be re-elected," Dr Jamieson said.
Next is the number of overhang seats - which are additional seats created when a party gets more electorate seats than its share of the party vote would indicate (we'll cover more on this in a post soon).
"If one side wins considerably more overhang seats than the other, that could dictate who can form a government afterwards," Dr Jamieson said.
Finally, Massey University professor of politics Richard Shaw says he'll be watching the Prime Minister Christopher Luxon's seat of Botany.
"He holds it with a huge majority, but if that margin falls appreciably it could be an indicator that National are in trouble in the electorates.
"If so, the chances of them creating an overhang may fall - and with it, the likelihood of a second term for the coalition."

A different election day vibe
Observing Kiwis voting today in Auckland was a rather surreal experience - at least for this Australian journalist.
New Zealand does elections slightly differently from Australia. There are strict election "blackout" rules that restrict all forms of advertising, commentary and campaigning.
The blackout even applies to reporting, with media banned from broadcasting anything that might influence voters.
So when you visit a voting booth on election day, it is a bit different to the (occasionally rowdy) festival of democracy you get in Australia.
Nobody is badgering voters with how to vote cards, and there are no party banners, signs or corflutes.
At the voting place the ABC visited in Auckland's inner suburbs this morning, there was one very familiar thing on hand, however - a sausage sandwich.

The counting begins
Voting has closed in New Zealand's election and we will soon see results trickling in.
In an election campaign overshadowed by cost of living pressures and an economy buffeted by international shocks, the National Party has been making its case to stay in government and Labour has tried to prise back votes it needs to regain office.
But experts say both parties ran negative campaigns.
"I'm not sure anyone has much enjoyed it," Richard Shaw, a professor of politics at Massey University, told the ABC.
"The policy contest which marked the opening stanzas has largely fallen away into an acrimonious exchange between the left and the right about how to game the electoral system through strategic voting."
National and Labour focused on criticising not just their rival parties but also the people involved, with Labour trying to seize on the unpopularity of Prime Minister Christopher Luxon and National seeking to exploit similar sentiment about Labour's Chris Hipkins, said Tom Jamieson, a senior lecturer in political science at Victoria University of Wellington.
"For a party leading the incumbent government, National ran less on their record than you might have expected on their record and more about what their opponents were likely to do in power."
On the other hand, smaller parties including New Zealand First, ACT, the Green Party, Te Pāti Māori and the Opportunity party focused their efforts on policy initiatives and their vision for the country, Dr Jamieson said.
"In an electorate with a plurality that has consistently indicated they are on the wrong track, it could be that these parties benefit from dissatisfaction with the direction of the government," he said.
Polls have indicated it could be a tight election.
Results released tonight will indicate the election outcome and are expected to arrive over several hours. The official count begins tomorrow, and the Electoral Commission is aiming to release the official results by November 27.
Professor Shaw expected it would be a long three weeks and said special votes - which take longer to count - may be consequential.
"We may have a clear result tonight, but that seems unlikely," he said.
