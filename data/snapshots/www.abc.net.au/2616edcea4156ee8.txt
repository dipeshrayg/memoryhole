In short:
China's top Communist Party leader in Shanghai is under investigation for "serious violations".
Chen Jining was widely tipped to take on a top leadership position within the party.
What's next?
The latest probe is a sign Xi Jinping's anti-corruption drive is expanding, with analysts warning "no one is safe".
China's top Communist Party leader in Shanghai is the latest official to come under investigation for "serious violations of discipline and law", a term commonly used to refer to corruption.
The Chinese government announced via state media on Tuesday that Chen Jining was subject to a probe by the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection but gave no further details.
The investigation into Mr Chen, who was widely tipped to take on a top leadership position, comes less than a month after the ruling party promised tighter discipline and loyalty.
It is a sign that President Xi Jinping's sweeping 14-year anti-graft campaign is still expanding after already punishing at least 7.9 million CCP members and officials, purging dozens of top officials and overseeing a clear-out of the military's top brass in recent months.
The 73-year-old Chinese leader is preparing for a key party congress next year where he will oversee a leadership reshuffle and almost certainly secure a fourth term as party leader.
Other officials who have been the subject of similar investigations have gone on to face prosecution, conviction and imprisonment, suggesting Mr Chen's political career is effectively over.
Who is Chen Jining?
Mr Chen is the fourth member of the Politburo, an elite Communist Party decision-making body of currently 21 members, to face investigation this term.
Mr Chen has been the party secretary in Shanghai, one of China's most prosperous cities and a commercial and financial hub, since 2022.
The role has traditionally been viewed as a stepping stone to China's top leadership. The country's current premier, Li Qiang, previously held the position and Mr Chen was tipped as a candidate for eventual elevation to the party's top leadership.
In the role, he met with many world leaders, including former UK prime minister Sir Keir Starmer during a bilateral meeting earlier this year and US officials including Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer and former US secretary of state Antony Blinken.
Mr Chen also hosted a delegation from the Swiss pharmaceutical company Roche in Shanghai last week, according to local media.
Before moving to Shanghai, Mr Chen served as mayor of Beijing for five years.
He also had stints as the environmental protection minister and president of China's elite Tsinghua University, where he studied environmental engineering as a student in the 1980s.
'Tremble and obey' is the message for officials, analyst says
Director of SOAS China Institute in London, Steve Tsang, told the ABC the message to Chinese officials following this latest purge was, "tremble and obey".
"The reality is that nearly practically everybody of that rank can potentially be taken down for corruption," he told the ABC.
"It is really not about a corruption, it is really more a matter about who happened to have got Xi Jinping's attention and who might be seen as potentially doing something or saying something that might have made Xi Jinping feel uncomfortable.
"The anti-corruption drive that Xi Jinping has is effectively the trading name for a party ratification operation."
Professor Tsang said he could not work out why Mr Chen had got "in trouble" but was not surprised that someone of his rank had been targeted.
He said it was possible Mr Chen was simply more focused on his job of improving Shanghai's economy rather than Mr Xi's priorities and supporting him to extend his term in office.
Wen-Ti Sung of the Australian National University also said Mr Chen's removal proved that "no one is safe in today's Chinese politics".
"Xi said at the fifth plenum that anti-corruption is 'always on the road'. Chen just became exhibit A," he told AFP.
Other Politburo members purged include General Zhang Youxia and He Weidong, both senior members of the Central Military Commission, which oversees the armed forces, and Ma Zingrui, a provincial party secretary.
