Jim Chalmers announces plan to let super funds place riskier investments in housing, energy, startups
In short:
Super funds will be allowed to invest 5 per cent of their portfolio in longer-term assets such as housing projects and venture capital, under a new proposal.
Funds would remain obligated to prioritise their members' best interests and will not be required to use the new option, or be directed towards any particular asset class.
It is unclear how many funds will use the new category.
Super funds will be able to take riskier, longer-term bets with their members' money, in an overhaul of fund rules that Treasurer Jim Chalmers hopes will promote investment in housing, energy and startups.
Under changes to the performance test, a benchmarking exercise introduced by former treasurer Josh Frydenberg, Mr Chalmers will propose to let super funds invest 5 per cent in so-called "emerging assets" with a lower minimum return.
The intention is to allow funds to choose investments which may have low or uncertain returns today but higher returns tomorrow, such as early-stage housing developments or renewable projects or venture capital funds.
Funds will not be required to invest in housing, energy or any sector, or to use the new option at all, and their obligation to invest in members' best interest would not change.

But the move hands a win to some industry super funds who have argued the current benchmarks, measured relative to average stock market returns year by year, have prevented them from making long-term investments with irregular return patterns.
It also fulfils a longstanding ambition of Mr Chalmers to leverage the vast pool of funds tied up in super - nearly $5 trillion - to support national priorities, first articulated in a 2022 speech on whether "workers' capital" could help "to solve our biggest challenges".
New class of investments would not need to be Australian
The Coalition and One Nation have positioned against any such move, described by Shadow Assistant Treasurer Kevin Hogan on Tuesday in advance of this announcement as "treating your super like a piggy bank for its pet projects".
But not only does the measure not direct super funds towards any particular sector, the proposal will not require the "emerging assets" to be Australian assets.
That would have required a specific rule, since super funds face no general requirement to invest in Australia or any particular jurisdiction.
While the move was welcomed by the Association of Superannuation Funds of Australia (ASFA) in a statement, and some super funds had wanted there to be no cap on their use of emerging assets, it is also unclear how many funds will use the new category.
In a submission to Treasury's consultation process, industry fund Australian Super said it did not believe the current super performance test was "constraining [its] investment in the types of assets canvassed", such as housing, energy or venture capital.
One area where possible investment has been envisaged is in investor-owned housing projects (such as so-called "build-to-rent" developments), which are common in the US but less so in Australia, and which the government has tried to encourage.
Venture capital funds are prime candidates because they often group together several startups, some of which will fail and others of which may have large pay-offs after a few years.
Those investments would risk failing the existing benchmarks.
Rather than the average returns of a benchmark portfolio, the new benchmark for emerging assets would be equal to inflation plus some additional return premium, which would be set under advice from the independent Australian Government Actuary.
Treasury says performance test has improved outcomes
The performance test was introduced after a Productivity Commission inquiry found some super products were failing their members, who were often too disengaged to shop around for a better fund.
It compares a fund's returns over a 10-year window with a comparable portfolio, such as the S&P/ASX300 for Australian equity investments.
The test also assesses whether the product fees are comparable to other similar super products.
In a consultation paper published earlier this year, Treasury noted that the number of failures had declined significantly since the test was introduced, with just 8,500 people in underperforming products in 2025 compared to around 1 million in 2021.
"Overall, the results suggest the test has been effective in reducing persistent underperformance across the system and strengthening trustee behaviour... [It] has removed weaker performers and encouraged consultation," the paper stated.
But Treasury noted some stakeholders, including some large industry funds, had questioned whether the test was still "fit for purpose" and in particular whether it was encouraging funds to "hug" benchmarks rather than pursue high returns.
Mr Chalmers said "a broad consensus" had emerged through consultation around "removing unintended barriers to investment opportunities that have strong long-term potential, but risk-return characteristics that differ from traditional benchmarks".
"Your expectation and our expectation is that our reforms will make a positive, tangible difference to retirement balances. They have the potential to unlock up to $90 billion of investment in emerging assets," he will tell an industry audience on Wednesday.
In a secondary announcement, Mr Chalmers will move to expand the test to cover more products, including those managed by third parties.
ASFA chief executive Mary Delahunty welcomed the shake-up and said it would modernise the performance test.
"We think that these kind of changes will mean that whilst funds will still look for the best possible return, more opportunities will be open to them to do so."
Conexus Institute executive director David Bell said he was sceptical the changes would lead to an uptick in investment in emerging assets.
"Regardless of the performance test, every super fund operates under the best financial interest duty," he said.
"So, you have to invest in the best financial interests of your members.
"If sustainability deals or nation-building type investment opportunities are yielding or expected to return a number like X per cent, but there's other opportunities around which are expected to return X-plus-2 per cent, or X-plus-3 per cent, then the trustees are broadly obligated, all else equal, to invest in those other opportunities."
