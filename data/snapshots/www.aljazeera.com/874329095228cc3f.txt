As China and North Korea cut the ribbon on New Yalu River Bridge this week, officials described the new border crossing in momentous terms.
Chinese Premier Li Qiang said the 3km (1.9-mile) bridge, which links China's Dandong and North Korea's Sinuiju, would "vigorously promote" trade and "strengthen emotional bonds".
North Korean Premier Pak Thae-song expressed his belief that the crossing would "accelerate the advance for the prosperity of the two countries" and "add fresh vigour" to their economic cooperation.
Analysts on China-North Korea ties are much more circumspect.
While the bridge is a concrete symbol of China and North Korea's efforts to revitalise relations that have waned from their Cold War-era peak, the prospects for trade are hindered by obstacles ranging from international sanctions to a North Korean leadership that prioritises security above all else, experts say.
"It reflects China's renewed effort to strengthen its relationship with North Korea at a time of changing regional alignments," Phillip Park, a professor at the University of North Korean Studies in Seoul, South Korea, told Al Jazeera.
"But whether this leads to substantially deeper economic integration, greater North Korean dependence, or a significant shift in Pyongyang's strategic orientation remains an open question.
"The bridge could facilitate trade and logistics, but it does not remove international sanctions," Park added.
China, whose troops fought alongside Pyongyang in the 1950-53 Korean War, has long been North Korea's most crucial economic benefactor.
China accounts for as much as 95 percent of official trade with the secretive country, which is among the world's most diplomatically and economically isolated states.
Before the four-lane New Yalu River Bridge officially opened on Thursday, road and rail freight between Dandong and Sinuiju, the main route for China-North Korea trade, travelled exclusively via the ageing Sino-Korean Friendship Bridge.
Opened in 1943, the bridge, which has a single-lane road and a rail track, can only carry vehicles weighing a maximum of 20 tonnes.
Jae-woo Joo, a professor of Chinese foreign policy at Kyung Hee University in Seoul, said the new bridge could "tremendously" boost trade between the sides, including the transfer of components banned under sanctions against Pyongyang's nuclear and missile programmes.
"It is therefore really significant to open this bridge with China," Joo told Al Jazeera.
After coming under strain from North Korea's nuclear weapons tests in the mid-2000s, Beijing-Pyongyang ties have warmed under Chinese President Xi Jinping and North Korean leader Kim Jong Un.
At Xi and Kim's most recent meeting in June, their seventh summit since 2018, the leaders pledged to enhance cooperation in areas spanning trade, defence, agriculture, law, science and technology.
Analysts have, in part, cast Beijing's deepening engagement with Pyongyang as a response to the latter's growing ties with Moscow, which have included the deployment of thousands of North Korean troops to the front lines of Russia's war in Ukraine.
Last month, North Korean and Russian officials opened the first road bridge linking their countries across the Tumen River.
North Korea and China's two-way trade grew 25 percent in 2025 to reach $2.74bn, according to figures from China's General Administration of Customs.
Bilateral trade has continued to grow in 2026, jumping nearly 20 percent to reach $1.5bn in the first half of the year, putting it on track to reach its highest volumes since the COVID-19 pandemic.
But while growing, trade remains relatively minuscule in the context of global commerce.
With China's overall trade hitting $6.36 trillion in 2025, North Korea's contribution to the total was a little more than 0.04 percent.
Official trade between the neighbours also remains substantially below its 2010s heyday.
Trade hovered around $5-6bn throughout much of the decade, peaking at $6.45bn in 2013.
Economists have offered explanations for the slowdown that range from tightened sanctions to China's waning reliance on imports amid its efforts to boost economic self-reliance.
Christopher Green, a senior consultant for the Korean Peninsula at International Crisis Group, said the new border crossing's significance should not be overstated, considering that China-North Korea trade "is fundamentally driven by politics".
"Lack of cross-border transit routes is not the main obstacle," Green told Al Jazeera.
"If the bridge opening signifies a political desire on both sides to give political cover to rising trade volumes, then a boost to trade should follow," Green said, describing ties up until now as "steady-as-she-goes" economic engagement.
"Time will tell. But the key is the politics of national security, not economics."
As much as officials have hailed the New Yalu River Bridge as a reflection of Chinese-North Korean cooperation, the project had a long and torturous road to completion.
Construction began in 2011 and was finished on the Chinese side in 2014.
But North Korean authorities only completed roads and customs facilities on their side of the river this year following repeated pauses in construction amid tensions with Beijing.
"The 12-year delay certainly suggests that economic cooperation is more conditional than the language of friendship might imply," Marco Mari, a PhD researcher at Bocconi University in Italy who studies China-North Korea economic ties, told Al Jazeera.
"The bridge can facilitate supplies and economic activity, while Russian support gives North Korea alternatives that diminish Beijing's leverage," Mari added.
"This does not necessarily imply broader economic reform or a comparable improvement in ordinary people's lives."
Weiqi Zhang, an associate professor at Suffolk University who studies relations between North Korea and China, said the history of China-North Korea economic ties is littered with abortive projects, such as the Hwanggumpyong Special Economic Zone, a North Korean-Chinese development near Dandong.
"There were cases, like the Hwanggumpyong Special Economic Zone - established by China and North Korea - where China built infrastructure on its side, but North Korea didn't even pave the road on their side," Zhang told Al Jazeera.
"One reason is that, unlike its neighbours, North Korean government doesn't really pay much attention to trade for economic development," Zhang said.
"International trade is used by North Korea more like a foreign policy tool."
With China wishing to demonstrate its "indispensability" as a trade partner and North Korea eager to strengthen their economic ties, trade is likely to grow, said Edward Howell, a lecturer in international Relations at the University of Oxford.
Even so, economic engagement will be subject to political considerations, Howell said.
"North Korea will obviously want to avoid facing any Chinese influence to change its economic model, but Pyongyang will want to reap the economic benefits of trade with its principal economic benefactor," Howell told Al Jazeera.
