This remarkable new material could solve a major carbon capture problem
- Date:
- November 17, 2026
- Source:
- Oregon State University
- Summary:
- Scientists have developed a new carbon capture material that turns moisture from an obstacle into an advantage, potentially solving a major problem with removing CO2 from industrial emissions. The material works even in extremely humid conditions and can be reused repeatedly, offering a promising path toward cheaper, more effective carbon capture.
- Share:
A newly developed material from Oregon State University could help solve one of the biggest challenges in industrial carbon capture: removing carbon dioxide from emissions that contain large amounts of water vapor. Instead of allowing moisture to interfere with the process, the material uses it to help maintain effective carbon capture.
The researchers have filed a patent application for the innovation, which belongs to a class of materials known as metal-organic frameworks, or MOFs. Their findings, published in Angewandte Chemie, could help make carbon capture more practical and affordable for factories and other industrial facilities.
The advance could have important implications for reducing greenhouse gas emissions. Industrial operations, including the burning of fossil fuels to produce energy, are major sources of atmospheric carbon dioxide. In the United States, industry accounts for 30% of total greenhouse gas emissions, according to the Environmental Protection Agency.
A New Material That Overcomes a Major Carbon Capture Problem
The new material, called BVR-X, has an unusual internal structure that keeps water and carbon dioxide largely separated within its microscopic pores. By directing the two substances into different regions, it prevents water molecules from occupying the spaces needed to capture carbon dioxide.
"This internal organization helps the material work under conditions that more closely resemble real industrial emissions," said Kyriakos Stylianou, professor of chemistry in the OSU College of Science.
MOFs are crystalline materials built from positively charged metal ions connected by organic molecules called ligands, which act as linkers. The metal ions form connection points, or nodes, while the linkers join them together into repeating structures resembling tiny cages. These structures contain extremely small pores that can trap gas molecules on their internal surfaces through a process called adsorption, much like a sponge holds substances within its structure.
One of the advantages of MOFs is their remarkable adaptability. Scientists can combine different metals and organic linkers to create materials with specific characteristics. According to Stylianou, millions of MOF designs are theoretically possible. Researchers have already synthesized more than 100,000 distinct MOFs and predicted the properties of hundreds of thousands more.
Why Carbon Capture Needs Better Materials
"The capture of CO2 is critical for meeting net-zero emission targets," said Stylianou, who directs OSU's Materials Discovery Laboratory, known as the MaD Lab. "MOFs have shown a lot of promise because of their porosity and their structural versatility."
Carbon dioxide is a major greenhouse gas released when fossil fuels are burned. As it accumulates in the atmosphere, it traps heat and contributes to global warming.
One approach to addressing the problem involves removing carbon dioxide directly from the atmosphere. Facilities designed for this purpose are beginning to operate in different parts of the world. The world's largest such plant opened in Iceland in 2024, although its annual carbon removal capacity is still relatively small compared with global emissions. According to Stylianou, the facility can capture roughly as much carbon dioxide in one year as about 7,200 cars produce over the same period.
Capturing carbon dioxide before it reaches the atmosphere is another option, and the technologies used for this purpose are considerably more established. At factories and power plants, carbon capture systems can intercept emissions as they travel through exhaust systems and smokestacks. Some of these systems use MOFs to adsorb carbon dioxide molecules from the passing gases.
How Water Makes Industrial Carbon Capture Difficult
A major obstacle, however, is that industrial exhaust often contains substantial amounts of water vapor. Many MOFs that perform well in controlled laboratory experiments lose much of their carbon capture ability when exposed to the humid conditions found in actual smokestacks.
The gases can be dried before carbon dioxide is removed, but doing so requires additional equipment and energy. Those costs can become so significant that the overall process is no longer economically practical for industrial use.
BVR-X offers a different approach. Rather than requiring moisture to be removed, the material responds to water in a way that preserves its ability to collect carbon dioxide. Its internal organization allows water and carbon dioxide to occupy different regions, helping prevent the competition that normally undermines carbon capture performance.
"Water usually makes capturing carbon dioxide more difficult," he said. "Our new material does something unusual: It responds to water by changing in a way that lets it keep capturing CO2 effectively even under very humid conditions."
Strong Performance Under Realistic Industrial Conditions
Laboratory tests demonstrated that BVR-X could efficiently capture carbon dioxide from a highly humid gas stream containing only 4% carbon dioxide.
That concentration is particularly relevant to emissions from burning natural gas, where carbon dioxide makes up a relatively small proportion of the exhaust. Extracting it becomes especially difficult when large quantities of water vapor are also present.
The experiments therefore tested the material under conditions that resemble an important real-world challenge, "where separating a small amount of CO2 in the presence of substantial water is particularly challenging," said Stylianou.
The researchers also found that BVR-X could repeatedly capture and release carbon dioxide without losing its effectiveness over dozens of cycles. This ability to regenerate the material is important because commercial carbon capture systems must reuse their capture materials many times to keep operating costs manageable.
"And the material can be regenerated and reused, maintaining performance through dozens of capture-and-release cycles and tolerating demanding conditions," he added. "Those are important qualities for practical carbon-capture technologies."
The results suggest that BVR-X could help overcome a major limitation of existing MOF technologies, particularly in industrial settings where removing moisture from exhaust gases would otherwise be costly.
Research Team and Funding
The study brought together researchers from several institutions. Stylianou worked with MaD Lab members Ankit Yadav, Emmanuel Musa and Andrzej Gładysiak, along with Micah Hickethier, Chun-Wai Chang and Kai Shen Choong from the Oregon State College of Engineering.
Additional collaborators included scientists from the University of California, Berkeley; the University of Oregon; and the ARAMCO Research and Development Center.
