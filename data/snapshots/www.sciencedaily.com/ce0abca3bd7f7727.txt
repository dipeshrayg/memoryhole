Scientists have found an unexpected new use for vitamin B12, a nutrient best known for its importance to human health. Researchers at the University of Córdoba have incorporated the vitamin into a rechargeable battery, demonstrating for the first time that it can serve as a catalyst in this type of energy storage system.
Vitamin B12 is essential for normal bodily function and is naturally present in animal products. The vitamin is produced by certain bacteria, which occur in nature and are also grown commercially to supply the food and pharmaceutical industries. In addition to its established applications in medicine, nutrition, and veterinary care, B12 is used to address certain nutritional deficiencies. It has also become popular alongside other B vitamins as a hangover remedy because of its potential to help reduce fatigue.
Now, scientists are exploring an entirely different application. By using the vitamin to support the chemical reactions inside a battery, they have developed a prototype capable of completing up to 250 charging and discharging cycles.
How Vitamin B12 Makes a Rechargeable Battery Possible
The new battery was developed by the Physical Chemistry research group (FQM-204) at the University of Córdoba's Chemical Institute for Energy and the Environment (IQUEMA). It uses zinc and oxygen from the surrounding air to produce electricity, with vitamin B12, also called cobalamin, playing a crucial role in the process.
To make the design work, the researchers attached vitamin B12 molecules to single-walled carbon nanotubes. The vitamin contains cobalt (III) at its center, which helps drive the electrochemical reactions needed for the battery to function.
The team has previously experimented with natural molecules as battery catalysts, including hemoglobin and catalase. These substances contain iron (II) and iron (III), respectively. Vitamin B12 represents the group's third effort to use a biological molecule for this purpose, but it has delivered an important advance: a battery that can be recharged repeatedly, completing as many as 250 cycles.
One of the biggest obstacles was keeping vitamin B12 inside the battery. Because the vitamin dissolves readily in water, it would normally be difficult to retain in a system that relies on a water-based electrolyte.
The researchers overcame this problem by securing the vitamin to carbon nanotubes, using an approach inspired by how amino acids connect to form proteins.
Manuel Cano Luna, one of the study's authors, explained that "we looked at the properties we can find in naturally occurring materials to use them in new batteries," while describing the team's search for useful biological compounds.
Their attachment technique imitates "the process by which amino acids bind together in proteins."
Alongside Cano Luna, the research team included Valentín García Caballero, Miguel López León, Pablo Gómez Argudo, Luis Camacho Delgado, José Miguel Rodríguez Mellado, María Teresa Martín Romero, José Abad López, and Antonio Jesús Fernández Romero.
A Safer, Lighter Alternative to Lithium-Ion Batteries
The findings, published in the Journal of Materials Chemistry A, could contribute to the development of battery technologies that depend less on the materials used in conventional lithium-ion batteries.
Although lithium-ion batteries are widely used, obtaining their raw materials can involve significant economic, environmental, and social challenges. Zinc-air batteries offer a potential alternative because they can store substantial amounts of energy relative to their weight while relying on materials that are more readily available.
Zinc is abundant and recyclable, and these batteries use oxygen from the surrounding atmosphere as part of their energy-producing reactions. Because they draw oxygen from the air rather than storing all the necessary reactants internally, they can also be lighter than other battery designs.
This advantage has helped make zinc-air batteries increasingly useful in compact electronic devices, particularly hearing aids. Another benefit is their use of non-flammable, water-based electrolytes, which can reduce fire risks and improve safety.
Why Vitamin B12 Is Key to Recharging
A major challenge in developing rechargeable zinc-air batteries is finding a catalyst that can efficiently support the chemical reactions involved in both producing and storing electricity.
During discharge, the battery converts chemical energy into electrical energy. Recharging requires the relevant chemical reactions to proceed in the opposite direction. A suitable catalyst must therefore be effective in both processes.
According to researcher Antonio Jesús Fernández Romero, vitamin B12 "has proven to meet the necessary conditions to act as a good catalyst because, during discharge, it catalyzes the reduction of oxygen to water and, during recharging, it promotes the production of oxygen."
This ability to support both reactions is what makes the vitamin particularly promising for rechargeable zinc-air battery technology.
Could Vitamin B12 Help Store Renewable Energy?
The successful prototype offers a new direction for researchers seeking affordable, sustainable alternatives to existing energy storage technologies.
If the approach can be developed further, batteries using vitamin B12 catalysts could eventually help address one of the biggest challenges facing renewable energy: storing excess electricity generated by sources such as solar and wind power for use when production falls.
Although the technology remains at the research stage, demonstrating up to 250 charge and discharge cycles shows that a familiar biological molecule can play an unexpected role in rechargeable battery design. The discovery adds to growing efforts to use naturally occurring substances to create safer, lighter, and more sustainable ways to store energy.
