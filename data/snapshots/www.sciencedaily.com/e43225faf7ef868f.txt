A massive galaxy cluster dating back to an era when such enormous structures were only beginning to take shape is challenging scientists' understanding of how the universe evolved. New observations from NASA's James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) reveal that the cluster contains an unusually dense concentration of matter, suggesting that some cosmic structures may have developed much faster than current models predict.
The galaxy cluster, known as XLSSC 122, has become the focus of three recent studies led by researchers at IPAC, Caltech's science and data center for astrophysics and planetary science. Their findings establish XLSSC 122 as the most distant galaxy cluster known to exhibit strong gravitational lensing, a phenomenon that allows astronomers to measure the mass of otherwise invisible matter. The research was presented at a press conference on June 17, 2026, during the 248th meeting of the American Astronomical Society.
Located approximately 10.4 billion light-years from Earth, XLSSC 122 stood out to astronomers because of its surprising maturity. Although it existed during a period when galaxy clusters were still assembling, it already displayed the size and organized structure typically associated with clusters in the modern universe.
Webb has now uncovered another remarkable feature. XLSSC 122 happens to lie directly in front of one or more galaxies that are even farther away. The cluster's powerful gravity bends and distorts the light traveling from those background galaxies, creating a rare effect called strong gravitational lensing. This fortunate alignment has given scientists an exceptionally precise way to measure how mass is distributed throughout the cluster.
"When we got those first images back from JWST, we said, 'wow, look at this, there's strong lensing coming from this cluster!'" said Kyle Finner, a staff scientist at IPAC and lead author of the first paper examining the cluster. "XLSSC 122 has now set the record for the most distant galaxy cluster displaying strong lensing, which is a valuable tool for astronomers."
An Ancient Galaxy Cluster Challenges Cosmic Predictions
The newly detected gravitational lensing has allowed researchers to examine the distribution of mass in an ancient galaxy cluster in greater detail than ever before. XLSSC 122 dates to a period known as "cosmic noon," roughly 10 billion years ago, when the universe was experiencing its most intense era of star formation.
During this time, galaxies were producing stars at rates up to 100 times higher than those observed in the present-day universe. Galaxy clusters were also becoming increasingly common as smaller systems gradually combined into larger structures.
Yet XLSSC 122 appears to have developed in an unexpected way. The new measurements show that a remarkably large amount of its mass is packed into its central region. According to standard cosmological models, the growth of massive structures should have been more gradual, making such a concentrated cluster particularly surprising at this early stage of cosmic history.
"XLSSC 122 is one of the first clusters we know of that formed in the universe, and it has a mass concentration that doesn't agree with our cosmological model predictions," said Finner.
To investigate the mystery further, Finner and his collaborators conducted two additional JWST studies, first authored by Zachary Scofield and Hyungjin Joo at Yonsei University. Together, the three papers provide a much more complete picture of this unusual cluster and offer new insights into galaxy cluster formation during cosmic noon.
James Webb Reveals Hidden Dark Matter
Astronomers originally identified XLSSC 122 in 2014 through an X-ray survey carried out by the European Space Agency's XMM-Newton spacecraft. Later observations with the Hubble Space Telescope helped establish its distance of approximately 10.4 billion light-years and confirmed that the cluster appeared unusually developed for its age.
However, Hubble did not reveal clear evidence of strong gravitational lensing. That changed when JWST captured distinctive curved arcs of light surrounding the cluster's center, providing the first definitive signs of this phenomenon in XLSSC 122.
"Before JWST, we couldn't do this level of science in the early, distant universe," said Finner.
The gravitational distortions are caused primarily by something astronomers cannot directly observe: dark matter.
Although XLSSC 122 contains visible stars and glowing gas, these familiar forms of matter account for only a small fraction of the gravitational lensing effect. Most of the distortion comes from dark matter, an invisible substance that has not been directly detected through light but whose gravitational influence is supported by extensive astronomical evidence.
Scientists estimate that dark matter is approximately five times more abundant than ordinary matter, which makes up stars, planets, and everything around us. Its gravity helps hold galaxies together and shapes the universe's enormous network of galaxy groups, clusters, and filaments.
Because dark matter plays such an important role in cosmic structure, determining how it was distributed billions of years ago offers a powerful way to test theories describing the universe's development since the Big Bang approximately 13.8 billion years ago.
"Strong lensing is a way to measure the dark matter without actually seeing the dark matter," said Finner. "It gives us a sensitive probe of our cosmological models."
A Wider View Reveals a Galaxy Cluster Merger
In their second paper, the researchers investigated XLSSC 122 using another form of gravitational lensing known as weak lensing.
Strong gravitational lensing produces obvious distortions, including the striking arcs captured by JWST. Weak lensing, by comparison, causes much smaller changes in the apparent shapes of distant galaxies. These subtle effects are difficult to identify individually, so astronomers rely on statistical methods to detect them.
The two techniques provide complementary information. Strong lensing is especially useful for measuring the mass concentrated near a cluster's center, while weak lensing can reveal how matter is distributed across its outer regions.
"Weak gravitational lensing can constrain mass much further out, so you can get a better picture of the surrounding cluster area," said Finner.
By combining these measurements with X-ray and radio observations from other telescopes, the scientists determined that XLSSC 122 is still undergoing a merger. Its galaxies are continuing to come together as the larger cluster takes shape.
The broader analysis also reinforced the earlier finding that an unusually large amount of mass is concentrated near the cluster's center.
Ancient Starlight Offers New Clues to Dark Matter
The team's third and most recent paper revealed another important feature of XLSSC 122: faint light produced by stars that are no longer bound to individual galaxies.
Known as intracluster light, this diffuse glow comes from stars scattered into the spaces between galaxies. Webb's observations represent the earliest known detection of intracluster light, providing another way to examine the development of galaxy clusters in the distant universe.
The widespread distribution of this light supports the conclusion that XLSSC 122 is undergoing a merger. As galaxies interact and collide, some stars are pulled away from their original galaxies and spread throughout the cluster. In XLSSC 122, many of these displaced stars appear not to have settled into its dense central region.
The researchers also uncovered an intriguing connection between these wandering stars and dark matter.
Near the cluster's center, the shape of the intracluster light closely matches the distribution of dark matter inferred from strong gravitational lensing. This suggests that the faint glow of stars between galaxies could potentially serve as a visible indicator of where invisible dark matter is concentrated.
If astronomers observe the same relationship in other clusters, particularly those from the early universe, intracluster light could become a valuable new tool for mapping dark matter across cosmic history.
"In this cluster, the intracluster light essentially traces the dark matter," said Finner. "That light tells us that the cluster is in a merging state."
Could More Ancient Galaxy Clusters Challenge Cosmology?
The next goal for Finner and his colleagues is to identify and investigate dozens of additional galaxy clusters from similarly early periods in the universe's history.
Finding these distant objects requires a different approach from studying them in detail. Although JWST is exceptionally powerful, its relatively narrow field of view makes it better suited to examining selected targets than searching enormous areas of the sky.
Instead, astronomers must first identify promising candidates through wide-area X-ray and radio surveys, similar to the observations that originally revealed XLSSC 122.
One promising technique relies on the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect, which occurs when energetic particles inside galaxy clusters scatter radiation from the cosmic microwave background, the faint afterglow of the Big Bang.
This interaction can create distinctive regions that appear as "holes" in observations of the cosmic microwave background. By searching for these signatures, astronomers can locate galaxy clusters that might otherwise be difficult to detect.
Finding more objects like XLSSC 122 could have major implications for cosmology. If other ancient clusters also contain unexpectedly concentrated dark matter and display unusually advanced development, scientists may need to revise some of their basic assumptions about how quickly large cosmic structures formed.
For now, XLSSC 122 provides an important test of existing models, but a larger sample will be necessary to determine whether its unusual properties are rare or part of a broader pattern in the early universe.
"It's still early in the JWST era," said Finner, "and if we can start to get data on tens or hundreds of these types of objects at this stage in the universe, then we can really start putting our cosmological models to the test."
