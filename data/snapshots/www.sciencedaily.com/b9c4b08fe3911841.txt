CERN scientists finally find evidence of a particle decay so rare it took 40 years to detect
- Date:
November 20, 2026
- Source:
University of Manchester
- Summary:
Scientists at CERN have found the first evidence of an incredibly rare particle decay after more than 40 years of searching, detecting a process expected to occur just once in every 10 billion beauty particles. The breakthrough could help physicists explore a mysterious microscopic realm where unknown particles and forces may be hiding.
- Share:
After more than four decades of searching, physicists at CERN have finally found evidence of an extraordinarily rare particle decay that occurs only about once in every 10 billion beauty particles. The long-awaited result could help scientists investigate a largely unexplored realm of physics where unknown particles and forces of nature may exist.
The evidence comes from the upgraded LHCb experiment at CERN's Large Hadron Collider (LHC). Researchers at the University of Manchester played important roles in the international effort, contributing to the advanced technology that made the measurement possible.
The elusive process, called 'Bd to mu mu', has a statistical significance of 3.2 sigma. That is strong enough to qualify as evidence, but physicists will need additional data before they can officially declare a discovery. Even so, the finding marks a major milestone in a search that has lasted more than 40 years.
It also creates new opportunities to test the Standard Model, the leading scientific framework describing fundamental particles and how they interact. By examining this exceptionally rare decay in greater detail, researchers hope to uncover subtle clues that could eventually point toward physics beyond our current understanding.
Why Rare Particle Decays Could Reveal New Physics
The LHCb experiment specializes in studying particles that contain beauty quarks. These are heavier relatives of the up and down quarks that make up much of the ordinary matter around us.
When particles collide inside the Large Hadron Collider, enormous numbers of beauty quarks are produced. However, they survive for only about one and a half trillionths of a second before decaying into other particles.
Scientists study these transformations because they may reveal the influence of particles that cannot be directly observed at the collider. By measuring exactly how frequently different decays occur, physicists can search for unexpected effects that might indicate previously unknown physics.
Particularly rare decays offer an important advantage. Because they happen so infrequently, their rates can be especially sensitive to contributions from undiscovered particles. Even small differences between predicted and observed decay rates could provide valuable evidence of new forces or particles.
A One in 10 Billion Particle Decay
The newly observed signal involves a process known as 'Bd to mu mu'. During this decay, a particle containing a beauty quark transforms into two muons, which are heavier relatives of electrons.
According to theoretical predictions, this transformation should occur only about once in every 10 billion beauty particles. Despite decades of investigation, no individual experiment had previously produced convincing evidence that it occurs.
The upgraded LHCb experiment has now achieved a statistical significance of 3.2 sigma. Although this meets the standard for reporting evidence, particle physicists generally require a significance of five sigma before announcing a confirmed discovery.
Professor Chris Parkes, Head of the Department of Physics and Astronomy at the University of Manchester, led the international LHCb collaboration during construction of its major upgrade. He also oversaw the collaboration's approval of the new findings.
He explained: "The upgraded LHCb was a major undertaking -- a fifteen-year design and construction project from a 1900 international member collaboration.
"The UK made the largest national contribution to the project, pioneering new technologies that have made today's result possible, where we probe nature at a new scale. But this is also just the start; we are planning the construction of an experiment that will exploit the capabilities of the next-generation High Luminosity LHC, probing physics at an even smaller scale."
Parkes, who became a Fellow of the Royal Society earlier this year in recognition of his contributions to LHCb, offered a memorable comparison to illustrate just how improbable the decay is.
"This decay is remarkably unlikely; the chance of it happening is roughly like going on a romantic date and, on your very first try, finding the one perfect match for you among everyone on Earth."
Advanced LHC Detectors Make the Breakthrough Possible
Detecting such an uncommon event requires instruments capable of following the movements of particles with extraordinary accuracy.
One of the most important components of the upgraded LHCb experiment is a silicon pixel detector developed with major contributions from researchers in the United Kingdom. Called the Vertex Locator, it measures the paths of particles generated during collisions inside the collider.
Positioned just five millimeters from the LHC beams, the detector allows researchers to determine precisely where particles originate and where they subsequently decay.
Dr. Stefano De Capua, who directed detector construction at the University of Manchester, described the extensive collaboration behind the technology.
"We worked with nearly 90 UK companies to construct this detector and trained the next generation of scientists and engineers on the novel technologies we are using. It is these detectors that we use to locate where the beauty meson was produced and pinpoint where it decayed into two muons."
The wider LHCb upgrade also allows the experiment to gather information at up to five times its previous rate. This greater capacity, together with improvements in tracking technology, is helping physicists identify events that were previously too difficult to measure reliably.
Exploring the Mysterious Zeptoverse
Beyond confirming an exceptionally rare particle process, the result could help scientists investigate what they call the 'Zeptoverse'.
This name refers to an almost unimaginably small scale, measured in zeptometers. A single zeptometer is just a billionth of a trillionth of a meter. Physicists suspect that effects from currently unknown particles and forces could become apparent when nature is examined at these extreme scales.
For now, the new measurement agrees with the Standard Model. Researchers have not found evidence that the established theory is wrong, but increasingly precise measurements could reveal deviations that current experiments cannot yet detect.
Such differences would be especially significant because they could indicate where the Standard Model stops providing a complete explanation of the physical world.
Professor Conor Fitzpatrick of the University of Manchester, who serves as Deputy Physics Coordinator of the international LHCb collaboration, emphasized the importance of finally obtaining the long-sought signal.
"There has been a more than forty year long search by physicists worldwide to observe this decay. It is a golden channel to observe the effects of new physics from beyond our current knowledge.
"The results from this experiment are expanding our understanding of nature at its most fundamental level. We are excited to have finally seen this elusive signal and looking forward to the future discoveries that will come from our data."
What Comes Next for CERN's Particle Research?
The upgraded LHCb experiment is expected to continue gathering a substantially larger collection of measurements into the early 2030s. With more data, physicists should be able to determine the frequency of this rare decay with much greater precision.
Researchers are also planning another major upgrade that could increase data collection rates by an additional factor of ten. This would improve their ability to investigate extremely rare particle processes and search for the effects of unknown particles at even smaller distance scales.
Professor Brian Cox, a particle physicist at the University of Manchester who did not participate in the experiment, highlighted how improvements in scientific instruments can lead to important advances in understanding nature.
"This is an important result and a beautiful example of how science advances. We upgrade our instruments, and we see nature in greater detail. Occasionally we see something we don't understand, and then progress is made. We cannot know whether this progress will be incremental or genuinely world-changing -- that's science! LHCb is a major scientific success."
The experiment receives funding from the Science and Technology Facilities Council, which operates within UK Research and Innovation. The UK LHCb collaboration brings together scientists from ten universities across the United Kingdom and the STFC Rutherford Appleton Laboratory.
Although the newly detected decay has not yet revealed any unknown particles or forces, it gives physicists a powerful new way to search for them. After more than 40 years of effort, an extraordinarily elusive process is finally becoming accessible to detailed study, potentially bringing researchers closer to understanding some of the universe's deepest mysteries.
