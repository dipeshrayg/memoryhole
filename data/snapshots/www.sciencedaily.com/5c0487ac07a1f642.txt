Time may be far stranger than we realize. Scientists have a way to test it
- Date:
- November 21, 2026
- Source:
- Kyushu University
- Summary:
- Time may be far stranger than we realize, and scientists have proposed a way to detect clocks experiencing multiple flows of time simultaneously. Using atomic clocks with dramatically enhanced sensitivity, researchers hope to reveal a never-before-observed quantum effect that could transform our understanding of time and gravity.
- Share:
We measure time constantly, from the ticking hands of a clock to the changing numbers on a smartphone screen. Yet despite how familiar time seems, physicists still do not fully understand its fundamental nature. Einstein's theory of relativity revealed that time does not pass at the same rate for everyone. Instead, its passage depends on how fast something is moving and the strength of the gravity it experiences.
Quantum physics introduces an even stranger possibility. According to quantum mechanics, a clock can exist in a state known as "superposition," in which its motion follows multiple possibilities simultaneously. Because motion affects the passage of time through relativity, this could allow different rates of time to coexist in a single quantum state. Scientists have never directly observed this remarkable connection between quantum mechanics and Einstein's theory.
Atomic Clocks Could Reveal the Quantum Nature of Time
Now, an international research team has developed a theoretical approach that could make this elusive phenomenon experimentally detectable. In a study published in Physical Review Letters, researchers led by Kyushu University, working with the Stevens Institute of Technology, University of Waterloo, the National Institute of Standards and Technology, Colorado State University, and Stockholm University, describe how today's advanced trapped-ion atomic clocks could detect what is known as the "quantum superposition of times."
Atomic clocks are among the most accurate timekeeping devices ever developed. Rather than relying on mechanical movement, they measure time using the extremely consistent frequencies associated with atoms. Their extraordinary accuracy makes them essential for technologies such as GPS, which depends on precise timing to calculate locations.
Modern atomic clocks have become so sensitive that they can detect the tiny differences in the passage of time predicted by Einstein's relativity, even between clocks separated vertically by only a few millimeters. This remarkable capability makes them promising tools for exploring effects that were previously beyond the reach of experiments.
A New Technique Boosts Sensitivity by Up to 1,000 Times
The researchers discovered that a clock's quantum motion can become linked to its internal energy through a phenomenon called entanglement. In quantum physics, entanglement occurs when two properties or systems become connected in a way that prevents them from being described independently. In this case, the connection produces measurable changes in the clock's quantum behavior, potentially providing evidence of different rates of time existing in superposition.
"It's the precision that led us to develop our theoretical model. We found that the atomic clock's motion becomes "entangled" with its internal energy. The signature of this entanglement is that the clock itself loses some of its quantum properties, which can be detected using modern techniques," explains Associate Professor Joshua Foo of Kyushu University's Institute for Advanced Studies, one of the paper's lead authors. "We introduced a new technique for controlling the atomic clock's motion, improving its sensitivity to this effect by 100 to 1000 times."
That improvement could make an enormous difference. By controlling the clock's motion more precisely, the proposed method could bring an exceptionally subtle quantum effect within the detection capabilities of existing advanced atomic clock technology.
A New Frontier in Quantum Physics and Gravity
The findings suggest that atomic clocks could become powerful instruments for investigating the quantum properties of time. Beyond opening new possibilities for fundamental physics experiments, the approach could also contribute to the development of even more accurate atomic clocks in the future.
For now, the research remains theoretical. The next challenge is to translate the proposed method into a working experiment capable of detecting the predicted effect while accounting for the disturbances and uncertainties that arise under real laboratory conditions.
"Naturally, bringing our theoretical model to reality is the big next step, and developing a detailed experiment that accounts for real-world unpredictability will give us further insight into our model," concludes Foo. "We are also interested in exploring whether atomic clocks could eventually be used to probe the quantum realm of gravity, the other fundamental question in physics."
If successful, such experiments could offer scientists a new way to investigate how quantum mechanics and relativity fit together. They might also provide valuable clues about quantum gravity, the long-sought framework that would explain how gravity behaves at the smallest scales of nature.
