A familiar dietary supplement combined with low-dose aspirin may offer an unexpected alternative to antibiotics for people with severe gum disease. In a year-long clinical trial, patients who received omega-3 and aspirin alongside conventional periodontal treatment experienced nearly identical clinical benefits to those who received antibiotics. Perhaps even more surprisingly, the improvements were still evident six months after supplementation ended.
The findings, published in the Journal of Periodontology, come from researchers affiliated with Albert Einstein Israelite Hospital, Guarulhos University (UNG), the University of Taubaté (UNITAU), and the Ribeirão Preto School of Dentistry at USP (FORP-USP) in Brazil, as well as Harvard University in the United States.
The study suggests that combining omega-3 with low-dose acetylsalicylic acid (ASA), better known as aspirin, could reduce the need for antibiotics in certain patients. The discovery is particularly timely given growing worldwide concerns about antibiotic resistance, which occurs when bacteria develop the ability to survive exposure to drugs designed to kill them.
Why Severe Gum Disease Is So Difficult To Treat
Researchers monitored 109 patients with advanced periodontitis over 12 months. Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease that develops when bacteria accumulate below the gumline, triggering an immune response that can gradually damage the tissues and bone that hold teeth in place. Without proper treatment, the disease can cause teeth to loosen and eventually fall out.
As periodontitis progresses, spaces known as periodontal pockets form between the teeth and gums. These pockets can become increasingly deep, making it difficult to remove harmful bacteria through ordinary brushing and flossing.
"Patients with severe disease have very deep pockets, which act as reservoirs for bacteria associated with the disease. It's a very challenging condition to treat," says dental surgeon Nídia Cristina Castro dos Santos, the first author of the study and a professor and researcher at Einstein.
Putting Omega-3 and Aspirin to the Test
The researchers randomly divided the participants into four treatment groups. Every patient received subgingival instrumentation, commonly referred to as scaling. This standard periodontal treatment involves removing bacterial deposits and hardened plaque from tooth surfaces beneath the gums. Each group also received a different complementary treatment.
Patients in the control group received inactive substitutes for the antibiotics, omega-3, and aspirin, supplied in capsules that looked identical to those containing the active treatments.
A second group received metronidazole and amoxicillin three times daily for 14 days. This combination of antibiotics has some of the strongest scientific evidence supporting its use as an additional treatment for severe periodontitis.
Participants in the third group took three grams of omega-3 each day, together with a daily dose of low-dose aspirin, for six months. The fourth group received both interventions, taking antibiotics for two weeks while following the six-month omega-3 and aspirin regimen.
The researchers assessed the patients' progress at three, six, and 12 months.
Omega-3 and Aspirin Rival Antibiotics
At the end of the study, approximately 58% of patients in the antibiotic group had reached the researchers' clinical treatment target, defined as having no more than four remaining deep periodontal pockets.
The omega-3 and aspirin group achieved an almost identical success rate of 57.7%. By comparison, only 23.1% of patients who underwent scaling and received placebos reached the same target. Interestingly, administering both antibiotics and the omega-3 and aspirin regimen together did not produce any additional benefits.
Although antibiotics can improve treatment outcomes in patients with particularly severe gum disease, they are not routinely necessary for everyone with periodontitis.
"The use of antibiotics isn't considered the gold standard of treatment. Although metronidazole and amoxicillin yield good results in the most severe cases, their use should be evaluated on a case-by-case basis due to risks related to bacterial resistance and possible adverse effects," says Castro dos Santos.
Helping the Body Naturally Resolve Inflammation
The findings build on earlier research into the potential role of omega-3 fatty acids in protecting oral health. In a previous investigation, also supported by FAPESP and published in Scientific Reports, scientists found that omega-3 supplementation reduced periodontal inflammation and bone loss in rats. The protective effects were particularly notable when supplementation was combined with physical exercise.
Although results from animal studies do not necessarily translate directly to humans, the earlier findings provided clues that omega-3 might help regulate the inflammatory processes that contribute to gum disease.
Omega-3 fatty acids are a group of fats found in foods such as oily fish and are widely available as dietary supplements. Beyond their nutritional importance, they serve as building blocks for certain molecules that help regulate inflammation.
Castro explains that omega-3 works differently from conventional anti-inflammatory medications. Instead of simply interfering with pathways that promote inflammation, omega-3 helps the body produce molecules involved in bringing inflammation to a natural conclusion and supporting tissue repair.
This process, known as the resolution of inflammation, is an active biological mechanism that helps the body return to normal after an immune response. In chronic inflammatory diseases such as periodontitis, inflammation can persist and contribute to ongoing tissue damage.
The researchers included low-dose aspirin because it can promote the formation of certain inflammation-resolving molecules. They suspected that combining aspirin with omega-3 could strengthen the body's ability to control the persistent inflammation associated with advanced gum disease.
Benefits That Lasted Beyond Treatment
The researchers had originally expected antibiotics and the omega-3 regimen to work best when administered together. Instead, the findings revealed that targeting inflammation could produce clinical improvements comparable to those achieved through antibiotic treatment.
"We imagined that the combination of antibiotics and omega-3 would be most effective in controlling periodontitis in these patients. We also believed that the results for the group that used omega-3 with ASA would be slightly lower than those for the antibiotic group. The result was surprising because the two therapies performed very similarly, paving the way for it to become a treatment option for these patients," says Castro.
Equally intriguing was how long the improvements lasted. Six months after patients stopped taking omega-3 and aspirin, their clinical outcomes remained similar to those observed when the treatment ended.
According to Castro, this persistence raises the possibility that adjusting the body's inflammatory response could have lasting therapeutic effects. However, scientists are still working to understand the biological mechanisms responsible.
A Potential Alternative Amid Rising Antibiotic Resistance
For dental surgeon Magda Feres, the study's lead author and a full professor at the Harvard School of Dental Medicine and the Graduate Program in Dentistry at UNG, targeting the body's inflammatory response could provide a valuable treatment option for certain patients.
"The combination of omega-3 and low-dose aspirin achieved clinical benefits comparable to those of the metronidazole and amoxicillin protocol, opening up a real alternative for those who can't take antibiotics, especially patients with allergies or intolerance to these medications," she says.
The potential to reduce antibiotic use has implications that extend well beyond dentistry. Antibiotic resistance ranks among the greatest threats to global public health, making it increasingly important to identify effective treatment strategies that do not depend on these medications.
"Demonstrating that it's possible to treat severe periodontitis by modulating the body's own response is new and relevant information that helps preserve antibiotics for when they are truly indispensable," says Feres.
More Research Is Needed Before Clinical Practice Changes
Despite the encouraging findings, the researchers caution that the results are not sufficient to justify an immediate change in how dentists treat severe periodontitis. The participants did not have other significant systemic or oral diseases, meaning the results may not apply equally to patients with additional health conditions.
Larger studies involving more diverse populations will be needed to validate the findings and determine which patients are most likely to benefit from each treatment strategy.
It is also important to recognize that omega-3 and aspirin were used alongside professional periodontal treatment, rather than as a substitute for it. Aspirin carries potential risks, including an increased risk of bleeding, and may not be appropriate for everyone.
Meanwhile, Feres and her colleagues are investigating how the different treatments affect the communities of bacteria living inside periodontal pockets. Early microbiological findings point to a decline in bacterial species associated with periodontal disease and an increase in microorganisms linked to healthier gums.
These ongoing investigations could help explain why targeting inflammation produced such promising results and whether the approach can reliably benefit a broader range of patients.
"It's an important step forward, but not the end of the story. Therefore, the message is one of cautious optimism: we have a promising, scientifically plausible alternative to antibiotics in select cases. Routine replacement will require more evidence," she concludes.
