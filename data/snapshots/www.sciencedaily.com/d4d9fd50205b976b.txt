Scientists discover a new type of depression that common antidepressants struggle to treat
Experts hope discovery will lead to alternative and more targeted behavioural and medical treatments, notably in young people in the early stages of these disorders.

November 24, 2026
- Source:

- Summary:
Researchers have discovered a new subtype of depression that may affect nearly one in four people with severe, recurrent, or persistent depression. Known as "circadian depression," it is linked to the body's internal clock and is associated with night owl tendencies, excessive sleep, persistent exhaustion, and more severe symptoms. Traditional antidepressants often work less effectively for these individuals, potentially explaining why some people struggle to find relief.

A newly identified form of depression may help explain why some people struggle to find relief from commonly prescribed antidepressants. Researchers at the University of Sydney have discovered a subtype called "circadian depression," which appears to be closely connected to the body's internal biological clock.
The discovery emerged from an analysis of clinical and genetic information from more than 10,000 Australians with depression. Nearly one-quarter of the participants in the study, which focused on people with severe, recurrent or persistent depression, showed characteristics of this newly identified subtype.
Circadian depression is associated with the body's 24-hour internal clock, which helps regulate when we feel awake and when we feel sleepy. People with this form of depression tend to respond less favorably to common serotonin-based antidepressants. However, treatments that target the brain circuits involved in controlling circadian rhythms, including melatonin, melatonin-targeted antidepressants and light therapy, may offer more effective options.
A Hidden Type of Depression Affecting Nearly One in Four Patients Studied
To investigate the biological differences between forms of depression, researchers examined information collected through the nationwide Australian Genetics of Depression Study. Their analysis included clinical histories and genetic data from more than 10,000 people who had experienced depression.
The researchers identified nearly 25 percent (2,604 participants) as having circadian depression. Several recognizable patterns distinguished these individuals from other people with depression.
Those with the circadian subtype were more likely to experience seasonal changes in their depression, sleep for longer periods when depressed, and wake up feeling exhausted despite spending more time in bed. They were also more likely to be night owls, meaning they naturally preferred staying awake late and waking later in the day.
The differences extended beyond sleep patterns. Depression often began earlier in life for these individuals, and their symptoms tended to be more severe (such as mania and higher levels of suicidal thoughts). Genetic analysis also revealed an increased predisposition to inflammatory and cardiovascular health problems, along with major neurodevelopmental disorders, including ADHD.
First author Dr. Emiliana Tonini from the University of Sydney's Brain and Mind Center said:
"Our research has identified circadian depression as a new subtype, affecting around a quarter of people in our study into those with severe, recurrent or persistent depression.
"Depression is not a uniform illness, and understanding the characteristics of different subtypes means that we can give people earlier access to treatments that will be most effective for them."
Why Common Antidepressants May Be Less Effective
One of the most important implications of the discovery involves how depression is treated.
Medications such as SSRIs and SNRIs (serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors) are generally among the first treatments offered to people diagnosed with depression. These drugs work by influencing chemical messengers in the brain that help regulate mood.
However, the researchers found that these medications tend to be less effective for people with circadian depression. Individuals with this subtype also experience more side effects, suggesting that conventional antidepressants may not be the best initial approach for everyone.
Because circadian depression is closely associated with the body's internal clock, therapies designed to influence that clock may be particularly useful. Light therapy, for example, uses controlled exposure to bright light to help regulate biological rhythms. Melatonin, a hormone involved in signaling when the body should prepare for sleep, and antidepressants that target melatonin pathways may also be relevant treatment options.
Co-senior author Dr. Jacob Crouse from the Brain and Mind Center explained:
"Because circadian depression is linked to people's internal body clock, treatments targeting the circadian rhythm, such as light therapy and melatonin, may be much more relevant, and clinicians should reach for these much earlier in people with this presentation.
"For those who find that antidepressants are not working for them, it is important to speak to your clinician for advice about your individual circumstances."
A New Approach Could Improve Depression Treatment in Young People
Recognizing circadian depression earlier could be especially important for younger patients, who may otherwise spend valuable time trying medications that are less suited to their particular form of depression.
Professor Ian Hickie, Co-Director of the Brain and Mind Centre and a clinical psychiatrist, warned that failing to identify this subtype in young people can contribute to unsuccessful early treatments and unnecessary side effects.
The findings highlight the importance of recognizing that depression is not a single, uniform condition. Different biological mechanisms may contribute to similar symptoms, meaning that treatments effective for one person may provide much less benefit to another.
By identifying patients whose depression is closely tied to circadian rhythms, clinicians may be able to offer more targeted treatments earlier rather than relying on the same initial medication approach for everyone.
"We really want young people to receive the most appropriate treatment early in their course of illness.
"Most young people will be offered SSRIs first up, but for at least a quarter there are better circadian-focused medical and behavioral options to help treat and manage their condition."
