About eight years ago, I was assigned to photograph the sentencing of Travis and Sarah Mitchell, a couple with deep roots in an insular faith-healing sect outside of Portland, Oregon.
The Mitchells, as members of the Followers of Christ, adhere to a doctrine that forbids medical care. Instead, followers rely on healing through faith. So when Sarah Mitchell gave birth prematurely to twins in 2017 - and both gasped for breath in their first hours - the couple didn't rush them to an emergency room. Instead, they turned to prayer.
One twin, Ginnifer, lived for four hours, each one a struggle. The family eventually sought medical care for her surviving twin sister, Evelyn, but only after the county's deputy medical examiner arrived at the location of the home birth and learned, though not right away, of Evelyn's existence. Even then, he had to seek help from police, who arrived at the Oregon City home and told reluctant family members to take the struggling infant to the hospital or they'd do it themselves.
The Mitchells pleaded guilty to criminally negligent homicide in the death of their infant daughter, Ginnifer, and to criminal mistreatment for failing to seek medical care for her surviving twin, Evelyn. The couple's lawyer told the judge that the Mitchells grieved the loss of Ginnifer and loved Evelyn. "Travis and Sarah are utterly sincere, decent and caring human beings," he told the judge. (The Mitchells and their lawyer did not respond to ProPublica and The Oregonian's recent requests to be interviewed.)
Today, my memory of the sentencing is more montage than detail. But I remember the crowded gallery, the couple in black-and-white-striped prison clothing, and their prison sentence of more than six years. Evelyn, who within days of her birth had been placed in the custody of Marshall and Jennifer Phillips, another couple in the Followers of Christ, wasn't present. She remained in the couple's permanent care.
The Mitchells went off to prison, and I went on with my life.
Such is the grinding rhythm of the daily news cycle. So often I see people on their worst day - and then never again. Stories don't really end, of course, but my role as a photojournalist is limited. I'm an observer rather than a participant, seeing mostly slivers of the human experience that offer only a window into the whole. It's rare that I get to see what happens next.
Never would I have dreamed that the surviving baby in this case, Evelyn, would be someone I'd later come to know and care about through my work. But that's precisely what happened eight years after that courtroom scene played out, when I began documenting the lives of Evelyn and her parents, the Phillipses. (In a legal filing in an ongoing case relating to Evelyn's custody, the Mitchells criticized the Phillipses for allowing her to participate in our coverage.) The resulting story, a collaboration between ProPublica and The Oregonian, is one of several recently published about faith healing and the Followers of Christ church. It intimately explores the Phillipses' journey out of the sect and illustrates how changes in Oregon state law - in contrast to neighboring Idaho, where the Followers have a handful of churches - have led to a reduction in child deaths.
To spend time around 9-year-old Evelyn is to behold a luminous, magnetic force of nature. It's impossible to imagine this same girl as a premature baby so small that the only items that fit her were doll clothes purchased from the supermarket toy aisle.

For months, as I documented Evelyn's life - from her baptism to her home life to doctors' appointments - my journalism colleagues worked on their own pieces of our project. Teresa Mahoney, video editor at The Oregonian, produced a video introduction to the story that included a sequence of footage recorded by body cameras worn by police officers who stood outside Evelyn's grandparents' house that night, at first nudging, then ordering, Evelyn's family to get her to the hospital. Each time I viewed it, as I watched the officers politely letting time slip away, I'd need to regulate my own breathing as I imagined Evelyn on the other side of that door, struggling to breathe herself.
I hope that reporter Noelle Crombie's words, Teresa's video and my photographs allow readers to see not only the journey of the couple who are raising Evelyn as their own but also the surviving - indeed, thriving - twin I came to know. That Evelyn is alive at all is, depending on your worldview, a miracle.
My work is all about observing and capturing moments, about freezing time. Here, I was witnessing the unfolding of time. I got to see what happened next.
