Ahead of next week's midterm elections, President Donald Trump has systematically demolished institutional guardrails that prevented him from overturning the 2020 presidential election, ProPublica's reporting has shown.
Trump has empowered his loyalists to try to transform the voting process. He launched an unprecedented - and largely unsuccessful - effort to find noncitizen voters. And he tried to restrict mail-in voting.
Our reporters have spent much of this year covering Trump's efforts to exert more federal control over elections. Here are five takeaways from their reporting.
1. The Trump administration has sought greater federal control over elections.
In April, Doug Bock Clark and Jen Fifield reported on Trump's efforts to "take over" the midterm elections, detailing how the administration has discarded or diminished endeavors across the federal government to protect the voting process or block foreign interference. Those efforts have included cutting staff on election-related teams at federal law enforcement agencies and finding ways to exert greater federal control over voting.
Our reporting led by Peter Elkind revealed that the Trump administration harnessed Homeland Security Investigations' personnel, technology and legal authority to try to back up Trump's unproven claim that illegal voting by noncitizens is tilting elections toward Democrats. Administration officials insisted the effort spearheaded by HSI was producing meaningful results. Our analysis of Justice Department data found, as of May, 41 alleged noncitizens were charged with voting illegally or other election-related crimes. Earlier this month, CBS reported 156 people have been charged since the beginning of the second Trump administration.
As part of that effort, the Justice Department has demanded states turn over unredacted voter rolls, which contain confidential information on millions of Americans and have historically been managed by states. Since September 2025, the department has sued 30 states and the District of Columbia for the confidential data. Wary of federal overreach, state officials - including some Republicans - have fought back, and so far every DOJ lawsuit that's been decided by a federal judge has been dismissed. (The Trump administration has appealed most of its losses to federal appeals courts and has said it will consider taking its fight to the Supreme Court. A Justice Department spokesperson said its "attorneys are skilled, principled advocates who argue every case honestly and in good faith.")
In a March executive order, Trump instructed the U.S. Postal Service to create new regulations for mail voting. The rules would have put the Postal Service in the unprecedented role of deciding whether to send or deny the delivery of voters' mail-in ballots. The Supreme Court in September blocked the rules, at least until after November.
The Trump administration has said its actions will make U.S. elections fairer and more secure - and keep those prohibited from voting, such as noncitizens, from doing so. Spokespeople for the DOJ and Department of Homeland Security emphasized that their departments are focused on ensuring elections are free and fair, and that they are working closely with the states to achieve those goals. Contentions to the contrary, they say, are false.
2. Trump has pushed out career officials - and is coordinating with a conservative grassroots organization to transform elections.
At least 75 nonpartisan career officials who'd held roles at federal agencies related to election integrity and safety have been fired or left the government.
Two dozen Trump appointees - including many from the election-denial movement - have been hired. Ahead of the midterms, current and former government officials and election security experts have expressed concerns that once-fringe actors who've espoused debunked conspiracy theories about elections are now in positions to control the narrative around the voting process. (A DHS spokesperson said it hires "experts with diverse backgrounds who go through a rigorous vetting process.")
At least 25 administration appointees have close ties to Cleta Mitchell, a lawyer who leads Election Integrity Network, a grassroots conservative group focused on transforming America's voting system. This month, Clark profiled Mitchell - and detailed how, despite not holding a formal role in the White House, she's played a key role in shaping the Trump administration's elections agenda. The White House has appointed her associates to help hunt for noncitizen voters and investigate accusations of voting fraud in 2020. (Mitchell declined multiple requests to speak with ProPublica, but wrote in an email: "You're my opponent. ... You're never going to write in any objective, non-judgmental way about the election integrity movement.")

Mitchell, who played a role in helping Trump challenge his loss in the 2020 presidential election, has been leading efforts to pass state and federal iterations of the SAVE Act. The 2026 version of the bill, known as the SAVE America Act, would require voters nationwide to provide proof of citizenship to register and present a photo ID to vote, and would compel states to turn over their voter rolls to DHS. Trump has repeatedly called the measure his top legislative priority, but it has been blocked in the Senate.
In July, Trump also pushed out the remaining members of the bipartisan Election Assistance Commission, a four-member board designed to be evenly split between Republicans and Democrats that sets standards for state voting systems. (At the time, a White House official wouldn't confirm the moves but said the president "reserves the right to remove individuals that may not be totally aligned with the important task of securing America's elections and ensuring every legal vote is counted.")
3. Trump officials asked a cybersecurity agency to secretly acquire a voting machine - and hack it.
A few months into Trump's second term, political appointees at DHS asked its Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency to procure a voting machine, hack it and report the findings so they could be given to the White House, two former administration officials with knowledge of the request told Clark and Andy Mannix.
CISA employees told the political appointees their request was impossible, in part because the agents capable of pulling off such an operation had just been laid off by Elon Musk's Department of Government Efficiency. (One of Trump's first actions when he returned to the White House was to dismantle the agency's election security work.)
Though not acted on, the request shows how the Trump administration has tried to use the agency for its own ends while draining it of resources for one of its critical missions: protecting the nation's voting systems. Since CISA became an agency in 2018, it has come to provide crucial support to thousands of local election officials grappling with increasingly sophisticated attacks.
But some state election officials said CISA provided them with little help as they planned for the 2026 midterms. And other state authorities told Clark and Mannix that they no longer trusted the agency and feared it had been compromised by the Trump administration. (A DHS spokesperson told us in April the "claim that DHS has a broken partnership with states and made our elections less secure is simply false.")
DHS did not respond to questions for our October investigation, but a DHS spokesperson previously told ProPublica that the changes at CISA were in response to "a ballooning budget concealing a dangerous departure from its statutory mission," which included "electioneering instead of defending America's critical infrastructure." A White House spokesperson declined an interview request but said, "President Trump has done more than any other President to ensure we have free and fair elections."
4. The White House floated an idea that would've shut down virtually all mail-in voting.
The White House initially proposed a change for the Postal Service that would've been even more disruptive than the rule the Supreme Court rejected in September.
As Fifield and Elkind reported, the Trump administration suggested all ballots be delivered through certified mail, meaning voters would need to be home to sign for delivery of their ballots. The Trump administration dropped this plan after the USPS said it would be too difficult to execute. It instead proceeded with the executive order that would've forced postal workers to serve as gatekeepers, deciding which ballots would be delivered and which wouldn't.
In an emailed statement, White House spokesperson Lauren Bis denied the administration had interfered improperly with the Postal Service, an independent agency whose governance structure is specifically designed to protect it from political influence.
The Supreme Court's order in September means that voting by mail can proceed as planned for the midterms, with states left to decide the rules for mail-in ballots.
5. The Postal Service's cost-cutting measures have led to delays and more rejected ballots in rural areas.
Amid the increased attention on the Postal Service's role in elections, a ProPublica analysis found that delays resulting from the agency's cost-cutting measures in recent years have endangered the validity of ballots cast in rural areas.
The Postal Service has consolidated processing of first-class mail into regional hubs, and the agency cut the frequency of mail pickups in half at many post offices 50 miles or more from those processing plants. The changes often added a day or more to delivery time and pushed back postmarking, an official mark confirming an envelope has been received by the Postal Service.
Across five states where the best data was available - Washington, California, Ohio, North Carolina and Oregon - the delays during this year's primary elections have had the most substantial effect in areas that voted for Trump in 2024.
Postal Service leaders say such delays are less likely in the Nov. 3 general election because of "extraordinary measures" the agency puts in place for these votes, when there's typically far more turnout.
