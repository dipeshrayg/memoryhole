The prime minister made a lot of the UK's new deal with the EU cutting costs for business and consumers during tough times.
In another world, Andy Burnham would have loved for the nation's supermarkets to clearly announce that they could cut food prices as a result of this latest agreement on food exports.
And while grocers have very much welcomed Friday's announcement, they have notably not committed to price cuts as a direct result of this move.
The managing director of M&S, Alex Freudmann, called the deal a "major step forward", saying it would remove bureaucracy. Tesco chief executive Ken Murphy added it was "good news for British farmers and producers".
Freudmann highlighted some of hoops that were hurting UK-EU trade, using the example that "getting our spaghetti Bolognese from Lincolnshire onto a shelf in Dublin takes hundreds of extra miles, mountains of paperwork, a vet's signature and even the Latin name of a cow".
Such measures will no longer need to be taken once this deal kicks in. Good news for business exports then, but will that lead to lower shopping bills here in the UK?
This deal will matter for food exporters - it may not have a clear visible impact at the checkout.
Privately, industry insiders say there are too many moving parts in the world economy, especially given the energy price shock resulting from war in the Middle East.
It is more a hope than will at this stage. The industry and government hope that the reduction in costs for food producers and sellers will be passed on to customers.
This is not the first time Labour has sought to reduce the weekly shop costs. Sir Keir Starmer's administration floated the idea that supermarkets might even offer some form of branded discounts, a suggestion that was met with collective bemusement by those in the industry.
However, there will be less paperwork, checks and red tape on food imports too, but they were already lightly enforced.
No longer will hundreds of pages of documents be required. No longer will perishable goods lose valuable hours or days at border checks. No longer will the incorrect colour of ink or Latin name for a meat disallow a shipment of British exports.
Some, though not all of the 24% hit since 2019 to the real value of agrifood exports, could be regained by a bonfire of this red tape.
In particular, smaller exporters of food will be able to return to the large EU market.
But the extra benefit here is unclear, and there could be extra checks and costs required instead on non-EU food imports.
The government's own assessment is that the economy will gain Â£5bn through this, or 0.14% by 2040.
Critics point out that this benefit does not include the quid pro quo - having to align with EU laws. Farmers have pointed to compliance with pesticides and fungicide regulation.
The classic example of the benefits of post-Brexit autonomy has been the development of precision-bred foodstuffs, such as Vitamin D-enhanced tomatoes.
The producer of this technology has, however, backed the deal after the government negotiated an "exception period" for ongoing UK sale.
Professor Cristobal Uauy of the John Innes Centre said that "aligning with the EU market will bring our science to a greater number of people".
Bringing all the agreements on food, emissions and the youth experience together, the economy is forecast to get an overall boost of 0.5%, or Â£17bn a year.
A modest boost, but high by the standards of past post-Brexit trade deals, for example, with Australia, India or Japan and the Gulf and the trans-pacific deal.
This UK-EU pact actually matches all of those agreements combined for expected growth measured by the Treasury's own assessments.
The biggest single boost to the overall economy would come from more European young workers. However, the economy benefit of this part of the deal per person reduces to zero, according to the government's own calculations.
Burham also said he hoped a planned electricity deal with the EU would cut bills, but that has not been done yet, and requires a contribution into the bloc's budget for poorer regions.
The electricity deal could help lower bills, but that contains a clear trade-off.
There are still some details to be revealed in the full legal texts of these documents.
The bigger point is whether this logic of gaining free-flowing access for food, in exchange for giving up regulatory freedom, will now apply elsewhere.
Is food now a blueprint for future negotiations? It had been the Starmer-Rachel Reeves strategy to spread this model to other sectors such as cars, and chemicals.
The big takeaway from today was that both the PM and his counterparts publicly acknowledged the end of the incremental piecemeal approach to aligning with the single market.
The question is whether the PM thinks what goes for food applies across the whole economy. Most economists predict that could lead to much bigger economic gains, but it will also be a much bigger constitutional battle.
