Stepping into a job like Mauricio Pochettino has done at Manchester United, where managers have come and gone frequently, will always lead you to wonder why the others have not lasted very long.
Irrespective of results, you face these first few days in your new post with suspicion.
So, while what happens in Pochettino's first game against Liverpool on Sunday is important, it will not define his early opinions of the club - good or bad.
Whenever you take over a team in mid-season, which I did on a few occasions, you don't usually get much time to work with your players before your first game.
Jumping straight in and going straight to the heart of a two-day preparation for a game, like Pochettino has done before United go to Anfield, is so difficult and I am not sure any manager can honestly make a profound difference.
A change of voice helps, I'm sure, but so does showing strength and confidence in your own principles and making sure the players understand you demand complete and utter commitment in all the games you face, starting with this one.
With time on the training ground being so limited, my initial work would focus on us being a team and working up and down the pitch together, by breaking down different scenarios inside the game and what we should do in them.
I'd keep the focus on hard work and making us difficult to beat - clean sheets are so under-rated.
Then, on the day, your first pre-match speech is always a massive one, and it will be the same for Pochettino too.
My first team talk would always focus on generating an 'us against the world' mentality. As a manager coming in now, you've usually got the job because the team has struggled, so you can play on that to motivate the players.
You can use media reports and stories to show the negativity against them - but then counter that by producing a real 'stand-up and be counted' kind of rallying call, before sending them out on the pitch, while mentioning your faith in them to succeed.
'What your own eyes tell you is most important'
I would have selected my team before I met the players, through my own knowledge and also with help from within.
Whichever club I went in at, I'd expect to know something about most of the players within the group in terms of their position and ability, but understanding the character of the squad was vital too.
The existing staff would know more about the lads' qualities in that respect than myself or anyone who had just arrived with me, so I'd always discuss that with the people I had inherited, from fitness coaches to the medical department.
It's what your own eyes tell you that is most important, however. Going into your first game gives you the opportunity to see for yourself what your players are made of - and it can be a completely different view from what other people see.
Most of the early games I managed would be hit and miss in terms of selection, but I would quickly work out the strengths and weaknesses of the group.
As I've mentioned in previous columns, one of my first aims at any club where I was manager was about putting out a team, not a group of individuals.
It does not take long to see when that is not happening. I can remember my first game of my first spell at Stoke, a 4-2 defeat at Walsall in the Championship.
I wasn't universally enamoured by certain crowd favourites in that team and, while any manager knows that leaving some of them out could have an adverse effect on the supporters' view of yourself, you have to stand up and make decisions based on what you see.
It is the same if there are other issues with the squad you have inherited too.
West Brom's first game after I was appointed manager was a draw with West Ham on New Year's Day 2015.
I sat in the stands at Upton Park with my son, Anthony, who was concerned how different the Albion team was compared to Crystal Palace, the last team I had managed in the Premier League.
He was right to worry about it, but we still found a way - a totally different way of playing, but still a way to win that suited the group.
All your staff have to understand your objectives
My initial aims after arriving at any club mid-season were pretty direct and also pretty basic too.
The staff I inherited did not just know the players, they were also the first group who had to understand my objectives and also my principles - like the work ethic I expected, for example.
Irrespective of what they were used to doing, it was imperative they followed my lead.
One of the clubs I arrived at were struggling at the foot of the table and also suffering from a glut of injuries.
In my opinion, the players were taking advantage of a very honest and hard-working medical department, who were quite accommodating on the treatment times for the lads.
I changed that immediately, so injured players were treated at 9am, 2pm and 4pm, with sessions in-between those times for strength and recovery workouts.
This did not go down well with the players because it impeded on their social lives and, in one case, a player whose injury record was not exactly brilliant came to me complaining that his wife expected him to pick his children up from school and being asked to be in at 4pm affected his private life too.
I did not budge, and funnily enough the player in question recovered quite quickly - as did the majority of the other injured players.
'Clear the treatment rooms, and make sure the players are fit'
Asking injured players to be at the training ground all day was by far the best way of getting them fit, in my experience, and the medical departments I managed were all working well past 4pm anyway so it was no hardship to them.
Just on that side of things, I would sometimes get the feeling some medical departments thought that, if they weren't treating players, then they weren't doing their jobs.
That was completely the opposite to how I saw it. I would congratulate people for having empty treatment rooms and applaud them for their work.
Treatment rooms are comfort areas that certain players would always gravitate to, to have a kiss and a cuddle if they are feeling sorry for themselves for not being in the team. That's one of the first things that has to change, because you cannot give your players any excuses or hiding places if they are not performing.
Similarly, I have always laughed at new managers who take over in mid-season and complain about the lack of fitness in their squad.
I am sure I have done it myself at some point, but then I had a reputation of managing extremely fit squads of players and it was always an area I studied really closely.
Early on in my management career, we weren't blessed with the advanced fitness equipment used in football today, but I was still able to quickly arrive at the level I believed the players should be at.
How? Simple.
Firstly, I had my own fitness runs that were tried and tested but, also, I knew that if during a fitness session they weren't moaning and sweating, they weren't working hard enough.
Privately, you can realise very quickly when and which additions to the squad are needed.
Pochettino will no doubt be thinking about that at United already, but he has 10 games in the Premier League and Champions League before the January transfer window opens.
In the meantime, everything has to be geared towards winning games. I always knew I needed to do that quickly, because it helps the players believe in what I was doing, which is important too.
I've outlined my principles I held at every club above, and they never changed, but my playing principles were determined by the quality of the group I inherited.
My big strength as a coach was relaying what I needed from every individual player in the team, and I'm afraid repeated sessions were the go-to remedy to get there.
I apologise to all the lads who had to put up with me barking out the same commands, day after day, until even our weaker links would remember their individual and the team's plays in and out of possession.
'Bounce or not, future recruitment is key'
It goes without saying that with United changing their manager again, their supporters will be hoping for an immediate bounce in respect of results and performance.
Michael Carrick's results when he took the reins at Old Trafford at the start of this year were fantastic, with six wins and a draw in his first seven league games - including victories over Manchester City and Arsenal.
As a consequence, he steered United from mid-table to third place and Champions League qualification.
Even if Pochettino delivers something similar then, for that success to last, he has to be able to have more control over the future recruitment into the club.
That has been an absolute disaster recently, and I don't believe blame for it can be thrown in Carrick's direction.
In fact, Pochettino should use what has happened as his argument for what needs to change to achieve his aims of bringing United out of recession and on the road to recovery.
Tony Pulis was speaking to BBC Sport's Chris Bevan.
