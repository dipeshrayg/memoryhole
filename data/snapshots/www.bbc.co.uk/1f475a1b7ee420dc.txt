Poundland has collapsed into administration, leaving the jobs of its 11,000 staff in doubt in the run-up to Christmas.
The company has appointed consultancy Alix Partners to oversee a sale of part or all of the discount retailer, with questions remaining about the future of the the company.
1. When will Poundland shops close?
This is the question that is most difficult to answer. In short, we don't know yet.
Poundland shops will continue trading for now and remain open as usual, at least in the short term.
Poundland collapsing has acted as a reset button on the process of selling the company.
Administrators are looking to either sell the business as a whole, or to break it up, the more likely outcome.
Before the collapse, Poundstretcher owner Fortress was interested in buying about 200 of the stores, and wanted to keep those trading under the Poundland brand.
But Fortress's position at the moment is unclear.
A source close to one of the bidders for the whole of Poundland when it was a going concern, Martin Property Group, has already said it is "unlikely" to make a fresh bid now due to "irreparable harm" having been done to the brand by the administration process.
2. Why is Poundland closing down?
That depends on who you ask.
Poundland's managing director Barry Williams believed Poundland was turning a corner and seeing a "strong recovery".
Although the chain was struggling when it was sold by Pepco last year for Â£1, in the year to September it recorded a profit of Â£18.1m, swinging back from a Â£68m loss in the previous 12 months.
Going into the vital Christmas period, first-quarter sales were up 3.3% .
The picture Poundland's management paint is of a retailer undergoing a turnaround until the owner, US investment firm Gordon Brothers, pulled the plug on funding for day-to-day expenses such as buying stock.
The Usdaw union, which represents shopworkers, said the owners forced Poundland into administration.
And Steve Smith, who co-founded Poundland in 1990, said "this shouldn't be happening really", adding that staff and suppliers have "worked so hard" to turn the business around.
Labour MP Antonia Bance said she had been "pretty appalled" by the conduct of Gordon Brothers over the past few weeks, and that it was going to make much bigger profits by breaking up the business rather than by keeping it going.
However, Gordon Brothers insisted the business was not turning a profit, and had missed budget forecasts.
A spokeswoman said while the management team had made Â£100m in cost reductions, the business had moved from a Â£70m loss to "around break-even" on paper.
"Break-even is different from generating the cash needed to buy stock, invest, and withstand increasing economic pressures," the spokeswoman said. "A few good weeks do not make a turnaround."
Poundland repeatedly missed budget targets over the year, the spokeswoman added.
Gordon Brothers was concerned that as inflation goes up next year, those pressures would have helped to make the business unviable, it said.
And Julie Palmer, managing partner at the insolvency agency Begbies Traynor, said Poundland's collapse "seemed somehow inevitable" and pointed the finger at challenges facing retailers - including higher costs to employ staff.
"There's plenty of other retailers that are feeling this cost squeeze at the moment, and this is the big name. But I think there will be another huge number of other retail failures as well," she told the BBC.
3. What will happen to staff?
Stores are still trading while the administrators work out if the business is viable.
The funds locked up by Gordon Brothers - at the last count about Â£70m - have now been released by the administrators.
This has turned the taps back on, so staff will continue to be paid in the run-up to Christmas, and so will suppliers.
But here's the catch. The administrators have a legal obligation to maximise value for "secured" creditors. It means the owners, Gordon Brothers, are at the head of the queue for being paid back the loans that were stumbling blocks in the negotiations.
But more importantly for staff, if the administrators can get more value by breaking up the business than by selling it as a whole, then that is what they have to do, even if the business proves viable.
If the business is broken up and its stores are sold off, the new owners would not be obliged to keep staff on.
4. Will shoppers still be able to buy from Poundland?
In the short term, yes.
From 26 November refunds for goods which people just change their minds about will not be given (but if the item is faulty you will still get a refund).
The immediate reason Poundland collapsed was that Gordon Brothers locked up funds for the day-to-day running of the business, including to pay suppliers.
This meant that stock was running dry at Poundland.
But those funds have now been unlocked, meaning suppliers can be paid, and supply chains can be re-established, at least in the short term.
If an item is on the shelves, then shoppers can buy it.
5. How will Poundland's collapse change the High Street?
That depends on what happens next.
Palmer said the administrators will have to get the best value for creditors, which could mean selling the business as it stands.
But if Poundland is broken up, administrators could sell the brand without the stores, or the brand along with some of the stores, or the stores without the Poundland brand to its competitors.
Some worry if Poundland disappears from the High Street, that is one less place that shoppers can find discount products. It is also one less chain providing competition and an incentive for other shops to keep prices down.
"Poundland is a place they go on the high street to get bargains and... given the cost of living challenges we face, that is of concern to us in government," Wes Streeting, the defence secretary, told the BBC on Thursday.
Poundland's customers, however, will likely turn to rival discount retailers in the hunt for bargains if the chain disappears from the High Street.
