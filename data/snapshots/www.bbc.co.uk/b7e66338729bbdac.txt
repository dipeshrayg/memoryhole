The meeting hasn't even happened yet.
But Thursday's mini summit of Vladimir Putin, Donald Trump and Xi Jinping already looks like a win for the Kremlin.
Whatever the outcome.
Think back to 2022. After Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine President Putin was an international pariah. Western leaders were determined to isolate him and his country over the war in Ukraine.
Fast forward to 2026: Vladimir Putin has secured an historic meeting in Shenzhen. For the first time in history, the leaders of China, Russia and the United States will sit down â just the three of them â for talks.
That puts Putin exactly where he wants to be: at the top table of global politics, rubbing shoulders with the leaders of the two great powers.
It allows him to boast that Western pressure on Russia has failed.
"We see such examples in the failed attempts to isolate our country, Russia, which has faced unprecedented sanctions pressure," Putin told a business forum of the Asia-Pacific Economic Co-operation organisation.
That was followed by a meeting with Xi Jinping.
But the Kremlin says the "showstopper" event in Shenzhen will be Putin's trilateral with Xi and Trump.
It allows him to play global statesman, even as the Russian military continues to pound Ukrainian towns and cities, and the Kremlin continues to reject the idea of an unconditional, comprehensive ceasefire.
One short meeting is unlikely to reshape the world. But it's the optics that are key. The picture. The photo-op. Three leaders, three big powers, discussing big issues.
In a way, Vladimir Putin will use this meeting to send a holiday postcard to the rest of the world. Instead of "Wish you were here", he'll sign it: "Look: I'm here."
But what of those who are not at the table?
Among European leaders, and especially in Ukraine, there is concern that the three-way meeting might produce more than just a pretty picture, though not in a good way for Kyiv.
Donald Trump has already said that in Shenzhen he wants to discuss his "favourite subject" - the war in Ukraine. The Kremlin would likely welcome an opportunity to argue its case to Trump and Xi without Ukrainian officials or European leaders in the room.
Last month Ukraine's President Volodymyr Zelensky expressed concern that the Putin-Trump-Xi meeting might resemble the Yalta Conference of 1945. That is when the leaders of the Soviet Union, Britain and the United States â Joseph Stalin, Winston Churchill and Franklin D Roosevelt - met to re-organise post-World War Two Europe.
"We really don't want this to turn into 'Yalta 2, 3, 4' with a redistribution of influence," said Zelensky.
It is unclear whether China would want the Ukraine war on the agenda in Shenzhen. In its official statements on the upcoming trilateral meeting, Beijing has talked more generally about the need to discuss "safeguarding world peace and promoting strategic stability".
In Shenzhen, Russia will make the most of the optics. But a diplomatic victory is not the same as a military one.
Nearly five years after President Putin launched his so-called "special military operation", Russia is still struggling to win its war on Ukraine.
Over this time, it has suffered huge losses on the battlefield.
And the Ukrainian strike on the road bridge linking Russia to the Crimean Peninsula â carried out on the eve of the summit - was a blow to Moscow's prestige. The attack severely damaged part of the multi-billion-dollar bridge built by Russia after it annexed Crimea from Ukraine.
The damage is not only a logistical headache for the Russian authorities.
It is a reminder that the Kremlin's "special operation" has not gone at all to plan.
The Shenzhen summit may give Vladimir Putin a big stage, and a bright spotlight.
It does not necessarily bring him closer to winning the war.
