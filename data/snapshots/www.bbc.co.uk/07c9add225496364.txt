Temperatures above 30C have also been recorded for the first time in parts of the tropical Pacific, while record sea-surface temperature anomalies and exceptional subsurface warmth have been observed.

El NiÃ±o is a naturally-occurring pattern, which happens about every two to seven years and tends to last for around a year.
However, the Pacific is also warming due to human-induced climate change and this is starting to mask the strength of the natural warming during El NiÃ±o.

Using that measure, the BOM's average weekly data - while only going back to 2008 - is significant as it now exceeds levels during the record-breaking December of 2015.
"We are currently witnessing an El NiÃ±o event that is unprecedented in the historical record," Dr Zeke Hausfather, a climate scientist at Berkeley Earth, told the BBC.
Strong El NiÃ±o in a warmer world
The warmth across the Pacific now extends well beyond the tropical region normally used to monitor El NiÃ±o.
Comparisons with previous major events in 1972-73, 1982-83, 1997-98 and 2015-16 show much more widespread ocean warmth across the Pacific and into the Gulf of Mexico.
Part of this reflects the long-term warming of the oceans but scientists say it is not the whole story.
"The strength of El NiÃ±o this year is spilling out of the traditional El NiÃ±o region," Dr Hausfather said.
The World Meteorological Organization (WMO) says there is currently no evidence that climate change is making El NiÃ±o events stronger or more frequent, external. However, it remains an active area of scientific research.
What is clearer is that El NiÃ±o releases additional heat from the oceans into the atmosphere.
Historically, global temperatures during El NiÃ±o years are typically higher than during neutral years.
Combined with human-driven climate change, that extra heat is expected to help make 2026 one of the warmest years on record, with unusually high global temperatures likely to continue into 2027.
No two El NiÃ±o events are exactly alike
Although the most widespread impacts are expected later this year and into 2027, El NiÃ±o's influence is already being felt across parts of the world.
The strength of an El NiÃ±o does not automatically determine the scale of impacts in any one location. Regional effects can vary considerably from one event to another.
However, some common patterns are emerging when compared with previous super El NiÃ±os.
During the 1997-98 event, severe drought across south-east Asia contributed to extensive peat and forest fires, while parts of East Africa experienced devastating floods.
The event also helped make 1998 the warmest year on record at the time.
A similar pattern developed during the 2015-16 El NiÃ±o.
Severe drought and wildfire activity returned to parts of south-east Asia, while southern Africa experienced one of its worst droughts in decades, triggering a major humanitarian crisis.
Another recurring signal is tropical cyclone activity. Strong El NiÃ±o events typically suppress Atlantic hurricanes while boosting activity across parts of the Pacific, a pattern that has also been observed during 2026.
The World Meteorological Organisation says drought and flood-related disruption is already being reported in several regions, with growing concerns over food security in parts of East Africa and the Asia-Pacific.
