In the Ecuadoran city of DurÃ¡n, riddled with drugs and rival gangs, Police Captain Jean Carlos Bustamante knows exactly what he is up against â not just the criminals who kill and sometimes dismember their rivals, but also the enemy within.
"We have many, many bad people inside our unit, inside the state, inside politics," he says. "We have many, many guys that are corrupt. They are in the pay of the gangs."
The only police officers he can trust, he says, are the ones right beside him - members of his hand-picked team. At 34, Captain Bustamante is the oldest among them and moves like the special forces officer he used to be.
With the sun setting, he leads his team into a hardscrabble neighbourhood where windows are sealed off behind metal grills, and lips are sealed by fear. This is the heart of gangland, controlled by a group called Los Aguilas (Spanish for The Eagles).
I ask what happens if local people speak out against the gangs. "They kill them," the captain replies. "So that's why they never say nothing."
He's heading for an abandoned house where he believes weapons have been hidden (gunmen don't keep their guns at home). He and his team line up, hugging the wall, heavily armed, and tightly packed. A battering ram gets them through the door.
They find two handguns â one rusty but the other ready to be fired - and ammunition for an assault rifle.
The captain says he's happy with the result. "The first reason is because we are safe," he says. "The second is that there are two guns less for the gangs."
But this is a small victory, in a war of attrition. He admits the gangs have "many weapons and much more money than the state".
He knows the risks only too well â having lost three close friends fighting the gangs. He has also lost his fear he says, "years ago", but his parents have not.
"My Mum says 'be careful, maybe you can die there'," he tells me. "My Dad calls all the time and asks 'my son, are you good? Tell me that you are good.'"
A line can be traced - in dollars and in broken lives - from the back streets of DurÃ¡n to the hotspots of London, and other capital cities. Around 70% of the cocaine reaching Europe and the US is funnelled out of Ecuador, according to the country's president, Daniel Noboa.
How did it come to this?
The drug pours in from neighbouring Colombia and Peru â the two biggest producers in the world. Powerful home-grown gangs â with links to Mexican cartels and Albanian mafia groups â help smuggle the cocaine onwards. They take advantage of Ecuador's weak border controls, underfunded security forces, and long Pacific coast.
In the process what was a peaceful tourist haven in South America has â in under a decade - become a killing ground.
Last year the murder rate was one of the highest in the world, with more than 9,200 people killed, according to Ecuador's interior ministry. That's despite an iron-fisted crackdown by President Noboa over the past two years. Critics say it has backfired. Top crime bosses have been arrested, causing the gangs to fracture, but also to multiply.
Many here believe the war against the gangs will never be won.
Captain Bustamante keeps fighting.
When we join him for another operation he's lying in wait getting a bird's eye view of the back alleys from a drone. He sees three figures moving around at the edge of a playground â "micro-traffickers" - caught in the act, he says. They are at the bottom of the drug chain, selling locally, in minute amounts, not kilos.
Within minutes he and his team have tracked the suspects to their door. The battering ram is out again, and soon they are in the kitchen, where a middle-aged woman protests, and a young girl bursts into tears.
The police move from room to room, by torchlight - there's no power - opening wardrobes and lifting mattresses. Upstairs, they find a hiding place behind a window. A young suspect emerges and is detained. Two others are handcuffed in another room. All three are led away, denying that there were dealing drugs.
Nearby the captain finds tiny paper packets of "H" stashed in a flowerpot. It's a highly addictive mix containing small amounts of heroin sold by neighbourhood dealers for $1.00 (Â£0.75) per packet.
One of the suspects is charged, and if convicted, he could get five years in jail. Prosecutors release the other two. Senior officers say that happens a lot. They blame corruption in the justice system, and the prosecutor's office.
"We imprison them [gang members] and they release them," says Colonel Santiago Gavilanes, deputy commander of DurÃ¡n police. "We know because when we carry out raids, we recapture them. Some of those who are released kill people and leave orphans behind." He tells us that the gangs have money to "buy laws, judges, and politicians".
We get an example of the buying power of the gangs from another police officer, in a different location. For his safety, we cannot reveal his name or where he is based.
He told us he was contacted twice by text and offered a massive bribe - "enough money to retire on". He turned it down.
Someone â a colleague perhaps â had informed the gang exactly where he worked and what he did.
"You never know who is watching," he said. We heard that a lot during a week in DurÃ¡n, and surrounding areas.
Back at the playground it's the end of another long day in body armour, but Captain Bustamante says he has to keep going. "Maybe, I am a little tired," he admits, "but it's my work. I believe we can win this war. We have to do it."
As we speak, one of the main gang bosses in the area has been spotted nearby. "We know he controls the dealers," says the captain, "but we have no leads or evidence, so we can't arrest him".
But police here insist they are having an impact. The murder rate in DurÃ¡n is down by half, they say, in the first six months of this year.
There are other indications of progress, they tell us, with streets no longer deserted after 19:00 local time, and bodies no longer found beheaded or dismembered. That used to be common, according to Colonel Gavilanes.
"We have managed to contain the gangs," he says. "Getting complete control is going to be a medium-term process."
Elsewhere the criminals are containing the police. Through a contact we arranged to meet a local gang on their home turf, about an hour's drive from the city. Their leader â whom we are calling Jonathan â told me he had four officers on his payroll.
"Whenever they're going to do an operation, they call me," he said. "They tell me to hide things, and to get out of here. When they arrive, they find nothing."
That early warning systems costs him about $1,500 (Â£1,100) to $2,000 (Â£1,500) a month.
At 37, Jonathan is just a few years older than Captain Bustamante, but he's across enemy lines. He says he joined a gang, at 18, to put food on the table.
He and his men (and boys) strolled through the rough-hewn streets, in broad daylight, brandishing weapons - including a semi-automatic rifle and a pistol. The youngest gang member we saw was a lean 17-year-old, wearing a bulletproof vest, and flip flops. All covered their faces.
Jonathan answered our questions inside a narrow room in a breeze block house, with his lieutenants crowding in. Most of the space was taken up by a bed. He admitted to being a drug pusher and a killer. "How many lives have you taken?" I asked.
"Well, until now about eight, I think. I don't remember well," he replied, with a handgun in his lap, pointing in my direction.
Jonathan claimed his gang didn't kill innocent people, just a rival criminal group trying to replace them. "They want to force their way in here," he said, "they come killing people, and we respond. The main thing for us now is to eliminate the other gang."
As we spoke, local people gathered near the doorway, children among them. Human rights workers in Ecuador told us boys can be recruited into gang life by the age of 12. Three years later they can be hitmen.
Jonathan makes no apologies for putting guns in the hands of teenagers or selling cocaine in the neighbourhood he claims to protect. He insists the drug trade cannot be rooted out.
"I think it's an issue that will never end," he says. "If they shut us down, another one [gang] will come along, and if they shut that one down, there will be another."
He expects to die, as he has lived â by the gun. "Death is paid by death," he said. "But if they come to kill me, I won't make it easy for them."
Two nights later, back in DurÃ¡n, we saw how gangs settle scores. The body of a man was visible under flashing police lights, lying where he fell, beside a motorbike. Bullet casings were still on the ground. Neighbours stood looking on, mostly in silence.
A man and woman held each other and wept â relatives of the dead man.
Police suspected a revenge attack â one gang against another.
Forensic teams lifted the body onto a stretcher, and into the back of a refrigerated van: violent death, on repeat. Another corpse was already inside.
A witness was willing to speak â but not to be named. "He already had a bullet in his back," he told us. "As he fell, they finished him off with a shot to the head. He was shot six times by two men on a motorbike."
"We are used to this now because it happens every day," he said. "You might just step outside and catch a stray bullet. You can't even go out or talk to friends or family because someone could shoot you."
He does not expect the president's fight against the gangs to deliver any change.
"Criminals are like ghosts," he said, in a low voice. "They pop up wherever they please."
Then he melted away, into the warm night air.
You never know who is watching.
