Hope is one of hundreds of workers bringing a discrimination claim against McDonald's. This week, a judge ruled against McDonald's attempt to have their claims struck out, a move that Hope welcomed.
McDonald's said: "Ensuring a safe, respectful and inclusive working environment across our restaurants and offices remains a fundamental priority."
The latest developments come after a BBC investigation in 2023 which heard that workers, some as young as 17, were being groped and harassed.
Last year, McDonald's staff told us they still faced sexual abuse and harassment.
Hope, who is now 22, says that her branch of McDonalds had a very sexualised, "toxic" environment.
She spoke of a time a manager came up behind her and put his hands on her shoulders. From then on, whenever she saw him coming over, she says she "moved quick" to get away from him.
"I got the hell out of there," she says.
Another time, she says a manager failed to protect her when she says she was being sexualised and objectified by two customers on a night shift.
Hope says she told the manager what was happening, but he told her to "just suck it up and deal with it".
Her main concern, however, was for the younger teenagers, who she says weren't being looked after.
"You could tell there were other staff members waiting for their 18th birthdays. That sort of like toxic culture where it's like, 'Oh, you know, it's not illegal now.' It was that feeling from quite a few of them," she says.
"And why would you? That is literally a child still."
Legal action
Hundreds of current and former McDonald's employees are bringing a discrimination claim against the firm.
The fast-food chain had attempted to have the complaints thrown out but this week at the London Central Employment Tribunal, Judge Glennie ruled that 450 cases can go ahead and they are now expected to be heard at the Employment Tribunal in April.
The BBC understands that these relate to indirect discrimination, not sexual harassment.
Hope told us she was not taking part in the legal case for money, saying they didn't know if they were going to get anything.
Instead, she said it was to try and "change the way they treat younger employees".
"It's going to change the way they treat allegations. It's going to shake up the whole internal process and make life easier for the kids who are now having their first jobs. She also accused McDonald's of trying to "delay the inevitable" by attempting to have the claims thrown out.
"And the judge quite rightly looked at them and said: 'No. We're not going to do that.'"
The claims were presented by law firm Leigh Day, which is acting for the current and former employees.
The claims focus on the vulnerability of young or female workers in the workplace and follow allegations of widespread harassment and discrimination across McDonald's restaurants in the UK.
Leigh Day partner Kiran Daurka told the BBC: "We're really delighted that the judge has found that these claims can go forward to a full hearing. It's really important for access to justice that these cases are heard."
A McDonald's spokesperson said they could not comment on the specifics of ongoing legal proceedings.
In a statement, they said the company had "embedded an extensive set of robust and far-reaching initiatives" as part of its "steadfast commitment" to its staff, adding that this work continues.
"Any form of harassment, discrimination or inappropriate behaviour is unacceptable and has no place in our restaurants or offices. We are confident that with the guidance and partnership of external experts, we and our franchisees have industry leading safeguarding measures in place," the spokesperson added.
