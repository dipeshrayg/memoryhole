Is women's cycling broken?
Is women's cycling broken? Or, more to the point, is cycling broken?
Unlike most other sports, teams do not see a penny of TV rights money - that goes to race organisers, such as ASO, who bring fans the Tour de France, among many other races.
This puts huge financial pressure on teams from the start, as they must find several sponsors for their income, and cover ever more costly ways to gain an advantage against rivals in performance and technology. Let alone consider paying a team of more than 30 riders and several staff.
If a team loses their sponsor, then their funding has gone. There's virtually nothing else to fall back on.
In the past two years alone about 10 British-based men's or women's 'continental' teams have folded.
Many in the sport believe ASO's market dominance holds the key to a more financially stable future in road cycling, by being part of wider negotiations with teams to allow them a slice of global TV rights revenue which would help the sport grow commercially to benefit all parties.
The 'one cycling' idea, as it has been known for some years, has been pushed by many teams and their leaders, but has never come to fruition, partly thanks to a lack of interest from the biggest earners in the sport.
In January this year cycling's world governing body the UCI launched a consultation period for men's and women's cycling on "key topics, including the economic model, the calendar and participation rules, fan engagement, safety and the credibility of sporting results".
