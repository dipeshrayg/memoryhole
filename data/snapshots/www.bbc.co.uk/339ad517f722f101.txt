National Insurance (NI) paid by firms on under-25s should be gradually lowered to help create more jobs, a major report will urge.
There are nearly one million 16-24 year-olds not in employment, education or training (Neets) in the UK, official figures show.
Reducing NI costs for firms starting with retail and hospitality firms - which provide almost 40% of jobs for these people - will help improve their opportunities, the report will suggest.
Collette Osborne, a hairdresser who owns two salons in Nottingham, said she feels "suffocated by employment costs" including NI and while she used to employ eight apprentices a year, now she cannot afford to take on any.
Her workforce has more than halved to 21 staff. Osborne told the BBC it costs "around Â£60,000 to train an apprentice before we can actually start allowing them onto customers' heads".
She said she has had parents so desperate for their children to find employment at her salons that some have even offered to pay their wages.
"I'm inundated with applications - and I mean inundated - which is heartbreaking because I can't take them on," she said.
Osborne does not feel she is getting help to offer jobs to youngsters.
"I feel like the government's foot is firmly on my neck," she said. "They promise breathing space. I feel suffocated, suffocated by employment costs."
The independent report by former Labour minister Alan Milburn has been in the works for a year and will be published on Tuesday in full.
He has already unveiled some of his proposals including:
- Reducing benefits - under-25s should no longer be able to claim the health element of UK-wide Universal Credit or personal independence payment (Pip), which is the main disability benefit in England and Wales
- A legal guarantee of a job, training or employment support with those who refuse to take up potentially losing their benefits.
- Doubling the threshold at which businesses start paying NI for 21 to 24-year-olds from Â£5,000 to Â£10,000.

However, for non-apprentices aged 21-24, employers pay 15% on annual earnings above Â£5,000.
This has been the situation since April 2025, after the then Chancellor Rachel Reeves increased the rate from 13.8% and lowered the threshold from Â£9,100.
Under Sir Keir Starmer, the government raised NI for firms but Milburn will say the Neets issue was not started by the increase.
Immediately axing NI for 21 to 24-year-olds would cost Â£5.1bn, according to research by the Resolution Foundation, a left-leaning think tank.
Milburn is set to say that an immediate across-the-board cut in employer NI is not the best use of several billions of pounds of public money.
But shadow work and pensions secretary, Helen Whately, said the government should "just stop doing the taxing".
Milburn told the BBC's Laura Kuenssberg programme he supported the government subsidising wages to encourage businesses to take on young workers.
"Because young people today are walking into the workplace often with a mental health problem, ADHD or anxiety, if you're a small business that's a cost that you've got to absorb," he said.
"The state has a responsibility to help them with that."
On Sunday, Work and Pensions Secretary Pat McFadden said the government would extend its subsidised jobs guarantee scheme for 18 to 24-year-olds who are claiming Universal Credit.
People who have been on benefits for 12 months will be eligible, down from 18 months and the scheme would be available for people in England, Scotland and Wales.
McFadden said the scheme, which guarantees workers a six-month job, "will help more than 90,000 young people into work by March 2029".
