Two Russian men secretly working for Ukraine's intelligence services have given rare accounts of operations they claim to have been involved with, that have targeted military assets and personnel inside Russia.
One of the men, whom we are calling Ivan, claims to have played a role from Russia in Ukrainian drone attacks on strategic bombers and munitions factories on his home soil.
He features in a BBC World Service documentary about the shadowy world of Russian saboteurs.
The documentary, by independent Polish journalist Marcin Mamon, also features a man we are calling Foma, a Russian living in exile in Poland.
Foma runs a network of undercover volunteers in Russia, that surveils high-level officers that the GUR - Ukraine's military intelligence service - is considering targeting, he says. He stresses they are never involved in the attacks themselves.
He was also tight-lipped about who they were surveilling, but after Lt Gen Fanil Sarvarov was assassinated in December last year in Moscow, Foma admitted he had provided the Ukrainians with intelligence on him.
There have been previous media reports of Russian sabotage groups, but it is rare to get access to someone operating on the ground, like Ivan, explaining alleged activities.
The GUR has confirmed that Ivan and Foma are both working for the agency. But it also has acknowledged that it cannot know the full picture. When Ivan's handler was pressed on whether he could be trusted, the handler replied: "I'm not sure of anyone, except myself."
And both the GUR and the men are cautious about how much detail they share, making it impossible to verify the activities the men claim to have carried out.
The broad tactics Ivan and Foma describe, however, have been acknowledged by the Ukrainian authorities. Kyiv has spoken of its strategy of co-ordinated drone attacks on what it claims are legitimate targets inside Russia, and has acknowledged it uses secret agents for this.
There have also been several political assassinations in Russia since the start of the war. As a matter of policy, Ukraine never officially admits or claims responsibility for targeted attacks, though a Ukrainian intelligence officer has previously admitted to the BBC it was behind the killing of senior military figure Lt Gen Igor Kirillov in December 2024.
It is difficult to confirm how many Russians are doing this kind of work for Ukraine. According to Foma, they number about 300-500, though this is impossible to verify.

Ivan and Foma say they are both opposed not just to Russia's war in Ukraine, but to President Vladimir Putin's regime - and believe his political future depends on the war's outcome.
"At the moment... I think the [Putin] regime might fall if they lose the warâ¦ Through our actions... we are trying to speed this up," Ivan says.
His work, he told Mamon, involved surveilling military sites, and locating places to hide drone components, in preparation for potential attacks on Russian assets.
"When these [drone] components are combinedâ¦ it turns out a formidable weapon, which with a single blow can destroy an object that costs many, many millions of dollars," Ivan said.
He sent Mamon a video which showed him apparently hiding explosives near a Russian military airfield.
If he had been caught, he would have been charged with terrorism, and jailed for decades.
Foma's work is different - he operates remotely, training his volunteers in Russia, online. Mamon watched him conducting one such tutorial - on how to trail a senior military official marked for surveillance.

Foma was once part of the Russian elite, so he claims to have a unique set of contacts and constant flow of insider information.
He showed Mamon on his laptop how he mapped military targets.
"This shows a database... the location of storage warehouses, and military units. Where generals liveâ¦ Where the pilots of strategic bombers who shell Ukraine live.
"In order to break the system, you need to understand how it works. Then you can aim for their weak points."
He said there were about "three dozen" people in his network split between Russia, Ukraine and elsewhere in Europe, with the contingent outside Russia providing logistical support, such as tracking targets' digital footprints.
Foma stressed that the GUR suggests the targets to be surveilled, but that he and his team check for themselves that these individuals should be considered military criminals.

But he argued that his team's actions were justified.
"I want people who do terrible things on behalf of Putin and Russia in Ukraineâ¦ to understand that they'll have to pay for it. I want them to get scared."
Foma stressed that he only passed on intelligence to the Ukrainians, and his network did not carry out any assassinations themselves.
Of the assassination of Gen Sarvarov, killed in December 2025, he later texted Mamon to say: "Keeping track of him took quite an effort. He lived alternately with his mistress and his colleague. Covered his tracks and constantly changed his schedule."
But Foma did not share any plans with Mamon and the BBC in advance.
Ivan, however, was tight-lipped about the work he had been involved with, even after the event.
Foma did his own digging on Ivan.
After checking with his military intelligence contacts in both Ukraine and Russia, he thought it likely that Ivan had been involved in a plan to strike Russia's largest military drone factory at Yelabuga in June last year, external. One person was reported to have been killed in the incident.
Ivan stressed that the aim is always to try to avoid harming civilians.
Operations have clearly been risky for Ivan too.
"The most serious case was when my car was stopped for inspection and I had the handmade explosives on me. The risk was very high - however as you can see, I was not detected," he said.
To mitigate the risk of being compromised, "there is no clear structure" to the work, he said, adding he had never met and did not know the identity of others he had been working alongside.
Foma has the same rule for his operatives.
"As soon as you start gathering people in groups, in general chatsâ¦ leaving a mark on the internet, with a high probability this whole group will be quickly detained or destroyed," he said.
Ivan stressed there were few people left in Russia suitable for this kind of work.
"Most of the ideological people have either left the country or are already behind bars," he said.
Many Russians involved in sabotage work on their native soil are therefore either oblivious - for example lorry drivers unaware that their cargoes have been compromised - or are individuals who have been paid, the BBC understands.
Ivan, on the other hand, says he is doing this work for ideological reasons - he used to be a member of the political opposition. As such, his movements would be expected to be monitored closely by the Russian authorities.
So it was a surprise that he managed to frequently leave Russia and return.

But later, the handler admitted he could not rule out the possibility Ivan might work for the Russians too, either voluntarily or under duress.
Foma thinks the fact that Ivan can travel freely does not necessarily constitute a red flag.
"There are all sorts of holes in [the country's governance], and if you know them, or are just lucky, then you can jump through the holesâ¦ and live in the system. Like a mouse in a piece of cheese," he told Mamon.
Perhaps more compellingly, if Ivan had also been spying for FSB, it would seem likely that he would share what he knew of Ukraine's attack plans with the Russian agency. More than a year before a devastating strike by more than 100 Ukrainian drones, Ivan had been telling Marcin there would be a major attack on strategic bombers on military airfields across Russia, though he never said when or where.
Marcin asked Ivan how he coped with the work.
"Fear is always present," he said. "It follows me everywhere. Even if you don't do anything, you're still afraid. This is Russia."
But he was optimistic that eventually his life would change.
"Putin won't live forever," he said.
