Nobody will be celebrating the fact that prices in the UK are rising at the fastest pace for a year.
Add to that an expectation of higher household energy bills and sharpening price increases and things look grim for everyone.
But there are some signs of hope that may take the edge off the rising cost of living. Here's how.
Energy bills forecast to rise by 26%
That headline is not missing a decimal point. The latest prediction for the increase in domestic energy prices in the new year is, indeed, 26%.
Millions of homes in England, Scotland and Wales are on tariffs affected by regulator Ofgem's price cap, which is set every three months. The next change for January will be officially announced next Wednesday, 25 November.

Not all energy use for the year is between January and March, but a big chunk is owing to the cold, dark days and nights.
How much you pay depends on your use, with heavy users of gas likely to be the hardest hit.
The government has promised a Â£100 payment for people living in homes in council tax bands A to D in England to help cover winter energy costs. Other nations are planning similar support.
The latest inflation data shows that government assistance can have an impact on the general rate of inflation, external, as recorded by the Office for National Statistics (ONS).
Domestic energy prices rose by nearly 4% in October, but this was offset in inflationary terms by a cut in VAT on electricity bills.
Supermarkets limit price rises
While the rate of inflation in general was 3.6% in the year to October, food prices only went up by 0.9%.
So why the difference?
It is partly the result of intense competition between the supermarkets. Like many retailers, they are facing higher costs but are not passing that onto their customers in full.
There may be some scars from the recent cost-of-living spike here. Shoppers suffered a period during the big rise in inflation in 2022-23 when food prices rocketed.
Those higher prices are embedded. While food prices overall aren't coming down now, they aren't rising anywhere near as fast as back then.
The detail here is very interesting. Take chocolate: world cocoa prices are down since 2024, and now chocolate prices are falling - down 2.9%, according to the latest data.
But the ONS has also pointed to cat food as one, albeit small, driver of the faster rate of rising prices.
Should high energy and fuel costs - which are driving the rising rate of inflation - persist, there are questions over whether the cost of producing and transporting food also goes up, and the chances of that higher cost being passed on to consumers are greater.
Economists have been closely studying whether there are any signs of those so-called second-round effects - where an initial price shock spreads to wages, services and broader consumer prices. So far, there are relatively few.
Food is, of course, a necessity.
If prices rise then it takes a bigger chunk of people's income and so has a deeper impact on those whose finances are already stretched, so the latest data on food prices is good news for many people.
The cost of borrowing money
One set of economists who will consider whether there is evidence of second-round effects are the nine people who set interest rates, which heavily influences the cost of borrowing money - such as mortgages - as well as the returns people get on savings.
The Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee meets again in December and, after that, in February.
Having just raised interest rates - for the first time in more than three years - to 4%, there's a widespread expectation among analysts that another hike is to come.
To be clear, we don't know if and when that will happen for sure.
The case for an increase would be if prices continue to accelerate. The Bank will want to slow that down.
The case against would be price rises remaining relatively confined to energy-related costs. Any easing of Middle East tensions could limit price rises without Bank of England intervention.
As far as wages are concerned, the latest official data suggests that rising prices are largely covered by rising pay.
That is an average - so is not true for everyone - but how long it remains the case may be key to how deep upcoming price rises cut into our personal finances.
