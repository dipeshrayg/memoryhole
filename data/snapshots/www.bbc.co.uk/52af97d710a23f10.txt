"Move on!" That's Theresa May's frank instruction to Andy Burnham.
She spent years wrangling with the EU to try to forge a decent Brexit deal before being shoved out of her job by her party. Her leadership was broken by Britain's relationship with Brussels. Now she is warning the current resident of No 10 not to risk the same.
In a rare interview, Baroness May - who presided over negotiations with the EU between 2016 and 2019 - told me Burnham ought to respect the public's decision 10 years ago, not "pat them on their head" as if they gave the wrong answer and should have "another go".
And she said she was "aghast" at what he was doing, dangling the possibility of rejoining the EU, warning it would absorb huge amounts of government effort.
"The hours that have to be put in, it's across the whole of government," she said. "It's not just prime ministerial time, [it's] cabinet ministers' time, officials' time, to-ing and fro-ing on those negotiations."
But while some on his own side are very keen to get on with a campaign to rejoin the EU - and the foreign secretary tells me this option is on the table - May is not the only voice urging caution.
Earlier this month David Cameron also said starting a conversation about rejoining would be "long and painful".
And former Prime Minister Sir Tony Blair has told the Sunday Times this weekend that Britain should only rejoin the European Union if it reforms and allows more innovation on technology.
Burnham promised a "brainstorm" on stage in Brussels yesterday, so what's his plan?
The prime minister's precise message on Europe is, maybe characteristically, not that precise.
From the by-election platform in the summer where he promised not to reopen old arguments and emphasised he was not proposing the UK considers rejoining, to telling the BBC the country could "go all the way" and go back in, to now the promises of a "national conversation" and "brainstorm" with the EU - his words have varied over time.
No 10 plainly wants to move closer to the EU, even if ministers suggest there really is no end fixed position and so this period of working it out is genuine.
They tell me that, in private, "Andy is pretty clearly where he is publicly - he does think it's a big discussion we have to have as a country, it's a national discussion in the context of the 10 year plan, but also has to involve the Europeans".
In other words, even though he makes no secret that he'd like to be in the EU and wishes we had never left, the prime minister won't conclude whether he'll promise to take the country back in until he has a better idea of what relationship he might get out of the EU - and, perhaps too, how the public responds.
Two quick reminders: Burnham has said it wouldn't happen before another election; and, remember there are elections in Europe too. This whole conversation might be transformed if Marine Le Pen becomes the president of France, or the march of other parties who are suspicious of Brussels continues.
The prime minister's body language in Brussels yesterday, though, and his proclamation that it was a "good day" for Britain, displays happiness in government at what they have achieved so far - closer ties on food exports, energy, and allowing young people to live and work more freely on both sides of the Channel.
Despite a last minute classic spanner in the works with a lack of agreement on tuition fees, the UK side feels positive they didn't back down on that issue yesterday, and the broader deals were concluded anyway.
And despite the protestations from the foreign secretary that the deals will all come into force whatever happens on fees, the government will start the legal processes to turn the agreements into law - although there are still plenty of opportunities for other countries to try and unpick them further down the track.
Of course, politicians' reactions to the deal depend which side they were on in the Brexit debate.
The Lib Dems, SNP, Greens and Plaid Cymru plead the case that it's not far enough. The Conservatives complain it's not fair, for example, that British farmers will have to follow EU rules to make life easier for those who trade with the bloc. Reform scream it's a betrayal of the 2016 vote.
But the feeling in government is the summit was a success and showed the EU will allow the UK to pick and choose where to cooperate, and is willing to listen to what the prime minister wants to do next. It's worth saying just as in any negotiation, the EU might always be up for giving if it receives in return.
Yet as May warns, doing deals with the European Union is simply, a long hard slog.
And one senior Whitehall source cautions the prime minister not to kid himself that an early feel-good factor doesn't mean future haggling would be a doddle: "British prime ministers can be oddly delusional about the EU - they can get seduced by the hope that good relationships with individual leaders will overcome the ideological inertia in Brussels which is overseen by French and German lawyerly high priests and priestesses.
"European leaders might actually be your friends, but they are not the ones in charge of decision-making in Brussels."
And a former negotiator warns that in his "brainstorm," the prime minister might well find the deal he's concluded this week just about rubs up against the limits of what can be done without going for the full-fat option - telling me: "There isn't much more to go with a strategy of incrementally building on the deal before it becomes quite a binary choice."
They add a reality check for any deal-making with the EU: "The EU have a very set way of doing things, that is more about their own unity than about who they are negotiating with."
Then of course, there is the politics at home. When cornered on this subject the prime minister and his cabinet often say the "British people will be the boss" - going on to cite polling that clearly displays disappointment with Brexit and a discernible interest in going back in.
But that's not the same as polling suggesting the public is demanding the government puts it at the top of the list.
As one senior Labour figure puts it, there has "certainly been a shift - and an openness to have the conversation" about rejoining, but the PM "has to be really careful about locking himself into a manifesto commitment that alienates a lot of people" alongside plenty of voters who might welcome a move.
What of May's warnings about getting tangled up in EU talks at the exclusion of all else?
One government insider from the time asks: "Can you even remember anything else that happened between 2017 and 2020? It's extremely naÃ ̄ve to think he can do all the other things that are extremely pressing, like defence, or Neets, while trying to play out a future with the EU - it won't happen."
It's worth noting, as some ministers do privately, that Burnham himself was not working in Westminster during the most intense struggles over leaving the EU - an advantage perhaps, that he doesn't carry that baggage, or a gap in his experience of how bad it was.
And yet, the prime minister - who, crucially, is confident in his ability to communicate with the public - is plainly resolved on exploring going back into the European Union, and choosing to take that argument on.
After what the government considers to have been a successful summit, that adventure looks more tempting to the many in Labour's tribe who think he is right to do exactly that.
After the last 72 hours, opportunity is knocking - but the warnings from prime ministers past ring out too.
Under May, Brexit affected nearly everyone and everything - trying to go back in under Burnham could perhaps affect everyone and everything too.
