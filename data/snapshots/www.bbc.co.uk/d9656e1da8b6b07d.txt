"It's just a hand. In the end, I'm still alive."
When an Islamist militant group threw a grenade at 11-year-old Ismael 'Ox' Oumarou and his friends in Niger, he didn't run away from it, he ran towards it.
He "just grabbed it", but while he was trying to throw it away from his friends, it detonated.
A promising taekwondo athlete who had taught himself by peering through the window of the local dojo, he lost his right hand in the explosion.
Everyone around him wrote off his sporting ambitions, no-one would imagine him one day at a World Championships, let alone representing the USA.
But Ox, who'd had to work since he was six and had been homeless at nine, has always known how to fight.
Raised by his grandma in rural Niger, he lived with about 40 other people and had been exposed to a lot of fighting as a youngster which he "thought was normal" and had been expelled from several schools.
His father was briefly on the scene when he was five but Ox says he would kick and hit him until he bled and also kick his mother.
Sent out to the streets of the capital city Niamey to sell water or anything he could to "make money just to be able to eat", Ox would go past a dojo to watch and copy people doing martial arts to learn "a way to survive" and fight back.
He began to dream of being like Jackie Chan and his kung-fu movie characters and began to teach himself some of the skills.
"I thought that, one day, I'm going to become strong like him. I'm going to go in a small village, somewhere far away from everybody. I'm going to get strong, then come back in my village, beat everybody," the now 25-year-old tells BBC Sport.
It was this, though, that led him to be kicked out of his grandma's home at the age of nine, with Ox saying he "started attacking people and fighting them in the street".
One day during a street fight a man approached him and said: "You have so much anger in you. I'm going to put you in something that can be useful for you."
It turned out he was a taekwondo coach, and so Ox began training with him, developing his skills and started to dream of becoming "the best at taekwondo in the world".
But then in 2012 everything changed in the grenade attack.
Ox says jihadist group Boko Haram, which kidnapped more than 200 schoolgirls from the village of Chibok in neighbouring Nigeria in 2014, were in his village trying to "take young girls and kill people" when they threw the grenade.
"I don't really know what I was thinking, I was just young and tried to get it away from everybody," says Ox. "But right now, I would do it again, even tomorrow."
Ox spent two weeks in hospital and left "with a lot of anger" inside him because of the uncertainty of how he would return to his new sport.
There were a lot of people, including family, who thought Ox returning to fighting after losing his hand was him "just talking nonsense".
"Who do you think you're going to be able to fight with one hand?" they would ask.
Perhaps they should not have doubted his self-belief. This was, after all, a child who when he watched his first taekwondo championship turned to his friend and declared he would one day beat the champion.
Ox tried to enter able-bodied tournaments but was turned away and began to think about stopping. But he loved fighting too much and eventually heard about Para-taekwondo.
"I didn't know anything about Para-taekwondo at the time, and even the people I trained with in Niger didn't know about it. I just wanted to fight," he says.
He used all the money he had to travel to competitions, sometimes having to sleep in the event venues as he could not afford accommodation.
He began to win medals, bringing home titles such as the Korean Cup and African Championship, and was starting to be seen as one of Africa's best Para-athletes. Ghana even offered him citizenship so he could represent their country.
But his aims were bigger. And, with Niger not having a long-term plan for him to continue fighting and Para-sport not being as developed in Africa as other places, he set his sights on the United States.
It is a move that Ox would not be able to make today, with Niger having been added to the USA's travel ban list in January 2026.
For him, getting the visa was the easy part. But he had no money for a flight and no way of getting it.
That was until a 3am knock at the door when one of his mother's friends - who had never met him - came round to say she would lend Ox the money to keep his sporting dream alive.
However, once Ox arrived in Washington DC in 2018, the reality of a non-English speaking, disabled 17-year-old moving alone across the world was anything but a dream.
He found himself sleeping on the streets and relying on other rough sleepers to help him translate.
Still determined, he travelled to Para-taekwondo tournaments where others helped provide equipment for him to compete and fed him when "food was hard".
Struggling in Washington made Ox look to other cities, trying out Las Vegas before heading to New York where he was faced with a choice between living in a house "full of drugs and guns" or sleeping rough.
He opted for the subway station floor and on the busy streets.
Desperate for work, Ox took jobs in security for less than the minimum wage and made an agreement to clean a restaurant in exchange for food. This arrangement was "really good" because it meant he could carry on with taekwondo.
He was still only 17, had seen people his age going to school and decided he should do that too.
Ox picked one and turned up, naturally raising questions from the school, which worked with the authorities to get him into foster care.
Living in a group home was "another tough situation" with a lot of "challenging people", but Ox says taekwondo kept him "disciplined and focused".
The director of the group home, Isabelle Farber, spoke French which helped Ox to adjust to life in the USA. They developed a good relationship, and she eventually adopted him before he turned 18.
He was hesitant about being adopted because he "didn't understand what family really meant at that time", but "she kept believing in me and showing me there could be more, and eventually she became my mum".
Then the Covid-19 pandemic hit, and all competitions stopped as the world went into lockdown, but Ox continued to train while in isolation.
Once the restrictions were lifted, with the financial support of his parents and through fundraising, he was able to continue training and travelling to compete - and in 2025 he became a United States citizen.
After finding a coach in New York and gaining his US citizenship, Ox could bring back gold medals from tournaments, such as the Swiss Open, the Spain Open and national events, to the country he now calls home.
He won another gold during the national qualifications to win a place in Team USA's Paralympics squad, and then beat a Paralympic silver medallist, Fatih Celik, during the USA Open.
Ox competes this weekend at the World Para-Taekwondo Championships in Muju, South Korea and is targeting the Los Angeles 2028 Paralympic Games.
To give himself the best opportunity of World Para-Taekwondo Championship and Paralympics victory, he travelled to Germany this summer to train with able-bodied taekwondo athletes and their coach.
They were hesitant for the Para-athlete to train with them at first, but he says once he arrived the coach realised "this guy can really fight" - so Ox started their intense programme, training three times a day.
"Becoming one of the best athletes in the world is a huge dream," says Ox.
And for him, LA 2028 is not just a chance to win a gold medal.
"It's a chance to use my story to change someone else's life," he adds.
