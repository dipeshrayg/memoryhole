Pupils to learn money skills for real life in curriculum shake-up.
Children will get a stronger grounding in the financial literacy skills they need to manage money, make informed decisions and succeed at work under major reforms to the national curriculum unveiled today.
From mastering times tables and percentages to understanding interest rates, payslips and mortgages, the changes will strengthen foundational knowledge while ensuring young people can put it into practice in modern life and work.
The reforms come as the government acts on the challenge highlighted by Alan Milburn's Young People and Work Report, to ensure every child benefits from high standards and a rich, ambitious education that connects classroom learning to the real world and opens doors to future opportunities.
Research highlights why financial education needs to keep pace with modern life. More than seven in ten (71%) children aged 7 to 17 make purchases online, with two-thirds (67%) of those doing so without adult supervision.
Yet only a third of children recall learning about money at school and finding it useful, while nearly half of parents (48%) believe too little time is spent teaching financial management skills.
As part of wholesale rewiring of the curriculum to be set out in the coming weeks, following research from the Initiative for Financial Decision-Making, pupils will learn the "big three" concepts of inflation, compound interest and diversification of risk, deliberately connecting maths to real-life topics such as interest, mortgages and savings in citizenship.
Rather than only learning how to perform calculations, we will support pupils to understand the reasoning behind them, articulate their thinking, and use that knowledge confidently. This could mean comparing supermarket prices, working out whether a discount really offers good value, calculating earnings from a part-time job or understanding how savings grow over time.
Education Secretary Lucy Powell said:
Young people are growing up in a world that is changing faster than ever, where spending money can be as easy as tapping a screen. But too many are leaving school without the practical skills and confidence they need to navigate adult life.
We need an education system that prepares young people not just to pass exams, but to seize the opportunities ahead of them. That means mastering the fundamentals of maths and English, while learning how to apply those skills in the real world, from managing money to navigating new technologies and preparing for work.
When children understand why what they're learning matters, they are more engaged and motivated to succeed. That's why we are building an education system that connects what children learn in the classroom to the world beyond it, giving every young person, whatever their background, the foundations to succeed whatever their path in life.
Financial literacy will take on a much stronger role in the curriculum, giving young people the knowledge and confidence to navigate the financial decisions they will face throughout their lives. In primary school, pupils will build knowledge necessary to manage money, judge whether a deal is really good value and make sense of the figures they encounter every day. Then in secondary school, new lessons will cover why prices rise and how savings grow, to spotting financial scams and navigating mortgages.
Alongside this, maths reforms will focus on deeper mastery of the fundamentals, cutting unnecessary content and better sequencing topics, giving pupils more opportunities to reason, solve problems and apply their knowledge. At GCSE, particularly at foundation tier, content will be rebalanced to strengthen the mathematical skills pupils need for life and work, including financial mathematics and statistics.
Rishi Sunak Co-Founder of the Richmond Project said:
I welcome the new curriculum's emphasis on the links between maths and financial literacy. I am glad that the team at the numeracy charity Akshata and I set up, The Richmond Project, has worked with the Department for Education on developing the new financial literacy curriculum.
The new financial literacy curriculum will ensure that no child leaves school without being taught 'The Big Three', the foundational concepts of compounding, inflation and risk diversification.
The Richmond Project is testing the new financial literacy curriculum in classrooms so that schools can teach it as effectively as possible from 2028. Our aim is to help all young people develop the skills and knowledge they need to make good financial decisions.
Sophie Pender, Founder of The 93% Club, the UK's membership organisation for state-educated people, said:
A life skill as essential as financial literacy shouldn't be left to your family and network to teach you. Not every child benefits from knowing people who work in finance or who have lived experience of things like applying for a mortgage, investing, or making their money work for them, meaning many start adult life on the back foot.
We also know that family attitudes to money can affect the choices you make. For example, when we surveyed state-educated people last year, 45% of working-class students said family or friends had questioned their choice to go to university because of concerns about debt, despite the likely increase in earning potential. Young people need to be empowered and educated to make that decision for themselves rather than relying on others' opinions.
Making sure every child gets this knowledge by putting financial literacy on the curriculum is an easy win for equality of opportunity, for the economy, and for the children who will benefit.
With young people increasingly navigating a world of online spending, digital payments and financial information at their fingertips, the reforms will help all young people become more confident in questioning the numbers and claims they see.
These most recent changes go hand in hand with the recently announced reforms to Key Stage 3 aimed at reigniting a love of learning and tackling the growing number of pupils switching off from school in their early teens.
