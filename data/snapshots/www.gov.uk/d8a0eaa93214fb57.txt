Minister of State for International Development speech at the RSA-UK Bilateral Forum Reception
Minister Kirsty McNeill's speech at the RSA-UK Bilateral Forum Reception on Thursday 26 November 2026.
Foreign Minister Lamola, High Commissioner, trustees, friends,
I am so moved to be with you today. From the outside this building may look modest, but the dreams dreamt here were anything but.
From 1978 to 1994 this was the London headquarters of the African National Congress and the nerve centre for the international struggle against apartheid.
We have just seen some of the extraordinary history held within these walls. The posters produced by campaigners here in Britain. The suitcase used to smuggle political leaflets into South Africa.
From this building, people organised. They campaigned. They built solidarity in the cause of a free and democratic South Africa.
From today's vantage point, it is easy to forget that what happened here carried real personal risk for those involved. As we heard on the tour, the building was bombed in 1982.
Apartheid relied on such violence for its enforcement, because so many people rejected the wicked lie that racism is somehow a natural or even desirable part of the human condition.
This centre will always stand as a rebuke to the racists. It will be a place we come to have our sense of hope replenished, because it tells a story of what can happen when people choose solidarity over silence.
The story belongs first and foremost to South Africans, and to those who sacrificed so much in the struggle for freedom.
But it is also part of our shared history.
People across Britain stood alongside South Africans in that struggle: campaigners, trade unionists, faith groups, students, parliamentarians and everyday citizens.
They understood that apartheid was not somebody else's injustice. It was an affront to our common humanity.
I wasn't part of that generation of campaigners. But years later, in 2005, when I helped lead Make Poverty History, I was there as a young activist in Trafalgar Square when Madiba came out of retirement to call on the leaders of the G8 to free those trapped in the prison of poverty.
His speech that day was addressed to the young people of Britain - and it was a challenge. He argued that we should never accept the idea that there are no great causes left - no fight for liberation worth devoting a life to.
And then he said something I will never forget - that "sometimes it falls upon a generation to be great. You can be that great generation".
I wonder each and every day whether we are living up to his challenge - and his example.
That is why this Centre is so important.
Because the best memorials do more than ask us to remember. They force us to ask ourselves what we will do with that memory.
Earlier today, Foreign Minister Lamola and our Foreign Secretary discussed what the partnership between our countries can achieve at the 14th UK-South Africa Bilateral Forum.
It is a very different relationship from the one whose history is recorded here. But I think there is a thread connecting the two.
At its heart, the struggle against apartheid was about human dignity: a belief that nobody should be denied their freedom, rights or opportunities because of who they are.
That principle does not belong in the past.
And it has particular resonance this evening, during the 16 Days of Activism against Gender-Based Violence.
Women were at the heart of the fight against apartheid, here in Britain as in South Africa.
The Anti-Apartheid Women's Committee was formed here in 1980, campaigning hard against the "triple oppression" suffered by African women - on the basis of race, gender and class.
They knew that dignity and freedom cannot be partial - that a struggle for liberation must be a struggle for women's liberation too.
Violence against women and girls is also a profound denial of freedom and dignity. It restricts women's choices. It silences our voices.
No country is immune, including ours. And none of us should accept violence against women and girls as inevitable.
Together, we are taking action. As founding members of the International Coalition to End Violence Against Women and Girls, the UK and South Africa are working to accelerate change.
And today, the Foreign Secretary announced additional UK support for work to prevent Gender Based Violence and support human rights organisations in South Africa.
We are proud to back those working every day so that women and girls can live with greater safety, freedom and choice.
And so as we work together on the great challenges of today and tomorrow, let our two great countries never forget the lesson in the history of this building. Progress is neither impossible nor inevitable.
It takes courage. It takes persistence. And it takes people willing to stand alongside each other when it would be easier not to.
So tonight, we remember where we have come from. But we also reflect on where we are going to - walking together as partners, equals, and friends.
We are forging a partnership between the United Kingdom and South Africa grounded in democracy, equality and human dignity. We will achieve great things together, and we will honour those who came before us as we do.
Next year, the doors of this centre will open to a new generation.
The best way we can honour the people whose stories they will discover is not just to remember what they fought for. It is to carry it forward.
Thank you.
