Historic move to dismantle leasehold and transform homeownership
A strengthened Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill has now entered Parliament, with new commitments going further and faster to help millions of leaseholders.
- Government announces new commitment to speed up ground rent relief for leaseholders, bringing in the £250 cap six months after the Bill become law
- Easier route for leaseholders to take control of their building now confirmed, with just a 50% neighbour vote needed to move to a modernised commonhold system
- New protections added to reforms will go further to protect homeowners against shock bills for major repairs, ensuring buildings are checked and plans made in advance
- Alongside a better deal for existing leaseholders, commonhold will become the norm for most new flats built in future after a managed transition
Millions of leaseholders will get stronger protections, faster relief from unfair costs and greater control over their homes as the government unveils a strengthened Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill in Parliament today (Thursday 19 November).
Ground rent relief will be prioritised for leaseholders, with a new plan to bring the £250 annual cap into force six months after the Bill becomes law, expected in 2028. This cap on bills could benefit up to one million leaseholders.
New protections to be added to the Bill will also tackle the shock of huge repair bills, requiring building owners to check their condition, plan for works and build up funds ahead of time. This will help reduce the risk of homeowners suddenly getting huge bills for big repairs like roof replacements or fixing lifts.
Alongside a fairer leasehold system that gives homeowners stronger rights and protections, existing leaseholders who want to take control will get an easier route to switch to a modernised commonhold system.
A revived commonhold model is expected to be up and running around 2029 and instead of everyone having to agree before a building can convert, just a 50% neighbour vote will be needed. This will make it far easier for homeowners who want to take control of how their building is run.
Commonhold means homeowners truly own their property forever, with no ground rent, no expiring lease and no third-party landlord owning the freehold.
For future homeowners, commonhold will also become the norm for most new flats following a managed transition. This will give homeowners, lenders and developers time to prepare for the new system, as a ban on the sale of new leasehold is expected to take effect two and a half years after the modernised commonhold model comes into force - around 2031.
Minister for Housing, Matthew Pennycook MP, said:
"This Bill marks a historic step towards dismantling the feudal leasehold system.
"Our ambitious reforms will give existing leaseholders a better deal while paving the way for a future where commonhold, not leasehold, is the norm for new flats."
The reforms will also:
- End unfair threats to people's homes: abolishing forfeiture and replacing it with a fairer, court-led system and repealing unfair enforcement affecting homeowners on 'fleecehold' estates.
- Cap rip-off admin fees: giving ministers powers to cap excessive permission and administration fees.
- Unlock cheaper, easier lease extensions and freehold purchases: including 990-year extensions, scrapping marriage value and stopping leaseholders paying their landlord's costs in most cases by fixing flaws in the Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024.
- Strengthen leaseholders' right to request gigabit broadband: helping more leaseholders access fast and reliable broadband.

Further information
All measures in the Bill are subject to parliamentary approval and Royal Assent. Timings for measures coming into force are therefore dependent on the Bill's progress through Parliament.
Since commonhold was introduced in 2002, fewer than 20 commonhold developments containing fewer than 200 flats have been created. The government is modernising commonhold so it can work at scale for new flats and existing leaseholders who choose to switch.
The existing forfeiture regime can ultimately put a leaseholder's home and equity at risk over unpaid sums as low as £350.
The Bill makes fixes to the Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 which, when commenced, will make it cheaper and easier for leaseholders to extend a lease or buy the freehold ("enfranchise"), including through removing the requirement to pay marriage value. Marriage value is an additional cost leaseholders currently pay when they enfranchise and their lease is 80 years or fewer.
A package of supporting documents was published alongside the Bill on 19 November, including:
- A Guide to the Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill, explaining the reforms and how the new system will work.
- The Bill's Impact Assessment, setting out the evidence and expected impacts of the reforms.
- The government's response to the Housing, Communities and Local Government Committee's pre-legislative scrutiny, setting out how it has responded to recommendations on the draft Bill.
- The government's response to the Moving to Commonhold consultation, confirming the approach to the new commonhold system and the ban on most new leasehold flats.
- Commonhold Implementation: Direction of Travel, setting out how homeowners and the property market will be prepared for the new system and the route towards implementation. A more detailed Implementation Roadmap to be published closer to Royal Assent of the Bill, to help prepare consumers and industry for a smooth transition to commonhold.
- The government's further response to the Strengthening Leaseholder Protections over Charges and Services consultation, including plans for mandatory reserve funds and changes around major works.
