Wildlife surveillance identifies isolated TB cases in wild beavers in south-west England
Isolated TB cases identified
The Animal and Plant Health Agency (APHA) has confirmed the presence of Mycobacterium bovis, the bacteria that causes tuberculosis (TB), in two wild beavers in south-west England. This is the first time TB has been recorded in beavers in the UK.
There is currently no evidence that beavers increase the risk of TB transmission to livestock, other wildlife or people.
The cases were identified through Natural England's established wildlife surveillance programme, which includes specialist post-mortem examinations supported by APHA.
The first case was detected in autumn 2026 and the second after a review of more than 70 previous beaver post-mortem reports collected in in spring 2025.
The cases are considered isolated and independent.
UK Deputy Chief Veterinary Officer, Ele Brown said:
We are reviewing the evidence for the source of infection, and colleagues in Natural England will be updating their biosecurity advice for bever release. However, at present there is no evidence that beavers increase the risk of TB transmission to livestock, other wildlife or people.
APHA Veterinary Director, Irene Cristofaro said:
The findings demonstrate the value of England's wildlife health surveillance programme, which enables potential disease risks to be identified, investigated and monitored by veterinary specialists including APHA and other partners.
This world-leading programme provides a strong foundation for understanding wildlife health and responding to emerging findings.
Natural England Director, Matt Heard said:
All beavers are health screened before release. Our licensing process is designed to strike the right balance: ambitious enough to restore this important species, but careful enough to co-exist successfully with other wildlife and farming businesses.
Natural England remains committed to beaver reintroductions, making best use of the licensing process that enables the benefits that beavers bring to a landscape while protecting nature, landowners and communities.
Livestock keepers should continue to follow existing TB controls and stringent farm biosecurity measures. Livestock access to watercourses should be restricted, with clean drinking water provided through troughs, reducing opportunities for cattle and wildlife interaction at water sources.
APHA is continuing to investigate the cases to better understand the circumstances of the infections and any wider implications, working closely with Natural England to review existing risk assessments and management plans for beavers in England.
Earlier this month, the Government formally adopted a long-term strategy, aimed at eradicating TB from England altogether by 2038.
