COP31 ends without deal as EU rejects final text for omitting mention of fossil fuels
EU member states on Sunday objected to adopting an 11th-hour deal that omitted explicit references to fossil fuels, the bloc's presidency said. The issue remains a major point of contention at the summit, which is taking place amid soaring energy prices and geopolitical tensions fuelled by the war in Iran.
COP31 President Murat Kurum acknowledged on Sunday a failure to reach agreement on an overarching deal at the UN climate conference after countries objected to the text.
"This text was not imposed on anyone," Kurum, who is also Turkey's environment minister, told a closing plenary in the Mediterranean city of Antalya.
"I understand that there is no agreement on this text. For that reason, these decisions have not been adopted."
The COP31 presidency proposed an 11th-hour deal Sunday that again failed to mention fossil fuels - an omission that deepened a tense standoff at the UN climate talks.
The last offering by summit co-hosts Turkey and Australia published deep into overtime left unresolved tensions over planet-heating fossil fuels but retained a much-sought goal to electrify the global economy.
The European Union presidency said it would "object" to adopting the contentious final deal.
"We will not endorse a cover decision... that does not even mention fossil fuels, which is the key driver of climate change," Ireland's Climate and Energy Minister Darragh O'Brien, whose country holds the rotating EU presidency, told the closing session of the conference.
The proposal needed consensus approval from nearly 200 nations at a closing session Sunday but they struggled to bridge differences during two weeks of grinding negotiations.
The EU and other nations pushed for strong language on transitioning away from fossil fuels, a call that faced resistance from oil-rich Saudi Arabia.
France called the latest proposal a "backward" step.
"Time is ticking and we are not there yet," French Ecological Transition Minister Monique Barbut said in a statement.
"The situation is difficult, and the outcome is uncertain. The oil-producing countries are putting up a fierce fight," a German official said.
A Saudi official declined to comment.
Fossil fuels are a flashpoint at the climate talks and the summit comes against a difficult backdrop of soaring energy prices and geopolitical tensions stemming from the war in Iran.
A draft deal unveiled earlier this week called for a faster phase out of fossil fuels but a second, pared-down version released Thursday stripped away all mentions of coal, oil and gas.
Threats of a walkout prompted Turkey and Australia to convene a hurried meeting between the EU and Saudi Arabia in a bid to broker a compromise.
The latest text introduced a reference to a previous global deal in 2023 to shift the world away from fossil fuels - but stopped short of naming them explicitly.
And it retained a contentious push to expand carbon capture and storage, a technology championed by the fossil fuel industry but criticised as costly and ineffective in cutting emissions.
"We are very disappointed with how the text looks," the environment minister of Antigua and Barbuda, Michael Joseph, said after leaving the COP31 presidency's offices.
"I can't honestly say that I'm okay with it passing" in its current state, Joseph said, adding that the draft was "quite silent" on fossil fuels.
Goal to electrify
The draft also called for global efforts to boost the share of electricity in total final energy consumption to 35 percent by 2035 and the expansion of "clean energy".
The summit had been under pressure to show progress in tackling soaring greenhouse gas emissions in a year of climate-linked disasters from Europe to Nepal and Indonesia.
Facing blowback over fossil fuels, Turkey and Australia pointed to "electrification" as a way to cut heat-trapping emissions and bolster energy security.
This works by replacing technologies that burn fossil fuels directly - like diesel cars and gas heating - with electric alternatives.
But some nations had demanded an explicitly "clean" target to ensure the electrification push was powered by renewable energy and not fossil fuels.
Around one-third of global electricity is produced using coal and another fifth using fossil gas.
Attempts by Saudi Arabia, India and Kenya meanwhile to delay the next set of reports by the UN's Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) have been another major source of friction at the talks.
The draft text "highlighted the importance" of these reports for an upcoming stocktake of global carbon-cutting efforts in 2028.
Dozens of countries across Europe, Africa and the Pacific wanted these assessments by the global scientific authority on climate change published in time for that crucial stocktake.
Countries also disagreed at COP31 over how to respond to the looming breach of the 1.5C warming target set under the Paris climate accord.
One of the latest drafts expressed "deep concern" that global warming would cross 1.5C above pre-industrial levels in the next few years.
The talks also jammed over long-running complaints that rich nations are shirking their obligations to help developing nations confront climate change.
The removal of a $120 billion target for climate adaptation angered developing nations.
