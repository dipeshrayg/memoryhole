How far-right Vox is winning over young voters ahead of Spain's snap election
Spain's Vox party is winning over many young people ahead of the country's snap election on November 29, particularly men. The far-right party is capitalising on the rising cost of living and anti-immigrant sentiment to expand its once-marginal electoral base.
In Spain, a growing number of young people say they will support the far-right, anti-immigrant Vox party in this Sunday's key snap election, according to recent polling.
One of those voters is Andres, a 19-year-old student in Madrid who told the news outlet Infobae that he believed Santiago Abascal, Vox's leader, "is undoubtedly the most logical choice" in the parliamentary election.
Andres is Catholic, believes that life starts at conception, opposes what he calls "LGBT+ ideology" and says he supports the idea of a traditional family.
He is among around 23.2 percent of people between the ages of 18 and 24 in Spain who support Vox, according to the latest data from the Spanish Centre for Sociological Research published in November.
That is just above the 22 percent who support Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez's Partido Socialista Obrero Espanol (PSOE), commonly known in English as just the Socialists.
That is a huge jump from 2023, when only 15.4 percent of people from the same age group voted for Abascal's party. The same increase has been noted among people between 25 and 34 years old, with polls suggesting that support for Vox has jumped from 15 percent during the last election to 22 percent in 2026.
This marks a turning point for Spain, as for the first time since the end of Francisco Franco's fascist government in 1975, the far right could join the country's government.

The centre-right People's Party, currently leading in the polls at 31.5 percent, has not ruled out an alliance with Vox.
One reason for this rise in support is because "this is a generation of young people facing significant economic challenges who have never experienced the dictatorship", explained Maria Elisa Alonso, a political scientist and lecturer-researcher at the Université de Lorraine specialising in Spain.
And because of this, they are drawn to the "narrative that blames immigrants for all the country's problems".
Economic insecurity
Young people in Spain are struggling to become independent. On average, they move out of their parents' home at about the age of 30, while their counterparts in the rest of the European Union do so at around 26, according to Eurostat.
One key factor behind this divide is soaring rent costs and hurdles to homeownership.
An average renter could pay around €1,170 to rent a 50-square-metre apartment in Madrid, according to the real estate portal Idealista. That is nearly half the average gross salary of €2,460, according to Spain's National Institute of Statistics.
Vox has promised to deregulate land use, lower taxes and combat illegal squatting to tackle the problem. But above all else, Abascal's party has blamed the left and immigrants for the high cost of living.
Immigration has become a key topic on the campaign trail ahead of the general election, spurred by the arrival of more than 70,000 migrants from Morocco in the Spanish enclave of Ceuta, in North Africa, in late July. The vast majority of those migrants have since returned home.
Immigration is a regular topic at Vox rallies. At one event, the party's leader Abascal denounced people he claimed lived "off the social benefits that Spaniards need and who have no business being in Spain". He added that "rapists" and "murderers" should be held "in prisons in their own countries". And he called for a "remigration" policy to, in his words, "reverse the migrant invasion".
"Vox's message is very simple," Alonso said. "There isn't really a political platform. Everything is the fault of immigration. They conflate immigration with crime."
"It's a narrative that resonates with these disillusioned young people."
Social media and masculinism
Vox uses social media to attract young voters, regularly posting short videos on patriotism, national identity, the traditional family and anti-feminist ideology on TikTok, Instagram and X.
A media outlet close to Vox has also been accused of spreading misinformation on social media. According to FRANCE 24's Observers team, social media users accused immigrants of election fraud after the outlet published an article alleging fraudulent registrations in a town near Madrid.
"There is a lack of critical thinking among some Spanish youth, a difficulty in discerning what is true, what is false, and what amounts to manipulation," Alonso warned.
There is also a gender divide among Vox supporters. While support for the far-right party has generally increased among young people, men in this group are more likely than women to support Vox.
Around a quarter of male voters under 35 intend to vote for the party, according to a survey by the 40dB institute published by Cadena SER radio.
Only an estimated 20 percent among women of the same age said that they supported Vox.
By contrast, around 32 percent of young women supported Sanchez's PSOE.
This trend is not unique to Spain, as female voters are more likely than their male counterparts to support progressive views in Europe and in the United States.
Notably, Vox has announced plans to push back against protections for women in Spain. For example, the party wants to dismantle a 2004 law meant to protect women who are victims of violence. The party also wants to cut funding for feminist organisations that support victims.
Vox also wants to roll back LGBT rights, challenging a person's ability to change their gender based on self-determination, which was established in a 2023 law.
A version of Spain from 'fifty years ago'
In 2023, more than half of men between16 and 24 years old said they felt that equality policies had "gone too far" and were now discriminating against them.
"Some young men see women as a threat to them and want to return to Spanish society as it was fifty years ago," Alonso said.
Abascal has also used stereotypically masculine imagery to court this group, posing often on horseback, in hunting gear or posing with weapons - symbols that the party often associates with traditions and rural life. "All of this resonates with young people who are also seeking a way to assert themselves and their masculinity," Alonso explained.
However, Spain's young people are far from a homogeneous bloc.
Hundreds of thousands of people, including many young people, have been protesting against the housing crisis since late September.
The movement gained national momentum after the eviction of Maricarmen Abascal, an 87-year-old retiree, who was evicted from an apartment where she had lived for 71 years.
She later died in hospital.
Over the following weeks, protesters' demands expanded to include purchasing power, wage increases and the deterioration of public services. Demonstrations culminated on November 11 with a general strike.
"Some young people are taking to the streets to demand better living conditions. Others, particularly young men, prioritise the defence of Spain and its identity over social issues," Alonso explained.
For the latter, a change in government is necessary to transform the country.
"They want to bring down the government and see Vox come to power," she said. The party has in turn responded to their sense of being "victims of the system".
