Did the US use 'blackout bombs' in Iran? Images reveal mysterious fibres at infrastructure sites
Photographs published by Iranian local media suggest that US forces may have used graphite bombs, also known as 'blackout bombs', against infrastructure in Iran in recent days. These weapons are designed to disrupt electricity supplies by causing short circuits rather than physically destroying power facilities.
On November 16, 2026, Iranian local media published photographs of 'suspicious' materials floating above a gas facility in Omidiyeh and an electricity dispatch station in Lali, two towns in Khuzestan province, southwestern Iran. The images appear to show thin, spiderweb-like fibres spread over the facilities.
FRANCE 24 found no trace of the photographs being published before November 16, 2026. However, due to their close-up framing, it could not independently verify where or when they were taken.
The incidents were reported mainly by Iranian local websites and independent military forums, rather than by Iranian authorities. So far, Iranian state media and outlets close to the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps have not reported on the incidents.
The fact that these reports originated outside official channels, given that some Iranian authorities have spread false information about the conflict, may lend them some credibility. However, this alone does not confirm that the reported incidents occurred.
The material resembles the graphite filaments dispersed by the American BLU-114/B munition, which was documented during the 1999 conflict involving Serbia, when graphite bomb attacks reportedly left more than 70% of the country without electricity. Its use during the 2003 Iraq War has also been reported.
The BLU-114/B is a specialised munition designed to disable electrical power infrastructure. According to a technical study, it releases submunitions that disperse large quantities of chemically treated carbon graphite filaments, causing short circuits in electrical equipment such as transformers and switching stations.
The impact of the suspected graphite bombs on Iranian infrastructure remains unknown, with no damage assessment made public so far.
However, Iran's national electricity company, Tavanir, has produced an instructional video for its employees explaining how to respond to a 'graphite bomb' attack. The video advises workers to shut down the power grid and wear industrial-grade masks, protective goggles and gloves. It also demonstrates how to remove graphite filaments using a specific chemical solution before safely burning the collected material.
'This is the first time we have seen what appears to be evidence of their use in Iran'
Although the exact dates when the photographs were taken remain unclear, Farzin Nadimi, a defence and security analyst and senior fellow at the Washington Institute, says the material visible in the images is consistent with the characteristics of BLU-114/B graphite bombs:
I would say it is not entirely surprising. These bombs are standard-issue weapons in the US military and have been used in several conflicts, including the war in Yugoslavia. However, this is the first time we have seen what appears to be evidence of their use in Iran.
There are two possible explanations for these photographs. They could be linked to the operation to rescue the pilot of an F-15 shot down in Isfahan province in April 2026, during which there was a massive power outage in the region. Alternatively, if the photographs show new incidents, the targets are likely to have been strategically important facilities, such as radar installations.
This would also be consistent with the locations reported by Iranian media. We know for a fact that the command centre for air defence systems in southern Iran is located in Omidiyeh. As for Lali, the other reported location, almost everything in the town is a military installation or is connected to military infrastructure, there are multiple important radar and missile sites near this town. These attacks have targeted strategically important parts of Iran's military infrastructure.
Since the start of the US-Israeli war against Iran on February 28, 2026, US President Donald Trump has repeatedly threatened to destroy Iranian infrastructure, including power stations and bridges.
In response, Iranian officials, including commanders of the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps and members of parliament, have also threatened to target infrastructure in Arab countries in the Persian Gulf region from which US attacks are launched.
