Estonia closes its border with Russia but can it keep Moscow out?
Estonia announced this weekend it would close its border with Russia, after blaming its eastern neighbour of attempting to sabotage one of its drone factories. But at a time when the internet has become one of Russia's most potent weapons to wage hybrid warfare, does closing the border even matter?
As of December 1, Estonia will close all its border crossings with Russia, Foreign Minister Margus Tsahkna announced on Sunday. The decision followed "clear evidence" that Russia's military intelligence agency, the GRU, had been behind a failed sabotage attack targeting a drone factory in the north the day before.
By closing the border crossings, Tsahkna said in a post on X, the Baltic country would "reduce Russia's ability to conduct hostile activities in Estonia and strengthen our efforts to prevent sanctions circumvention".
This is not the first time Tallinn has accused Russia of staging hybrid attacks on its soil. As recently as August, the country's foreign ministry summoned Russia's chargé d'affaires after another attempted sabotage, on another Estonian drone factory.
"Russia's aim is to make us afraid, make us hesitate and weaken our support for Ukraine," Prime Minister Kristen Michal said of the latest incident, noting Estonia had therefore decided to do "the opposite", and respond firmly.
The border closure would "send a clear message that attacks on Estonia's defence industry and defence capabilities are unacceptable", he said.
The question is, though, whether a closed border can actually prevent, or even limit, Russia's hybrid war on Estonia.
Anton Shekhovtsov, a Russia specialist and director of the Centre for Democratic Integrity in Austria, said he was surprised at the measure, noting there were no direct links between the botched sabotage operation and the soon-to-be-closed border.
Especially since none of the eight suspects arrested in the case is Russian: They are all Latvian nationals and crossed into Estonia via other borders.
On-demand operatives
As Russia has stepped up its efforts to destabilise European countries supporting Ukraine, it increasingly appears to be outsourcing its hybrid operations.
Elena Grossfield, a Russia expert at King's Centre for the Study of Intelligence, called it the "uberisation" of hybrid war, in which "operatives are recruited for individual assignments, often online".
One recent example is the attempted assassination of Bulgarian journalist Christo Grozev in the UK in 2024. "[They] were all Bulgarians accused of working for the FSB," Shekhovtsov said.
The incursion of an explosive-laden drone at Leipzig Airport this summer is another. The suspects are believed to have made their way to Germany via Serbia and Turkey.
In other words, Shekhovtsov explained, "with recruitment taking place online, through messaging apps, and payments being made in crypto, Russian agents can stay at home. They don't need to be physically present in the country they're targeting".

According to him, Estonia's decision to close the border "won't prevent Russian intelligence agencies from continuing to target Estonia, just like they have done up until now".
Grossfield, however, noted it may "complicate communication and the transport of materials".
But even though Estonia's border closure could mean that Russian sabotage operations in the country may "become more complicated, and more expensive to organise", it is unlikely to stop them altogether, she said.
The dangers of closing the border
Shekhovtsov said that one reason for that is that Russian agents have developed strong ties with organised crime networks in Europe and can therefore delegate the logistics of carrying out attacks to them.
The attack on a Starlink satellite communications ground station in Poland in September, for example, was conducted by a Turkish criminal network that had been commissioned by Russian intelligence services, the German magazine Der Spiegel reported.
But Estonia's decision to close its border does not come without risks.
"One of the consequences might be that Estonians who have family or businesses on the other side are affected," Grossfield said.
This may, in turn, make them easier for Russian spies to approach, and make them more willing to assist Russia. "The Estonian government may want to discourage these contacts," she said.
Shekhovtsov said domestic politics likely played a role in the sudden decision to close the border. While Finland decided to do so already three years ago, "Estonia has probably been looking for an excuse to do the same", he said.
In addition, Estonia is holding parliamentary elections in March, and the government wants to appear strong in how it handles Russia.
Grossfield said that the shuttering of the border sends a powerful political signal, but "doesn't prevent authorities from taking more targeted measures behind the scenes".
If the government response had been limited to only announcing closer cooperation with other European intelligence agencies, Estonian voters likely would have been angered.
Even though, Shekhovtsov pointed out, it is probably still the best way to counter Russia.
