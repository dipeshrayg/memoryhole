Trump administration will allow pharmacies to make popular peptides
Unproven but popular peptides touted for wellness have been available on the gray market, despite an official ban. Now, the Department of Health and Human Services is looking to bring them out of the shadows.
HHS said in a new policy that it will exercise enforcement discretion for a half dozen peptides, essentially looking the other way when they're made by small compounding pharmacies.
Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has been a vocal supporter of peptides, saying he has used some of them himself.
Peptides are strings of amino acids, shorter than proteins, that can exert powerful effects in the body. Some occur naturally and others are synthetic variations. Wellness influencers and some clinicians have promoted unproven peptides for a wide range of uses, though the evidence for their safety and effectiveness is sparse.
So even though these peptides have not been through the Food and Drug Administration's rigorous drug review and approval process, they could become easier to get. But the policy's focus on smaller producers may mean that pharmacists will have a tough time meeting demand.
In July, a panel of advisers to the FDA voted in favor of expanded access to six peptides through compounding pharmacies. However, at the same meeting the FDA's own scientists recommended against unwinding the existing restrictions on them.
The new policy says compounding is a safer alternative to illicit "research-use-only" peptides that are available online. They're often sourced from sketchy suppliers overseas.
The peptides, and some of their unproven uses, are:
- BPC-157, ulcerative colitis
- Epitalon, insomnia
- KPV, wound healing
- MOTS-c, metabolic health
- Semax, migraine
- TB-500, wound healing
Joshua Fritzler, the president of Olympia and Wesley Pharmaceuticals in central Florida, says people have been asking about these peptides for years. But because they were illegal to compound, his company didn't make them.
The new policy doesn't change their legal status, but it does create a path for small compounders, called 503A compounding pharmacies. It doesn't apply to the larger ones, called 503Bs, that are outsourcing facilities producing higher volumes of medicines.
So at Fritzler's company, only its smaller compounding facility will be able to make the peptides - and only in small quantities, he says.
And that has implications for consumers.
"We won't be able to meet demand," he says, explaining that Olympia will likely only compound one of the six peptides and needs to get its facility ready first. "I don't think all the [503]As in the country could meet the demand."
The Alliance for Pharmacy Compounding's CEO Scott Brunner wrote in a statement that his group is pleased with the administration's decision to allow the peptides to be compounded because it offers a "more accountable alternative" for patients seeking gray market peptides.
"It also brings patients into a system where pharmacy standards, regulatory oversight, and serious adverse event reporting apply," he says.
Brunner warned, however, that it may take some time for compounding pharmacies to begin dispensing these products to patients. That's because ingredient suppliers need to make sure their active ingredients meet the policy's requirements - and pharmacies need to verify that, too.
Several consumer advocacy groups say the policy puts patients at risk, however. The nonprofit Public Citizen noted that Kennedy seems to have acted unilaterally rather than through the typical rulemaking process.
"By allowing unproven and potentially unsafe peptides to be manufactured, marketed and prescribed without concern about potential federal enforcement actions, Kennedy has undermined the FDA and threatened public health," Dr. Robert Steinbrook, Health Research Group Director at Public Citizen wrote in a statement. "All peptide substances should go through the standard FDA approval process for new drugs. The HHS Secretary should support, not sabotage, needed FDA enforcement actions to protect consumers."
Steinbrook advised clinicians to talk to their patients and tell them about the risk of contamination and other adverse effects.
The Partnership for Safe Medicines, which includes various pharmacy and pharmacist organizations around the country, filed a citizen petition last week, urging the FDA to ignore the recommendation from its advisory committee in July to allow compounding pharmacies to make the six peptides.
"Today's decision leaves Americans in a grey area as to whether peptides are safe or not, even though the FDA's own scientists recommended against voting in favor of these substances due to concerns with evidence," its executive director Shabbir Safda wrote in a statement.
