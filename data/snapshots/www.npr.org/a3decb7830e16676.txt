Some of the most pristine ecosystems left in the world are found in the Pacific.
These remote islands and atolls are biodiversity hot spots, home to thriving coral reefs â¦

More than a million square miles of the Pacific Ocean are safeguarded as U.S. marine national monuments and sanctuaries.
Now, the Trump administration is opening some of these waters to commercial fishing.
Thousands of miles from California, the Pacific marine monuments are centered on tiny islands and atolls.
One of them is Palmyra Atoll, about as far from a continent as you can get on the planet.
No one lives here permanently, but itâs home to a field research station run by The Nature Conservancy, a conservation nonprofit. U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service staff also rotate in, since the atollâs land is protected as a national wildlife refuge.
For the researchers and staff stationed here, some days their closest neighbors are the astronauts passing overhead in the International Space Station.
For decades, scientists have studied Palmyra Atollâs unique ecosystem, a window into the vital role that atolls play for marine life across the Pacific.
âAtolls are small when you look at them on the map, but they have an oversized impact on the ocean around them,â says Alex Wegmann, lead scientist of island resilience at The Nature Conservancy.
âThey are very important epicenters of biodiversity and of abundance.â
Atolls are formed by ancient volcanic islands surrounded by coral reefs. As the volcano erodes and disappears below the water, the reef continues to grow upward.
Eventually a ring of low-lying islands forms, built from the skeletons of the reef that surrounds it.
For millennia, Pacific atolls were important stopover and cultural sites for Polynesians who crossed the Pacific Ocean on seafaring voyages.
Colonial powers reshaped many atolls, removing native forests to plant fast-growing coconut palm plantations. Although many were abandoned, the palms continued to spread, crowding out native trees.
World War II brought even more changes. As fighting in the Pacific escalated, the U.S. military remade many atolls, building bunkers, air bases and refueling stations. Ship channels were dredged through coral reefs. Some atolls were used for nuclear and biological weapons testing.
Still, Pacific atolls remain among the wildest places on the planet.
Seabirds use them as nesting sites to raise their young, returning to the open ocean to hunt for fish. Sooty terns spend years at sea, flying thousands of miles before coming back to land to breed.
The reefs surrounding many atolls are still largely untouched, with coral colonies the size of city buses.
At Palmyra Atoll, blacktip and grey reef sharks cruise the reefsâ edges. Scientists have found it has one of the highest densities of predators in the world, a rare example of a food chain that remains intact.
Pacific atolls also attract deep-water species that swim hundreds of miles, like tuna. In the warm, nutrient-poor waters of the central Pacific where food is sparse, atolls are like bustling cafÃ©s.
Scientists put satellite tags on yellowfin tuna, tracking where they swam for months, and found they congregate around atolls.
Seabirds rely on those tuna and other large predators to corral schools of small fish. As tuna feed, they drive the schools near the oceanâs surface, where birds can reach them.
This interconnected ecosystem, stretching from treetops to the deep sea, is one of the reasons the waters around Pacific atolls were granted federal protections.
