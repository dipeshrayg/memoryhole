So scientists working in South Africa's largest national park, Kruger National Park, tracked dozens of elephant carcasses that had been lying on the savanna for anywhere from eight days to nearly eight years, some freshly rotting, others skeletons that had been picked bare.
BARBER: We talked to the lead researcher, Courtney Reed, about this. She's with UC Santa Barbara and Princeton, and she said something unexpected happened. First, elephants create what they call cold spots where the diversity of microbes and plants goes way down.
COURTNEY REED: Right, like, if you think about an elephant carcass, when it initially dies - right? - it just flattens everything.

REED: It was really cool to see these, like, herds of impala and other animals come through and choose selectively to, like, go for that really, like, green nitrogen-rich grass right on top of that carcass site as opposed to, you know, the other grass that was available just 10 meters away.

ROTT: And their new study found that people who had the worst symptoms six months after a COVID infection had the least diverse microbiome in their respiratory tracts of all the people they were looking at, and that people who primarily dealt with respiratory issues had different microbial makeups than people with, you know, brain fog, for example.
