Google employees protest AI in schools policy
More than 550 Google employees signed a petition to allow schools access to Search without AI. Parents and educators protested outside Google offices in 6 cities last week for the same ask.
ROB SCHMITZ, HOST:
Parents and educators want Google to provide an AI-free version of the search engine for students. The backlash started after the company announced that they would not unbundle AI from their tools for education. Now some Google employees are joining in the push to provide AI-free access to students. NPR education reporter Sequoia Carrillo is following the story. She joins us now. Hey, Sequoia.
SEQUOIA CARRILLO, BYLINE: Hi.
SCHMITZ: So parents have mixed feelings, obviously, about using AI in the classroom. Why is Google taking such a firm stance on this?
CARRILLO: I think mixed feelings is actually kind of a nice read of the situation. I've pretty much only talked to very angry parents, and we have a lot of very angry legislators, too, at the state and at national levels. Why Google's taking such a firm stance? There's no clear answer. I mean, some will point you to the fact that Google's very heavily invested in AI, and they want schools to be just as invested in it.
And I actually spoke to some people who pointed me towards two court cases earlier this year. Google got those dismissed by arguing that AI Overview and their search function are one tool. And those dismissals might mean that breaking them up could potentially be legally difficult for them. And when I talk to Google, they would not give us comments. So who is to say right now?
SCHMITZ: That's really interesting. So it sounds like Google has sort of backed itself into a legal corner on this issue, and it sounds like Google's own employees are angry about this.
CARRILLO: Yes. So Google employees are now weighing in. A small percentage signed a petition to allow schools the option to use search without AI overview, and the union for Google employees says that they're worried that this could damage the company's reputation among students and teachers and end up with schools ultimately shutting out Google's products altogether. There is some merit to that. Earlier this month, we saw protests across the country outside of Google offices because of this exact stance.
SCHMITZ: So what are schools doing to prepare for this change?
CARRILLO: People are definitely finding workarounds, and if they haven't already found it, they're definitely working on it. They say something as simple as the Chrome extension might be able to help districts keep the search engine. Fairfax County, a very large, well-funded district in Northern Virginia, was actually already doing this. They're piloting a kind of ad-blocking-type tool for AI on their school devices that they developed.
SCHMITZ: OK. So if one school district in Virginia can do this, why is this an issue? Why doesn't everyone do this?
CARRILLO: It's a great question, but Fairfax County is a huge district, and they also are very well funded. We have a lot of small school districts in the country, and they are not able to develop their own Chrome extension in-house. And even if they could license it from someone else, they don't often have the staffing to keep up with the IT demand on something like that. A former Google for Education employee, Tracy Pizzo, now works with some of those districts to help them adapt to the AI landscape. And she said it might be time for something else.
TRACY PIZZO: Do I think that schools should be looking at where else they could go? Yes, I do. And I would encourage, at this moment, schools to say, we're not doing this.
CARRILLO: But of course, that takes time, and schools don't really have that right now. Like, school is in session, and the changes are already announced.
SCHMITZ: How likely is it that Google will end up backing down here?
CARRILLO: So some of the company's biggest fans - and I've spoken to a few of them, especially at the superintendent level - many that I spoke with feel like the company does listen to them and that this was just a failure in communication. Google said in the initial announcement of this on their blog that they will listen to feedback and evolve their approach to integrating AI in schools. But since the protests and this week's petition, and - there's really been no follow-up from them. So some of the company's critics think that this is an ultimatum, and they are throwing their weight around here.
SCHMITZ: So what's next?
CARRILLO: It's hard to tell. The change is supposed to roll out in the coming months, so we'll keep following it. For now, schools are definitely scrambling a bit as they head into the holidays.
SCHMITZ: That is NPR education reporter Sequoia Carrillo. Thanks so much for joining us.
CARRILLO: Thank you.
SCHMITZ: And a note - Google is a financial supporter of NPR.
