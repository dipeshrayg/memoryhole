Common advice about kid snacking is outdated. Here's what parents should know
Pediatricians recommend kids eat about five times a day: three meals and two snacks. Parents often carry snacks so kids can eat on the go. Here's what science says about snacking.
A MARTÍNEZ, HOST:
The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends kids eat three meals and two snacks a day. Many parents often carry crackers, bars and cookies around them so their young ones can eat on the go. Science journalist Michaeleen Doucleff looked into whether that's really a good idea.
MICHAELEEN DOUCLEFF: Back in the 1950s, when Kelly Brownell was a young boy, so-called snacking barely existed. Children ate three meals and maybe had one snack a day in one place - at home.
KELLY BROWNELL: As an example, nobody ate in their automobile. Maybe if you took a long trip, your parents would make sandwiches to bring along, but that would be about it.
DOUCLEFF: Brownell is an emeritus professor of psychology at Duke University. He says today kids' eating patterns look strikingly different. On average, American children snack about three times a day. They eat nearly everywhere they go, and most snacks are ultra-processed.
BROWNELL: So norm after norm after norm has been changed, and it doesn't mean that things have changed in a positive way.
DOUCLEFF: These changes, he says, aren't accidental. Earlier this year, Brownell and his colleagues published evidence showing how the ultra-processed food industry intentionally shifted family snacking habits. Ashley Gearhardt contributed to the new study. She's a professor of psychology at the University of Michigan. She says big food companies used a series of tactics to turn all contexts in which parents used to say no to snacking, like in the car, into snacking zones.
ASHLEY GEARHARDT: It's not just cake at birthday parties anymore. It's cake for breakfast. It's cake in the car after you went to the gas station. It's 24/7.
DOUCLEFF: Companies placed vending machines at parks, pools, schools, gyms. They launched marketing campaigns targeted to kids and designed special packaging so snacks don't spoil and are easy to carry around.
GEARHARDT: That means you can have a purse full of snacks all the time that isn't going to go bad.
DOUCLEFF: The result?
GEARHARDT: It's become so normal to just constantly be snacking, snacking, snacking. And certainly, it's a huge benefit to the Industry.
DOUCLEFF: Not necessarily beneficial to children. Gearhardt and Brownell say it's time to clarify what constitutes a healthy snack and how many kids actually need. Dr. Tamara Hannon is a pediatric endocrinologist at the Indiana University School of Medicine. She says the common advice that kids need to snack two times a day is outdated. It doesn't account for the rise of ultra-processed snacks.
TAMARA HANNON: The problem is what we know about child nutrition comes from studies of child feeding of not-processed foods. So fruits, vegetables - like, the foods that our grandmothers made.
DOUCLEFF: So, Hannon says, parents need to go back to giving kids these nutrient-dense foods.
HANNON: Snacks are really a piece of fruit or a vegetable, maybe with some cheese, a banana with some nuts. I mean, like, it's a very small amount of food, and it's seated at the table as you would a meal.
DOUCLEFF: It's not in the car. If you do give kids a processed snack, avoid ones with added sugar. And avoid ones with high levels of total carbohydrates, such as crackers, pretzels and puffed corn. The University of Michigan's Ashley Gearhardt says your body treats the carbs in these foods like it does sugar.
GEARHARDT: The flours and those cereal, starchy, savory puffs - they also, like, spike your blood sugar, too. And so I think it actually sets kids up to be in this kind of craving-and-crashing cycle as well.
DOUCLEFF: She says, simply stop carrying these high-carb foods with you, and kids will stop associating snacks with your purse. Gearhardt says, then your kids might do something surprising - eat more nutritious foods at meals.
For NPR News, I'm Michaeleen Doucleff.
