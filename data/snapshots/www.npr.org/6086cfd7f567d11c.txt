What does marathon training do to your body? A reporter becomes the test subject
Before running the New York City Marathon, NPR's Elissa Nadworny gets a bone scan, blood work and a treadmill test to settle a question from Mom: Is this bad for my body?
ELISSA NADWORNY, HOST:
About a million people will attempt to run a marathon this year, and I am one of them. I signed up for the New York City Marathon in November - 26.2 miles.
Completing the first run of this marathon training cycle. About 2 miles in, and I'm already tired.
This will be my fifth marathon. After my fourth, I said it was going to be my last. But how could I pass up New York? When I told my mom...
Hi, Mom.
JAN BRYDON: Hi, Liss (ph).
NADWORNY: ...She was, let's say, less than enthused.
BRYDON: I think I said something like, another marathon? I'm not sure that's so good for your body.
NADWORNY: (Laughter) Why not?
BRYDON: Well, that's a lot of pounding and a lot of stress 'cause you've got to do all that training.
NADWORNY: Yeah.
BRYDON: I mean, I know it's good to run, and that's healthy for you. I just worry about all your joints, and training for a marathon requires so much time and so much running.
NADWORNY: I couldn't stop thinking about this. What does marathon training do to the body? Yes, it's true - the marathon distance comes from a story from 490 BC when a courier ran from Marathon to Athens and then collapsed and died from exhaustion. I mean, was my mom right?
I guess my question to you is very simple, which is, like, is training for a marathon good for your body, good for your age?
SCOTT TRAPPE: Yes.
NADWORNY: Scott Trappe runs the Human Performance Lab at Ball State University in Muncie, Indiana. He's been researching running for years and coauthored the book "Running: The Athlete Within."
TRAPPE: It's going to improve cardiovascular health. It's going to improve your blood volume, your red cell mass, your plasma volume. Your muscle health is going to improve. All these things gradually improve over the, you know, three months or whatever it is of training.
NADWORNY: I ask him about my mom's worries about stress on the body or my bones.
TRAPPE: The adaptations that happen related to that - they're all very, very positive, but we can have overuse injuries.
NADWORNY: In one study in the U.K., a group of researchers did MRIs on marathon runners' knees. They found cartilage and bone improvements in the subjects' knees during four months of training. Yes, immediately following the marathon, there was worsening of the cartilage to the kneecap because running a marathon is intense. But this was reversed by the six-month follow-up MRI.
TRAPPE: So I think if done smartly, it's a very healthy activity.
NADWORNY: To prove that the science is on his side, he invites me to come out to his lab in Indiana for some before and after testing. So three months before the race, I fly there for my before testing.
