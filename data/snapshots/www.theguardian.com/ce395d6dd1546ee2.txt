The governor of the Bank of England has said he fears banks could attempt to pass on the £1.5bn cost of Labour's tax increase on their profits.
The chancellor, John Healey, used his first budget last month to temporarily increase the bank corporation tax surcharge from 3% to 6% for a 15-month period from January, with the proceeds funding "breathing space" to relieve pressure on household finances.
While the level of the increase was less than banks had feared - there had been wide speculation that Healey would reinstate the 8% tax level that was in place until 2023 - the Bank's governor, Andrew Bailey, said he would not be surprised if banks attempted to pass the costs on to customers to "protect their earnings".
"It's a market economy, so these are decisions banks will have to take," Bailey said in an interview with the Times. "You could understand that they will look to pass it on because they will be targeting a rate of return.
"How successful they will be depends on all sorts of things: market conditions, competition within the industry and so on. It wouldn't be surprising to find out that they will start by trying to do that."
Britain's biggest banks have been reaping bumper profits from the global rise in interest rates since the inflation shock driven by Russia's war in Ukraine and conflict in the Middle East.
Banks have profited by pushing up the borrowing costs on loans at a faster rate than interest rates for savers and hitting millions of mortgage borrowers with increases in monthly repayments. Together the UK's largest banks - HSBC, NatWest, Barclays and Lloyds Banking Group - have generated £200bn in pre-tax profits over the past five years.
At the budget Healey said he was raising the tax on banks to help fund an energy giveaway of £100 for 20 million households, helping to soften the blow from the surging cost of living amid the war in the Middle East. He said the measure would cost £2.6bn.
The increase in the bank tax - which partly reversed a cut made by the Tories - also came as Healey sought to repair the public finances amid damage from rising borrowing costs triggered by the Iran war.
The chancellor had faced intense lobbying over the measure. In the wake of the budget, the JP Morgan chief executive, Jamie Dimon, has said that he intended to find ways to offset the impact of the tax increase on bank profits.
Speaking at an event in London last week, Dimon said he would seek to claw back the increase and was "setting an example so that [governments] understand the trade-offs of these unfair penalties".
Healey's intervention came as households faced mounting pressure on finances. This week, the energy regulator for Great Britain said gas and electricity costs will soar by 26% in January, lifting average annual bills to more than £2,000.
House prices fell at their steepest rate in more than three years in October, as would-be homebuyers found themselves priced out because of rising mortgage rates.
On Thursday, the Bank of England said that 6.5 million households were facing higher mortgage costs in the coming years as their deals come up for renewal at significantly higher interest rates.
More than 400,000 UK homeowners will face a monthly increase of £500-a-month over the next three years.
Earlier this month the Bank of England's monetary policy committee voted to increase the base rate from 3.75% to 4%, the first rise in more than three years.
