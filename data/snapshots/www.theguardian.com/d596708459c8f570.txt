Traditionally, around this time of year, people remark that Christmas feels as though it's coming earlier than ever. In the BBC's case, it actually is.
Mortimer & Whitehouse: Gone Christmas Fishing, The Repair Shop at Christmas and other festive BBC specials will be available to watch on iPlayer from 27 November, up to a month before these shows debut on the television itself. This means you can watch Mary Berry avidly talk about the joy of eating mince pies, followed by a Christmas-themed murder in the Caribbean in Death in Paradise, well before the tree is up (unless, of course, you put up your tree earlier than ever, too).
The BBC is making the change to reflect growing viewer interest in festive shows in the lead-up to Christmas, with its analysis showing an increase in views, especially at weekends. It is also an attempt to counter a drop-off in interest in some Christmas programming from 28 December.
"They need a bigger window. Five days isn't enough," says Dan McGolpin, director for BBC iPlayer and TV channels. "People may be interested in watching on a weekend in early December or mid-December, so the BBC gets better value for money for the investment in that programme."
It is also perhaps an acceptance that the once-treasured festive television schedule is not the driving force it was in the past. "We have to modernise. We can't bury our heads in the sand," says McGolpin. "If we keep trying to do things the same way, things just get progressively smaller."
This shift will cause a fresh dilemma for the many people who can be found, pen in hand, circling the shows they intend to watch in the Christmas Radio Times - because you could turn on iPlayer and watch some of these shows at any time anyway. When the BBC made the announcement, "in the office, there was a sharp intake of breath and a certain amount of concern", says Radio Times's editor, Shem Law. Staff broke into huddles to work out what such a change would mean for their "legendary" double issue, which last year came to more than 300 pages.
When it became apparent that key BBC Christmas shows, from the Strictly Christmas special to Amandaland and the new Call the Midwife prequel Sisters in Arms, were to air first on television as usual, it felt less consequential. After all, it's not like everyone is suddenly going to start streaming festive shows the moment they're available on iPlayer. "I have yet to meet anyone that binges Christmas specials," says Law.
Significant Christmas shows have been ring-fenced for television, says McGolpin, especially those that feature "big moments to bring the nation together". Choosing which specials to air from the end of November is "more of an art than a science", he says. New dramas such as A Tale of Two Cities (starring Kit Harington and François Civil), which will air after Christmas, have been held back, along with shows that cannot go up early because they aren't yet ready.
To ease concerns that releasing festive treats early on the iPlayer would cannibalise the ratings when they are shown on television, McGolpin says that they "haven't found much of that at all". Previous experiments where the BBC released shows on iPlayer first found that ratings were not adversely affected when the same shows appeared on "linear TV" (the new name for traditional live broadcasting), as linear and streaming are "almost different audiences". He adds: "We're not expecting that everyone's going to rush out on the day we release everything and watch every programme."
"I think it's a bit of a shame," says Law. He has "a huge amount of sympathy" for the BBC's need to reflect changing audience tastes, with some festive shows having a limited shelf life. Yet "being locked in a room with your family for four or five days is hell on earth - and the only thing that really alleviates that is occasionally you get to watch some television together, and the rows stop, and everybody's enjoying it," says Law. "Somebody saying, 'Oh, I watched that three days ago' or, 'Let's watch it on iPlayer' - that's not quite the same."
"It's indicative of the times we live in," Law adds. "And yeah, I think it is inevitable."
Early festive TV treats
A Ghost Story for Christmas: The Bride's Chamber
A Mark Gatiss adaptation of an unsettling famous ghost story is an annual Christmas Eve tradition on BBC Two. This year's adaptation of a Charles Dickens tale will be available to view 27 days earlier.
The Mandy Who Knew Too Much
Diane Morgan's absurdist comedy creation, Mandy, was supposed to air as a Christmas special last year, only for the programme to be pulled by the BBC days before transmission with little explanation. Now, more than 11 months on, we will be able to see what the fuss was about.
Mary Berry's Christmas Countdown
Who needs Top of the Pops Christmas when you have Mary Berry revealing (and eating) some of the nation's favourite festive foods, alongside Miranda Hart and Greg James? Will a mince pie be at No 1?
Mortimer & Whitehouse: Gone Christmas Fishing
It wouldn't be Christmas without Bob Mortimer and Paul Whitehouse pretending to celebrate months before the actual day. In their latest special, they are joined by Steve Coogan and Glastonbury 2027 headliner Sam Fender. There's also an emotional tribute to their much-missed terrier, Ted.
Death in Paradise Christmas Special
The best way to enjoy Christmas? Solving the brutal murder of a notable patriarch on his private island in the Caribbean, of course! Police detectives Seb (Shaquille Ali-Yebuah) and Mattie (Catherine Garton) are dispatched to solve the case. Don't worry: there is some tinsel.
