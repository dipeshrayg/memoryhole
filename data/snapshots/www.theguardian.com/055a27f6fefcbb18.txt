You're probably sick of hearing how You Can See Everything, about the disgraced Theranos founder Elizabeth Holmes, is the buzziest, coolest, 100% freshest film of the year, but coming out of the screening last week, I couldn't help but wonder whether Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim had been watching a lot of 90s Iranian cinema. And one film in particular: Close-Up, by Abbas Kiarostami.
If it feels as if You Can See Everything blends truth, lies, fiction and documentary in mind-bending new ways until you're no longer sure what the hell you're watching, then Kiarostami, who died in 2016, practically invented this mode 36 years ago. The context could not be more different - analogue 90s Tehran rather than tech-drunk 2020s California - but the themes are the same: Close-Up is a strange story of impersonation, deception and performance, made even stranger by the film-maker's deliberate meddling.
The setup contains some uncanny parallels. Like You Can See Everything, Close-Up focuses on a convicted fraudster: Hossein Sabzian is an unemployed loner who is charged with impersonating one of Iran's greatest film-makers, Mohsen Makhmalbaf. Posing as Makhmalbaf, Sabzian convinces a family that he's going to make his next movie in their well-to-do home, and will even cast their sons in it. This is pre-internet, so the family can't just Google what Makhmalbaf really looks like.
Sabzian is soon caught out and arrested - but, like Fielder, rather than maintain the arm's-length objectivity of the documentarian, Kiarostami mischievously inserts himself into the narrative. We see him visit Sabzian in prison then, with a latitude Fielder and Oppenheim can only dream of, he gains permission to film Sabzian's trial.
The trial, a much looser affair than a western one, is gripping in itself. Sabzian's justification for his deception is almost as bizarre as Holmes's. He is not a conman, he insists, he's a cinephile, an artist. He was committing to the role of playing Makhmalbaf, and the subterfuge earned him a respect he could never achieve as his lowly self. He's a persuasive speaker in the courtroom, but is this heartfelt testimony or masterful improv? The family aren't convinced: "He's still playing a role, even if a slightly different one," the son protests. "Instead of playing Makhmalbaf, now he's playing the sensitive soul."
Kiarostami confounds things even further by intercutting the courtroom drama with reconstructed flashback scenes from this bizarre affair, with Sabzian and the family playing themselves, in their actual home. There are also apparently staged scenes with actors - the comically inept journalist who broke the story, for example. Notions of fact and fiction are cast adrift. Can we take anything we're watching at face value? Is Sabzian a documentary subject or an actor, playing a director? Or perhaps a screenwriter? Kiarostami is, in effect, taking the fantasy Sabzian concocted and making it real. Even Kiarostami wasn't sure what he'd made. Looking back on Close-Up in 2005, he said: "I wasn't directing the actors so much as being directed by them."
The similarities don't stop there. You Can See Everything's extreme closeups, for example - could they have been inspired by a movie called Close-Up? Kiarostami even explains to Sabzian (and the viewers) at one point that he is using a wide-angle lens to film the courtroom, and a different closeup lens just for him, inviting us to scrutinise Sabzian's face in minute detail, just as Fielder does with Holmes.
And the fantastically awkward reality-folding-in-on-itself moment in Fielder's film when Amanda Seyfried finds herself on the phone to the real Elizabeth Holmes is mirrored in Close-Up: the film concludes with a surprisingly emotional encounter between Sabzian and the real Mohsen Makhmalbaf (although Kiarostami later revealed that elements of this were also staged- make it stop!).
Has Fielder seen Close-Up? It's more than likely. Lance Oppenheim certainly has. In an interview last month he described it as "a favourite movie of mine". In a separate interview he said: "The two movies that made me want to make movies were Orson Welles' F for Fake and Abbas Kiarostami's Close-Up." He's not the only one: in 2023, Fielder's sometime collaborators Benny and Josh Safdie judged it among their top five films of all time. "It made me think about using movies to manipulate life in its own way," Josh said. "You're using the real people to recreate, and then by recreating you're understanding everything that happened in the first place, and then it's a movie, so you really have no idea what to believe."
Fielder has been operating in this territory for some time, not just with You Can See Everything but also with his other genre-meddling works, Nathan for You and The Rehearsal, both of which also play brilliantly with reconstruction, artifice and meta-performance.
You Can See Everything is by no means a rip-off of Close-Up. For one thing, unlike Fielder, Kiarostami doesn't put himself in the picture, leering though the window like a stalker. And the whole vibe is very different. But what's so bracing about both films is that sensation of destabilising uncertainty, of not really knowing what we're watching. Is anyone being real with anyone? What makes reality "real" anyway? In our time of genre fatigue at the cinema but deepfakery and misinformation online, this mindwarping, hall-of-mirrors mode of cinema is clearly connecting with audiences, even when we think we've see it all before.
