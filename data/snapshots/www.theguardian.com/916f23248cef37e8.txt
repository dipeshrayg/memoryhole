I met Meg at a nude life-drawing class. I was struck by how free she seemed and how powerfully that came through in her modelling. I asked if I could photograph her for my project Body Like Mine. This series explores the difference between how our bodies are perceived externally and how we experience them ourselves. We see a body and we bring our own judgments and assumptions on to it, but the lived experience is very different.
Rather than portraying an idealised body, the project aims to explore what it feels like to inhabit your own body, and to see it as worthy of attention without asking it to become anything else first.
My photography is collaborative. I brought some visual ideas to the shoot, and Meg brought her own ways of moving and expressing herself. The photograph developed between us. I took references from art history: Jenny Saville's nudes and Lucian Freud's work have inspired me, along with the sense of flesh and physical presence in Rubens' paintings.
I lit her in a painterly way and used a canvas backdrop. As I was photographing her, I found myself getting lower down. I wanted the body to have a real presence in the photograph, to become the portrait itself. It was Meg's idea to scrunch her belly in that way. I asked her to look away so that her face wouldn't be the first place your eye went. I love how fleshy the image feels. Portraits often rely on the face and eyes to communicate who someone is. Here, I wanted to explore how much of Meg's presence and personality could come through her body and gesture.
Meg loved it and was delighted when the image was selected to be displayed in the National Portrait Gallery. Her nude modelling is only a small part of what she does - most of her time and energies go into raising awareness about overconsumption and the "tat" that fills our lives and ends up in landfill. She's a superstar on TikTok!
"Fat women's femininity is [often] stripped from them and ... I am masculinised and called brave for just existing in this body. When I am life modelling, I feel the most feminine and deeply in touch with myself. I am proud to take up space and represent people who often are not," she says.
My commissioned work specialises in boudoir and nude photography. Coming to this has been a personal journey. I was very critical of my body - I was one of those women who couldn't look in a mirror. For me, being behind the camera was also a way of avoiding being in photographs. I liked having that control. I started out doing headshots, but when you run your own business, you need to be visible. I became frustrated with all the negative feelings I had about myself, and one day I stripped off and I took my own photographs of my nude body. And when I looked over the images I was shocked to realise that I didn't hate them. In fact I thought they were beautiful.
And then I noticed a change in myself. I was walking down the street taller. I wasn't wearing cardigans to hide my arms. And I realised that maybe I can help others. I don't think a photograph can resolve someone's relationship with their body, but it can be one part of seeing themselves differently, feeling more connected to their body, or recognising that they are worthy of being seen.
But there is a tension in my work between beauty standards and how women want to be seen. Much of the boudoir photography I do sits in that culture that shapes how women think they should look and what makes a body beautiful or desirable. But who decides what makes a body either of these things? Women's bodies don't exist to meet someone else's expectations of beauty or desirability, which change over time. There is room for wanting to feel sexy, but whose idea of sexy are we working towards?
When I make or look at a nude photograph, I ask what the pose, light, colour and composition are communicating. Whose perspective is shaping the image, and how much say does the person being photographed have in how they are seen?
With Body Like Mine, I want to give more space to people's own accounts of living in their bodies, particularly where those experiences differ from what others assume when they look at them. The next person I'm photographing lives with endometriosis, and another participant is experiencing memory loss. I want us to explore how those experiences might be expressed in a photograph, guided by what each person wants to share.
Before we photograph, I ask each person to choose a few words that describe what they would like us to express together. Those words give us a starting point for the images. Meg's were "real", "joyful" and "free".

Top tip: Talk through boundaries before artistic nude or boudoir photoshoots, and keep checking in as you work. Make it easy for someone to change their mind, and be ready to adapt a pose, change direction or put the camera down.
