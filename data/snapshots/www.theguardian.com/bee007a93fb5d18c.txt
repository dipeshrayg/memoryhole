Wall Street shakes with swings for Nvidia and oil prices but recovers most of its early losses
The US stock market steadied itself on Thursday after absorbing big swings for Nvidia's stock and oil prices.
The moves suggested Wall Street sees limited fallout for the artificial intelligence industry, which carries immense influence on the market, despite the sudden fall of one of its biggest evangelists, the Associated Press notes.
After dropping nearly 1% at the start of trading, the S+P 500 erased all of the loss and then drifted between small gains and losses before dipping 0.3%. The Dow Jones Industrial Average was down 88 points, or 0.2%, with an hour remaining in trading, while the Nasdaq composite was down 0.5% after trimming an early drop of 1.6%.
At the center of the swings was Nvidia's stock. It plunged 14% at the start of trading following the resignation of Jensen Huang, its CEO, co-founder and face of the brand.
It's a stunning fall for Huang and for Nvidia, which had become Wall Street's most valuable and influential stock because of its central role in the AI boom. Its stock price set a record last month after soaring an astounding 1,722% in four years. That compares with the already healthy gain of nearly 109% for the overall S+P 500 over the same time.
But Nvidia's price dropped 27% from then through Wednesday on worries about whether Huang would remain as CEO and how Nvidia would navigate a future without him. Nvidia's board answered the first question on Thursday morning, when it said Huang resigned, and it named chief financial officer Colette Kress as its interim CEO.
The second question remains unanswered. And it looms over the entire market because Nvidia and the broader AI industry have been the main reasons the US stock market has set so many records in recent years.
Nvidia, to be sure, is still selling AI chips at a quick rate regardless of who stands on a stage and talks them up, and analysts said it remains the industry's leader. That helped Nvidia quickly halve its early loss on Thursday, and its stock was most recently down 5.4%. That paring helped the broader market to recover.
Outside of Nvidia, stocks across the AI industry swung in the uncertainty.
CoreWeave, which buys Nvidia chips and then rents out access to them to customers over the cloud, fell 8.2%. Micron Technology, which sells memory for computers that's been getting gobbled up in the AI frenzy, sank 2.1%.
But Broadcom rose 5.2%, and Advanced Micro Devices added 5.9% on hopes the rival chip companies will gain a competitive advantage. Those two gains came close to offsetting the drop for Nvidia, which was the single heaviest weight on the S+P 500. That suggests investors see the AI business expanding to other players rather than depending solely on Nvidia.

The Trump administration faces an investigation of its scheme to take a 15% cut from any Nvidia chip sales going to China, as part of a deal to secure export licenses for the semiconductors.
Under the scheme, Nvidia chips made in Taiwan could be sold to Chinese companies if they were imported to the US first for a security review. The commerce secretary could then grant an export licence to sell the chips to China.

Half the tech world made up its mind before the facts could be assessed properly. Jensen Huang was "irreplaceable", said the chorus at the start of this week. Nvidia would melt without the co-founder's brilliance, apparently. The chipmaker's board had to find a way to save its genius for the sake of corporate America and the entire AI universe.
It was always nonsense because, after the passage of only a few days since his confession of infidelity, Huang looked less like a towering corporate titan and more like a political liability.
An extramarital affair is not usually a resigning matter in itself - saintliness is not an absolute requirement of the job. But the circumstances here were different. A relationship with the Chinese boss of a Singapore-based firm suspected of smuggling Nvidia chips into China? That displayed recklessness on top of mere unfaithfulness.
Did he know the Megaspeed executive, Alice Huang (no relation), was regarded as a Chinese spy by US officials? It hardly matters. In the ultra-political world of in-demand AI computer chips, there were enough reasons for him to be careful in the extreme. The conflict of interest, and the risk of landing Nvidia in a quagmire of national security inquiries in Washington, would have been obvious.
Reports of a second affair with a senior Nvidia employee compounded matters - US companies tend to be sensitive about such relationships. But the company's directors might also have wondered what else could be revealed in coming weeks. Rule number one in these situations is to get all the bad news out on day one.

However, sitting in Nvidia's boardroom, the job was not to worry about the initial stock market response but about what was best for the business. For multiple reasons, it was time to "rip off the Band-Aid and take Nvidia into its next chapter", as Dan Ives, a senior tech analyst, put it.

From washing dishes at Denny's to rockstar of AI: the rise and fall of Nvidia's Jensen Huang
Dan Milmo
Jensen Huang wasn't always in his trademark leather jacket. When the multibillionaire, now former Nvidia chief executive was a teenager in the US, his first job was waiting tables and washing dishes at a branch of Denny's.
"It teaches you humility, it teaches you hard work, it teaches you hospitality," he said in 2023 of his time at the diner chain.
Decades later, having become one of the richest people on the planet, Huang put on a Denny's apron in tribute to his former employer at an Nvidia event in 2025.
By this point Huang had become a rockstar of the artificial intelligence boom - befitting his signature item of clothing - feted around the world by investors, fellow tech executives and politicians.
The company that Huang co-founded makes the computer chips that are integral to the development and operation of AI systems such as ChatGPT.
Demand for Nvidia's products has surged as hundreds of billions of dollars have been poured into a technology that, according to its many boosters, will transform the global economy. The crude analogy is that Huang and Nvidia have become the main purveyors of shovels in the AI gold rush.
In technical jargon, its AI chips are known as graphics processor units (GPUs), which were originally designed to handle the graphics for video games. But it turned out that a GPU's ability to carry out masses of technical calculations simultaneously and quickly is very handy for developing and operating AI systems, which have a voracious appetite for data crunching.
Nvidia is also very good at creating networks of its chips, which produces the computing power artificial intelligence needs, and provides the CUDA software that helps the company's GPUs perform so well for AI customers.
Jen-Hsun Huang, 63, was born in Taiwan to a chemical engineer father and a schoolteacher mother. Decades later, the island would become the epicentre of chip manufacturing and the foundry for Nvidia's highly advanced chips.
Also in DC, the Democratic House minority leader Hakeem Jeffries said lawmakers should receive a classified briefing on the scandal surrounding chipmaker Nvidia.
The company is seen as among America's most important, because it makes advanced chips that are powering the AI boom.
"We need a classified briefing made available to members of Congress as it relates to the Nvidia situation and to the extent that our national security has been compromised," Jeffries said at his ongoing press conference.
"The American people deserve answers, and we're going to work to make sure that happens."
