Some mid-morning reading: a lovely Moving the Goalposts piece by Tom Garry with Gemma Darvill, coach of the first England women's Parkinson's team and how restorative football has been for her dad, who lives with the condition.
When Gemma Darvill's dad, Les, was diagnosed with Parkinson's disease aged 58, joining a walking football team stopped him from confining himself to his own home, and she believes the game saved his life.
What she could never have foreseen was that, eight years later, she would find herself coaching the first England women's Parkinson's team, helping transform the lives of women with the disease from all across the country. She is set to embark on a journey to Toronto to help spread awareness of the ways in which walking football can help put the brakes on the progression of Parkinson's symptoms.
A teacher at Swallowfield primary school in Woburn Sands, in Milton Keynes, Darvill's interest in walking football was sparked by supporting her dad. She now coaches what is believed to be the first walking football team specifically dedicated to women with Parkinson's, the Parkinson's Pioneers, who train in Solihull and have seen a tenfold increase in their membership in just over three years. That led to her appointment as the coach of the first England women's national side.

Mikel Arteta has revealed that he likes to set players challenges, such as delaying their meals before matches and making the dressing room hotter, to ensure they are fully prepared for any eventuality.
Arsenal's flight to Italy for their Champions League opener against Napoli this week was delayed for more than five hours, with Arteta forced to cancel his pre-match press conference after the squad arrived in Naples just before 10pm local time. The disruption proved no handicap as they made it 13 successive league-stage victories in Europe, thanks to Martin Ødegaard's second-half winner.
Arteta has a reputation for his innovative methods having previously employed pickpockets during pre-season training camps and preparing for a trip to Anfield by pumping crowd noises through a speaker. The Arsenal manager has now explained how his players have been trained to expect things to go wrong. "I love those challenges. I try to create them in pre-season," he said. "So when they happen, we already experience them and we already know. So, get to the stadium very late, change the routine as soon as the players have to do something else. Because when it happens, and it can happen, you can miss the flight, the train can be delayed, there's so much traffic, and I don't want the players, the staff as well, panicking.
"So, when that happens, adapt to it. 'Okay, we have another two or three hours on the plane, how can we use them?' Play together, spend some time together, sleep, use it, but certainly don't complain and then use it as an excuse because that's not helpful at all."
Asked what other methods he has employed, Arteta added: "Just delay the meals, delay the transport, take a different route. We have done many things: make the dressing room hotter, more difficult, more challenging, less [massage] beds. Those kind of things. Because we are going to have to adapt to different contexts and we will try to anticipate it and create the best possible conditions. But when there is that element of stress or surprise on matchday, it's important [to be prepared]."
