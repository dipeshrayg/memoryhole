A young maid busies herself in a vast, high-ceilinged apartment, serving her master steaming coffee in fine china and polishing the hefty family silver, as she picks her way across the checkerboard parquet lit by stylish Poul Henningsen lamps. Woman Unknown, which won the Golden Lion at the Venice film festival this year, is set in the immediate aftermath of Denmark's liberation from Nazi rule, but you wouldn't know that from the opening scenes: five years of occupation, martial law and rationing have left the splendour of the Danish bourgeoisie miraculously untouched.
The trauma of wartime violence only comes rushing back in occasional bursts: blurry flashbacks show shadowy figures pointing guns at temples and bodies thudding to the floor. But the red fabric billowing through the fog of her memory is not the Nazi flag but liberated Denmark's white-crossed Dannebrog. The black-clad executioners are not SS officers but resistance fighters reckoning with collaborators.
To maid Marie, who had a sexual relationship with an occupying German officer and lives in fear of the ritualised humiliation of female "horizontal collaborators" on the streets of Copenhagen, liberation day is not the end of the terror but its start. However, the film asks, was her wartime conduct any more shameful than that of her factory-owning employer, who shored up his wealth by continuing to do business with the Germans?
May el-Toukhy's film, out in the UK in March, is part of a raft of new high-profile productions that revisit Nazi-occupied Europe from the perspective of collaborators. Emmanuel Marre's A Man of His Time, released in France in September as Notre Salut, tells the story of a careerist bureaucrat clambering up the administrative rungs of the Vichy regime, while Xavier Giannoli's Les Rayons et les Ombres, or Rays and Shadows, paints a controversially sympathetic portrait of Jean Luchaire, a pacifist advocate of Franco-German reconciliation, who ended up a fervent pro-collaboration propagandist.
Resistance and collaboration are hardly taboo subjects for European film, but for most of the postwar era directors have approached them from the point of view of those who stood up against totalitarian rule: the resistance fighters of Roberto Rossellini's Rome, Open City, filmed on the streets of the Italian capital just months after liberation; the Montmartre theatre-owners who hide Jews in their basement in François Truffaut's The Last Metro; or a Jewish spy infiltrating Nazi ranks in Dutch director Paul Verhoeven's Black Book. In 1974, when Louis Malle made Lacombe, Lucien, a film about a 17-year-old country boy who joins the Gestapo rather than the resistance, it scandalised France and saw the director accused of failing to choose his side.
The new generation of collaboration dramas is more comfortable in the moral grey zone. Giannoli's film takes its inspiration from a line by Victor Hugo: "Every man on earth has two faces, Good and Evil. To blame everything is to understand nothing." But as far-right nationalist parties are primed to win elections all over Europe next year, is there something troubling about films that revisit the 1940s and suggest we see through the eyes of those who sided with oppressors, rather than those who stood against them?
Many historians will say these films simply provide a more realistic picture of what life in Nazi-occupied Europe looked like on the ground. "One of the biggest myths people told themselves after 1945 was that Europe liberated itself," says Halik Kochanski, whose 2023 book Resistance: The Underground War in Europe 1939-1945 was one of the first comparative studies of the subject. Armed resistance, Kochanski says, was "generally much smaller than people tend to think", actively pursued by "no more than 5% of the population in most countries".
The willingness, but also the ability to fight back varied from country to country. In Poland, a German policy to partition the occupied country, wipe out its intelligentsia and eradicate any signs of Polish nationhood was met by a population with a relatively recent history of uprisings and clandestine operations. Resistance was a matter of survival.
In most of western Europe, though, it was more complex. The Netherlands had no recent history of occupation, and a flat geography that made armed resistance a logistical challenge. In neutral Denmark, which the Nazis took in 1940 to attain a gateway to the Baltic Sea, the king was allowed to stay on the throne and the country was even allowed free elections. As a result, resistance remained low-key until the Germans instituted martial law in 1943.
Norway had the archetypal Nazi collaborator in puppet government leader Vidkun Quisling, and a democratic government in exile, which made its postwar reckoning straightforward. But not all of its resistance heroes have stood the test of time. Earlier this year, literary historian Narve Fulsås discovered that literature Nobel laureate Sigrid Undset, an anti-fascist icon who fled to the US when the Nazis invaded, continued to be published in Germany right up until 1940, in spite of the fact that the Nazis had Aryanised her publisher in 1936 and sent its Jewish founder to a concentration camp.
Even in France, the country that has mythologised its resistance like no other, dissent was not widespread until the Germans decreed forced labour in 1942. Like Henri Marre, the protagonist of A Man of His Time, many French people were distrustful of the occupiers but found it easy to convince themselves that working with them was in the national interest. "We can't let the Germans call the shots," Marre tells one of his colleagues.
Kochanski says: "The Vichy regime and the collaborating administrations in the Low Countries all said, 'We are the shield that will protect our populations from the worst of German depredations.' What built the resistance was that shield being broken - and people found it was their local authorities who were calling them up for forced labour and rounding up Jews."
Even then, armed uprisings or acts of sabotage were less common than postwar cinema has made us think. This was largely because those found to be involved in resistance activity faced torture and death - and not just their own. On 10 June 1944, a German Waffen-SS unit murdered 642 civilians in the village of Oradour-sur-Glane, in retaliation for the French resistance's nearby killing of a Nazi officer.
Kochanski recalls imposing a dilemma on a group of friends down the pub. "Imagine you lived under Nazi occupation and you decided to shelter a Jewish friend. That's fine, you are taking that risk. Now, imagine all the people in our group agree to help you hide this friend. That's also fine, because we're all taking that risk. But do you have the right to cause everyone in this pub to be executed because you have decided to hide a Jew?"
Bernhard Fulda, a professor of modern history at Cambridge, sometimes asks prospective students if they would have joined the resistance in Nazi Germany. He is sceptical of those who say yes. "I have a problem with that kind of moral grandstanding," he says. "If you haven't lived under a dictatorship, how do you know what you would have done?"
Fulda caused a stir in Berlin in 2019 with an exhibition about the political affiliations of Emil Nolde, one of a handful of artists in the Nazis' notorious "degenerate art" exhibition who were also fully paid-up members of the party. "How can we be certain we wouldn't have done what most people did: trying to muddle on, keep a low profile, possibly even consider some opportunistic career moves?"
In Rays and Shadows, Jean Luchaire's collaboration allows him to supply Jewish friends and family with paperwork enabling their escape. He even tolerates a resistance cell printing anti-Nazi leaflets in the basement. But his main motive is to maintain a lifestyle of parties and drugs.
In A Man of His Time, a story based on director Henri Marre's real-life great-grandfather, the Vichy administrator's promise to shield the French people turns out to be flimsy. Instead of supplying the German side with the 25 trucks they have demanded in order to vanish French Jews off to concentration camps in the east, the bureaucrat decides to give them only five trucks, with half the amount of petrol requested. "That way," he insists, "we've done our best. They can't complain."
Resistance could be subtle and still effective. "One mistake people make," says Kochanski, "is that they see resistance as meaning armed resistance. I see it in its broadest sense, which is really anything to harass or hamper the enemy."
This might have been be an act as simple as a farmhand keeping a record of milk deliveries to a German military unit and passing it on to resistance cells tracking troop movements. In Norway, mass resignations among judicial and church authorities, sports associations going on strike, and ordinary citizens spamming the education ministry with protest letters all helped to spoil Quisling's Nazification plan.
One of the most extraordinary acts of non-violent resistance took place in the Netherlands, where a group of artists calling themselves Persoonsbewijzencentrale, or Identity Card Centre, painstakingly forged the compulsory IDs the Germans had introduced to track the population. Initially intended to help out fellow artists, the plan grew until the group had forged 80,000 documents and ration cards for people in hiding. Eventually it was exposed and most of its members executed.
This cell's activity was captured by Violette Cornelius, whose photographs are currently on display at the Nederlands Fotomuseum in Rotterdam. Since these photographs effectively identified the members of an illegal group, the negatives were buried in eastern Netherlands and only re-emerged long after the end of the war.
Cornelius was later part of a collective of photographers who documented the German occupation in the final year of the war, using cameras hidden under coats and inside shopping bags, in anticipation of an imminent reckoning after liberation day. It was the kind of heroic work missing from the current crop of collaboration dramas - but even here there was ambiguity. "Photographers played a kind of double role in the resistance," says Loes van Harrevelt, curator of the Rotterdam show. "Some used their darkroom skills to help forge ID cards, but many also helped take the portraits for the real documents in the first place."
For Europeans, films about the second world war are always a form of collective therapy - as much about making sense of the past as to explore, or fantasise, how we would behave in similar circumstances. Perhaps the idea of a pure act of resistance is simply inconceivable to modern minds hyper-alert to accusations of hypocrisy.
"In the 1980s," says historian Fulda, "the moral righteousness that is required to say, 'You guys would have supported the fascists and I wouldn't' came much more easily to people. But in the contemporary context, shaped by the climate crisis, finger-pointing is much harder because we know our own lifestyle choices contribute to the problem. Many people try to be eco-friendly but still fly with easyJet. Perhaps collaboration is no longer such a useful concept when everyone is complicit."
