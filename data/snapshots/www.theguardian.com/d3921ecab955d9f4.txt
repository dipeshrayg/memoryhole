Pound steady, UK government borrowing costs ease a day after budget
The pound is steady and gilt yields dipped, a day after John Healey delivered a cautious set of tax and spending plans in his maiden budget to help households hit by "global economic storms," as he put it, which had little impact on markets.
The stock market has risen, with the FTSE 100 index 42 points ahead at 10,736, a 0.4% gain. Shell is the top riser, up 3.2%, after profits in the latest quarter doubled.
Sterling - the best performing major currency against the dollar this month with a 0.5% gain - dipped 0.1% against the greenback to $1.3310. It has gained a similar amount against the euro in October.
Government borrowing costs also eased. The yield on the 10-year benchmark gilt dipped 3.7 basis points to 5.308% this morning while two-year, five-year and 30-year yields are also down.
Jonas Goltermann, chief markets economist at Capital Economics, said the "No harm" budget has kept gilts out of the firing line.
The UK budget announcement has proven the non-event that newly-minted chancellor Healey had signalled in advance. While his first budget is hardly inspiring, it contrasts favourably with the initial efforts of some of his recent predecessors. By sticking to a gradual fiscal tightening path, and to the expectations set over the past couple of months for a muddle-through budget, Healey has probably done enough to keep the gilt market on side for now.
We think that gilt yields will fall over the coming year as the UK's fiscal risk premium remains contained and the Bank of England policy takes a less hawkish path than money markets discount.
Speaking on BBC radio 4's Today programme this morning, Healey vowed to cut the welfare bill before the next election, with a "tough" approach to young people who do not accept support to get a job or training.
European Central Bank president Christine Lagarde quit (effective from February) because she could no longer remain neutral about French politics and wanted greater freedom to express her views, Reuters reports, quoting an unnamed source.
France is gearing up for a presidential election next year, where far-right candidate Marine Le Pen is the favourite to win.

Ireland's economy grows rapidly for second quarter
Ireland's economy grew rapidly for a second quarter, adding to hopes for continued expansion across the eurozone despite surging energy costs.
It expanded at a quarterly rate of 5.5% between July and September, down from 10.2% in the previous quarter, according to preliminary estimates.
Ireland's statistics office said growth was driven in part by information and communications, which includes AI-related activity.
The government has long cautioned against using GDP to accurately measure economic growth as it is routinely distorted by multinational activity. Its preferred measure, modified domestic demand (MDD), is not included in the early estimates.
GDP is still used to calculate Ireland's share of activity across the eurozone and can be subject to large revisions. GDP was 3.9% higher on a year-on-year basis in the third quarter.

Haskel told BBC radio 4:
Whilst the meteorological weather has been kind to us over the last summer period, the economic weather has been much less benign. And one way to think about that is that the borrowing rates which the chancellor faced have gone up very severely since we did our last forecast in March.
Now there are lots of numbers flying around, so forgive me for one more number, which is £14bn. That's the increase in the borrowing bill just from the increase in interest rates alone that the chancellor had to find before he even stood up and did any of the types of policies which you talked about.
To give people some sense of the scale of that, that's about a third or so of the entire Department for Transport budget. So that's a lot in comparison to the other services.
Real household disposable income per person, the amount of money that people have got after inflation and tax bills was growing at about 2% a year before the financial crisis of 2007-8.
We're forecasting that because of all the shocks and so forth we've had, that growth rate has gone right down. If you take the 10-year span from the beginning of the pandemic, that measure of income will have grown by 1% over that entire 10 years, not per year, but over that entire 10 years, and that's a very very difficult outcome obviously for people. The government's welfare bill is going up from around 10.5% of GDP to around 11.5% of GDP, Haskel said.
So that's a big increase. What's interesting about that increase is that I guess if we were having this conversation 20-30 years ago, we would think of welfare as primarily being payments to unemployed people, whereas it's much more now payments on disability and payments for pensioners.

What we've worked out in our report is that over the last three years of our forecast, so from 2029 onwards, that's an area where the government hasn't yet done all the various detailed spending plans for all its individual departments. So we've done the following calculation: we've asked the question: Suppose the government carried on with its commitments to defence, which the chancellor has talked about very eloquently, with its commitments to teaching and education, with its commitments to health. What would other departments have to withstand? What would be the deal for them over the last three years? And the answer to that is a cut by around 2.6% in real terms in all of those other departments, in order to meet those commitments.

He said John Healey's plan to freeze fuel duty until the middle of next year and then increase the levy significantly in subsequent years was unrealistic. The same outlook applies to Whitehall budgets, which are on course to shrink in real terms (adjusted for inflation) from next year but provide the same level of services.
It's like when my friends say they are going to diet next week and when that doesn't happen say they will diet twice as hard in the following week. And that is how I feel about the budget more generally.

Despite mounting problems, France is not (yet) close to a full-blown crisis. Recent market moves seem to mostly reflect France's worsening fundamentals. ECB president Christine Lagarde to leave nine months early
Graeme Wearden
Christine Lagarde, the eurozone's top central banker, is stepping down nine months early.
The European Central Bank announced this morning that Lagarde would step down as its president on 7 February 2027; her term was scheduled to run until the end of October next year.
The ECB's governing council thanked Lagarde warmly and gave her a long standing ovation, as it met to set interest rates across the eurozone.
Lagarde has been seen as a candidate to take over the running of the World Economic Forum, which holds its annual meeting in Davos each January. WEF founder Klaus Schwab stepped down last year after being accused of financial and ethical misconduct - which he denies.
Lagarde's early departure fires the starting gun on the race to replace her as ECB chief, at a time when the eurozone is strugging with weak growth and above-target inflation. Potential candidates include Dutch central bank governor Klaas Knot, Pablo Hernandez de Cos of Spain, German central bank chief Joachim Nagel, and Nadia Calvino, the President of the European Investment Bank.

Volkswagen slumps to biggest loss in over a decade
Jasper Jolly
Volkswagen has slumped to its biggest loss in more than a decade in the latest quarter, as Germany's largest carmaker suffered from falling sales in China.
The Wolfsburg-based carmaker lost €4.2bn (£3.5bn) before tax between July and September, the highest since the end of 2015, it said on Thursday. Its operating loss was €5.8bn, driven by a previously announced impairment of €6.4bn on its stake in the Porsche sportscar subsidiary, and another €1bn on its Chinese operations.
Sales in the first nine months of 2026 fell 11% to 5.9m, with a "shrinking" market in China to blame, according to Arno Antlitz, Volkswagen's chief financial officer.
Antlitz said the company's earnings were "too low to continue investing strongly in our future".
Volkswagen blamed its decline in profitability on a long-running price war in China that is spilling into other markets around the world, as well as the increased share of lower-margin electric vehicles.
However, Micheal Dean, senior autos analyst at Bloomberg Intelligence, said the carmaker's guidance suggested a "sharp rebound" was likely in the last three months of 2026 after a "messy" third quarter. He said:
An 11% higher European order book and Audi recovery underpin the 4Q improvement and bode well for 2027 as restructuring savings build, helping offset China weakness, BEV [battery electric vehicle] mix margin dilution, rising costs and negative pricing.

Chancellor 'navigates first budget without spooking gilt markets'
John Healey said on Wednesday that higher inflation and interest rates had hit the government's finances, ratcheting up the government's interest bill by £14bn this year alone, but promised to stick to Labour's fiscal rules.
The Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR) said the chancellor would meet the most important rule - to balance day-to-day spending with tax revenues - with a diminished headroom of £15bn by the target date of 2029-30, against the £24bn his predecessor Rachel Reeves had built up in the spring.
Borrowing is scheduled to be higher than expected in the short term, but the chancellor promised to rebuild the buffer against the rules to £22bn by the end of the five-year forecast period.
Investors and analysts said the budget left Britain's fiscal outlook largely unchanged, which explains the muted impact on financial markets.
A 'continuity budget' with fiscal headroom upside potential pushes the pound's perceived fiscal risks to the background.
Matt Tickle, chief investment officer at the insurance group Howden Employee Benefits, said:
The new chancellor has successfully navigated his first budget without spooking gilt markets. Albeit, that is partially thanks to helpful changes to OBR growth assumptions, which have offset the falling fiscal headroom due to rising yields. We should also remember that the substantial changes have been pushed back to the 2027 spending review.
The chancellor was facing an uphill struggle given elevated bond yields seen in both the UK and across the G7. Bond yields will continue to face short term headwinds from global events in the spectre of rising energy prices and knock-on effects to broader inflation. However, the combination of fiscal discipline and the Bank of England's sensible overhaul of its quantitative tightening program should support lower gilt yields over the medium term. We remain positive on gilts over a two-year timeframe.

Lloyds finance chief hopes tax increase on banks is 'temporary measure'
Kalyeena Makortoff
Lloyds Banking Group's chief financial officer William Chalmers said the bank hopes that the tax increase on banks announced in the budget is a "temporary measure," as the lender's quarterly profits doubled.
John Healey used the budget to double the additional levy banks pay on their profits, to 6%.
The additional bank tax will only last for 15 months and marked a less significant rise than some in the industry had feared. Banks' share prices were among the highest risers in the blue chip FTSE 100 index on Wednesday.
This morning, the Lloyds share price fell as much as 6% in early trading, and is now down 3.1%, despite the bumper results. Jefferies analysts say revenue missed expectations by 1%, and the net interest margin was also below forecasts.
The bank's pre-tax profits in the latest quarter doubled to £2.3bn, with a 10% rise in net interest income, as the bank continues to benefit from higher interest rates. The figure was flattered by a £800m hit it took on motor finance provisions this time last year which was not repeated, thereby boosting the year-on-year comparison.
Chalmers said:
We lend out 99 pence of every pound we receive in deposits and our ambition is to do more. In this context, we know that the increase in the surcharge announced by the chancellor yesterday, we recognise that tax is a matter for the government. The fiscal environment is challenging.
Ultimately, it is important that the UK banking sector is able to deliver banking services to businesses and households and communities in a cost effective way.
In order to promote UK growth, it is also important that the sector is internationally competitive and not disadvantaged compared to other financial centres. Additional taxes make both of these objectives harder to achieve. We therefore very much hope that yesterday's tax increase is indeed a temporary measure to help ensure the business environment remains consistent with supporting long term investment.
Richard Hunter, head of markets at the trading platform interactive investor,said:
With provisions for the motor finance investigation all now but buried and with higher income flowing through, Lloyds has enjoyed a bumper quarter which puts the bank in prime position to meet its full year targets.
Some of the clouds which have been overhanging the sector leading up to the budget, and the domestic banks in particular, have now partially parted. The increase to the bank surcharge was less than feared, manageable and temporary and led to a small relief rally in Lloyds' shares yesterday. Other concerns also remain on the back burner, with the level of defaults and arrears low and stable, although in typically conservative fashion Lloyds has increased impairment provisions for the quarter to £313m, or £930m in the year to date.
This of course anticipates potential rather than actual losses, such that releases are possible in the future should the bad debts fail to materialise. The higher figure is based on several scenarios, such as the inflationary impact of the Middle East crisis, lower GDP growth in the UK, a rise in unemployment and limited gains in residential and commercial property prices. Even so, at the moment, there are few if any signs of deterioration across its lending book.
Introduction: Healey's cost of living support package 'badly targeted,' says Resolution Foundation
Good morning, and welcome to our rolling coverage of business, the financial markets and the world economy.
The £4.4bn cost of living support package delivered by John Healey's budget on Wednesday is "badly targeted," which means that even families receiving help face higher energy bills than during the last energy price shock, according to the Resolution Foundation.
The poorest half of households will gain £158, on average, from support measures this year and next - just £14 more than the £144 received by the richest half - highlighting a "complete failure to target help at those who need it most," the think tank said.
Typical energy bills net of support are forecast to be £1,750 this year (2026-27) - higher in real terms than during the last energy price shock (2022-23) when net bills were £1,685.
The chancellor raised taxes on banks and landlords to fund a £100 winter handout for 20 million families as official forecasts made clear there is no end in sight for the cost of living crisis.
The Resolution Foundation's analysis found that UK tax rises stand out globally. The UK's internationally remarkable consolidation in its public finances, touted by the chancellor in his budget speech, is entirely due to a rising tax take during the current parliament. The £76bn improvement in the UK's primary balance - the gap between receipts and non-interest spending - between 2023-24 and 2028-29 is more than fully accounted for by £107bn of higher tax revenues.
Whitehall gets a bigger boost than households. The budget included a £9bn top up to departmental budgets over the current spending review period - twice as much as the £4.4bn cost of living support package.
Past gain, future pain for living standards. Median real annual disposable incomes for working age households are set to grow by a paltry £600 over the course of the Parliament. Worse still, those gains are already behind us. Between now (2026-27) and the end of the parliament (2028-29), typical incomes are forecast to fall by £1,050 (3%).
Ruth Curtice, chief executive of the Resolution Foundation, said:
Faced with a tough backdrop amid global economic turmoil, the chancellor delivered a scattergun budget that did little to address the sharp challenges facing Britain. Cost of living support is being spread far too thinly and will leave even those families receiving help facing higher energy bills than they did during the last energy price shock.
Stepping back, this parliament is increasingly looking like one of two halves, which yesterday's budget did little to address. The first half has been marked by rising living standards, solid economic growth and extra funding for public services - funded by huge tax rises - none of which was enough to prevent dramatic half-time substitutions in Downing Street.
The new occupants will hope for better political fortune in the second half, but it could prove tough going against a projected £1,000 fall in disposable incomes. A more dramatic change of tactics may be needed, and we did not learn how the government will choose between public service ambitions and household incomes. Next year the choices will unavoidably get starker.
