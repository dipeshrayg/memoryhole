Most Formula One drivers might slip away to the beach or the golf course on a week off between races. Lewis Hamilton is not most drivers, though. Instead he went to the Rafah border crossing between Egypt and Gaza. He greeted aid workers in Arabic, toured the checkpoint and visited the Egyptian Red Crescent warehouses in nearby El-Arish, where relief supplies are prepared for distribution. Hamilton made the visit on a day Israeli airstrikes and gunfire killed at least seven Palestinians in Gaza, adding to a death toll that's climbed above 74,000 since war broke out three years ago. He wore dark glasses and dressed head to toe in black.
"The past three years of Israel's genocide in Gaza is appalling," he wrote on Instagram of his visit, before turning the lens on himself. "I know so many people that feel that same helplessness. That same need to do something rather than watch. I don't want us to get used to their suffering. I don't want us to look away."
When it comes to addressing issues outside sport, Hamilton has never been one to hold his tongue. His willingness to keep stating his beliefs plainly, and the principles he has consistently stood up for, set him apart from US sports stars, many of whom have toned down progressive rhetoric considerably during Donald Trump's second term. The contrast won't be lost on Hamilton's legion of American fans as Formula One returns to the US this weekend for the Las Vegas Grand Prix.
Hamilton, a seven-time world champion and the only Black driver in Formula One history, occupies a singular position in sport. Unlike, say, Colin Kaepernick - who is nobody's all-time sporting great, Super Bowl run aside - Hamilton made himself impossible to deny or ignore. And unlike, say, LeBron James - who once set the tone for athlete activism, but has since backpedaled from politics during Trump's second term - Hamilton still welcomes all the smoke. As Formula One has exploded in popularity in the US over the past decade, Hamilton has remained its most US-facing ambassador. He is the Kardashian boyfriend as likely to turn up at the Met Gala as at a Denver Broncos game, not least because he's a part-owner of the NFL team. Now he carries the flag as sport's apex protester.
When US athletes staged silent protests during the national anthem before games, Hamilton was the one making sure Formula One couldn't look away. During the 2020 and 2021 seasons, drivers gathered for a moment of silence in "End Racism" shirts before each race. Hamilton and four-time champion Sebastian Vettel were the most consistent kneelers. "There was never a second of doubt in my head [about kneeling], first and foremost out of respect for Lewis, and to support him, and the message," Vettel said. "That's why I was happy to kneel."
Hamilton is quick to stick his neck out on US politics, putting the hammer down as readily as he does through Maggotts and Becketts. When Trump took aim at kneeling NFL players in 2017, Hamilton defended free expression. "I think it is open for anyone to have freedom of speech, and I guess we can all play a role in trying to make a difference in the world - particularly if your leader is not helping in that area," he remarked. In 2020, Hamilton took aim at Trump's leadership again, saying he "turned off the lights and hid in his bunker" during the unrest that followed the killing of George Floyd.
Hamilton's influence extends beyond Formula One. Bubba Wallace, Nascar's most prominent Black driver, has cited Hamilton as an inspiration in his own efforts to push his hidebound sport to confront racial inequality. In a 2025 interview with Time, Hamilton vowed to continue his diversity work even as organizations rolled back support amid Trump administration attacks on DEI. "I'm not going to change what [Trump] does or what the government does," Hamilton said. "All I can do is try to make sure that in my space, in my environment, I'm trying to elevate people."
Hamilton has taken a two-pronged approach. In 2020 he became a founding co-chair of the Hamilton Commission, an initiative designed to address Black underrepresentation in UK motorsport and Stem careers. Its 2021 report identified barriers ranging from school culture and university recruitment practices to workplace culture, and estimated that fewer than 1% of people working in Formula One came from Black backgrounds.
Drawing on the commission's recommendations, Hamilton launched Mission 44 (taking its name from his racing number) to support underrepresented young people in their education and employment pursuits, not just those aspiring to careers in motorsport. He pledged £20m of his own money to get the project rolling. By 2026, seven Mission 44 scholarship recipients were working in motorsport - five of them at Formula One teams.
Behind the wheel, Hamilton has helped change the racial complexion of the grid by inspiring kids from similar backgrounds to dream big. Nineteen years after making history as Formula One's first Black driver, Hamilton now shares the grid with French-Algerian Isack Hadjar, Thai-British Alex Albon and British-Indian Arvid Lindblad. It may not be much longer before he's racing alongside Ugo Ugochukwu - the Black American who became Formula 3 champion in September and is reportedly slated to drive practice sessions for Cadillac in 2027. "It's been amazing to see his rise through the class," Hamilton said during the Spanish GP weekend. "Really exciting for next year."
Bringing an Formula One race back to Africa for the first time since the 1990s has been one of Hamilton's long-running ambitions, and last month's announcement of the African Grand Prix in Rwanda marked the realization of that dream. Now 41, Hamilton is determined to be on the grid when the race makes its debut in the early 2030s. After the announcement, he expressed his excitement at racing in Rwanda and showing the country off to the world. But he also acknowledged the urgent human-rights concerns surrounding Rwanda, in keeping with his history of calling out Formula One for getting into bed with autocratic regimes.
Hamilton may have the stature to speak freely on matters inside and outside his sport, but that's easier said than done in motorsport, where teams live and die on sponsorship money and drivers can lose their jobs for failing to bring in enough of it, regardless of how well they perform. Hamilton's pro-Palestine stance isn't just the kind of thing that could cost him financial backing; it threatens to make him more vulnerable at Ferrari, the circuit's flagship franchise, where Hamilton and teammate Charles Leclerc are locked in a power struggle (much as they would have you believe otherwise), with free agency potentially looming for Hamilton again after the 2027 season.
Inevitably, there will be skeptics who dismiss Hamilton's outspokenness as grandstanding from an athlete nearing the end of his career with not much to lose. But Hamilton has long made clear that his legacy means more to him than his place in the record books. "It's great having these wins," he said after a 2020 Eifel Grand Prix victory that drew him level in the all-time wins column with Michael Schumacher, still Hamilton's best comparison. "But I think the more important things are what you do out of the car. That's really where I think the impact can be made, in terms of wanting to be remembered."
Hamilton continues to use his considerable platform to draw attention to the world's injustices, even when doing so carries risks. In that respect, he offers a model for would-be athlete activists in the US - not just in what he says, but in his willingness to put his feet on the ground and stand 10 toes down.
