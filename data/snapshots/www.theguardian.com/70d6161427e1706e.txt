Lace up those corsets and wriggle into those breeches ... it's time for a huge new TV retelling of Pride and Prejudice. Jane Austen's classic romance will come to life once more with writer Dolly Alderton's glitzy Netflix version, starring Emma Corrin as Elizabeth Bennet and Jack Lowden as the self-proclaimed "first ginger Mr Darcy".
As a new generation of fans are set to fall in love with Lizzy, Darcy and the whole Bennet clan, it's only right to celebrate all the incredible shows that have transported us back in time. Colin Firth's wet white shirt might fly the flag for great period drama, but there are whole other old worlds spanning all centuries out there ready to stream. Our writers share some of the very best ...
Brideshead Revisited
A floppy-fringed fop in cricket whites and stripey blazer, carrying a teddy bear named Aloysius? Count me in. Granada's 1981 adaptation of Evelyn Waugh's novel pretty much invented an entire TV genre. Like Saltburn 40 years later (but without the grave-humping), it saw an Oxford student form a homoerotic friendship with an eccentric toff and get sucked into the drama of his dysfunctional aristocratic family.
There had been great period pieces before - most notably I, Claudius - but they were stagey affairs, filmed on wobbly sets. Brideshead was a giga-budget game-changer, bringing in the cinematic scope and lavish locations we take for granted today. Poster boys Jeremy Irons and Anthony Andrews were supported by a West End's worth of luvvies, including "Larry" Olivier and "Johnny" Gielgud. Over 11 stately episodes, it set the gilded template for ye olde dramas ever since. Michael Hogan
Pride and Prejudice
Pride and Prejudice was sometimes painful to watch, as Austen's brute romantic realities - five pillars of next-generation Bennet, consigned to shrivel in genteel destitution unless they can get someone to fancy them, and fast - met the callous 90s, where the sisters have to laugh at their desperation or else they're a killjoy. Andrew Davies's script was fabulous but not kind. The great houses of the English aristocracy brought an elegantly tense, here's-what-you-could-win atmosphere; like Location, Location, Location meets Squid Game.
Consequently, there was much more riding on Colin Firth and Jennifer Ehle than a plain will-they-won't-they: their unbearably slow though entirely predictable passion (even if you'd never met the book!) had to rescue the family, the society, the viewer, the very blades of grass all around, from the stress of a world in which the stakes were both impossibly high and pitiably trivial. And they really, really delivered: Ehle's expression, as she's almost winded by genuine feeling, is exquisitely evocative. Firth, of course, was never able to erase the cultural mark he made by jumping into a lake with his shirt on. The pair had a brief fling during and after filming, almost (one imagines) as a courtesy to the cultural fervour they created. It didn't last, but it would have been rude not to. Zoe Williams
Wolf Hall
Technically, it can't be done. You cannot take over a 1,000 pages of the most luminous, intricately woven prose ever written, put them on screen and lose nothing in translation - or at least nothing you don't gain back tenfold elsewhere.
But that is what writer Peter Straughan and director Peter Kosminsky, plus a handful of actors at the top of their game, did when they adapted the first two volumes of Hilary Mantel's trilogy about the rise and fall of Thomas Cromwell, and how he oversaw Henry VIII's split from Rome, marriage to Anne Boleyn and eventually resolved on a scaffold at dawn the king's Great Matter once and for all. There's simply nothing like it - for beauty, for delicacy, for horror, for heartbreak. Every line, every frame, every fleeting expression on an actor's face does double, triple duty as an engine of story and creator of an experience that allows you to watch the past unspool as if for the first time and stands as a testament to human creativity and the power and refuge of art. Lucy Mangan
Bleak House
Bleak House is the celestial moment when BBC period drama, the baronet of its adaptation for the screen Andrew Davies, the enormous cast of characters, and Charles Dickens himself were all at their very best. The outcome is 15 exquisitely taut, tense, addictive, fog-ridden half-hour episodes (except the first one which was an entire glorious hour), restoring Dickens to the serial format that made him so famous, broadcast twice weekly with cliffhanger endings so finely judged even the Victorians would have approved. Anna Maxwell Martin, in her first lead role, delivers an astonishing performance as Esther Summerson. Gillian Anderson's haunting (and haunted) Lady Dedlock is unforgettably sombre and devastatingly sad. Charles Dance's Tulkinghorn is the most terrifyingly serpent-like family lawyer known to the profession, all hooded eyes and hissed threats. And there will never be a more comically spot-on Mr Guppy in televisual history than Burn Gorman. Millions tuned in every week to see how Jarndyce and Jarndyce would pan out (badly, of course). I still rewatch it every single year, hanging on every perfectly-paced word. The benchmark of grip-the-nation British period drama, then and now. Chitra Ramaswamy
Mad Men
Period dramas are so firmly associated with bonnets and windswept moors that suggesting anything that doesn't fit that description feels faintly sacrilegious. But despite depicting an era maybe even your mum can just about remember, and being [drops monocle into snifter in horror] American to boot, Mad Men is a proper period drama. And not just because the costumes are on point - though they are, with every last Sterling Cooper employee's pocket square positioned just so.
No, what makes Matt Weiner's ad industry saga so great is its ability to make its chosen age - buttoned-down late 50s giving way to the charged, chaotic 60s - both alien and achingly close to home. After all, nostalgia, as Don Draper reminded us in the show's most memorable sales pitch, translates as "the pain from an old wound". It's a show that both seduces and repels you with its vision of the not-so-distant past - capitalism starting to curdle, sexism and racism both casual and deeply ingrained. But like an appealing if inadvisable Old Fashioned, you can't help yourself from drinking it down. Gwilym Mumford
The Crimson Petal and the White
In truth, I have always struggled with period dramas, since it's as if all the fun stuff - the characters, the plot, the identifiable human behaviour - is obscured behind a thick screen of frills and waffle. But Lucinda Coxon's 2011 adaptation of Michel Faber's novel cuts straight through all that. It's a queasy, bilious four-parter that heaves with fury from the outset. Romola Garai plays Sugar, a sex worker fuelled by hatred for all the men who casually abused her and her friends. Chris O'Dowd plays William Rackham, an ineffectual rich kid who wants to be a writer but has no talent for it. He falls for her, despite having a mentally ill wife at home, and invites her to become the governess to his daughter. Sugar gets to see all the opportunities afforded to the upper classes and maximises upon them, while William stumbles into a trap of his own making. The Crimson Petal and the White represents period dramas at their nastiest. If only more would follow suit. Stuart Heritage
To Walk Invisible
Any Brontë fan who has walked around the tiny, rickety rooms of the sisters' Haworth home will have spent hours imagining the young women huddled together, scratching away with pens to tell their stories. Leave it to Sally Wainwright to put her ideas of them on screen in this exquisite feature drama about Charlotte, Emily and Anne - and their brother Branwell, whose alcoholism deeply affected their lives and works. It shows not just their fight to get published, using pseudonyms, but their increasingly desperate need to earn money as unmarried women. Filmed in a replica of the Brontë parsonage and on the surrounding Penistone Hill Country Park, it's like living daily life and walking the moors with the sisters - played almost precisely how I picture them by Finn Atkins, Chloe Pirrie and Charlie Murphy. Forget Margot Robbie in red latex! This is the real Brontë experience. Hollie Richardson
Tipping the Velvet
It has been almost 25 years since it aired on BBC Two, and in many ways, Andrew Davies' adaptation of Sarah Waters' debut novel does look and feel of its time. It told the story of fictional Victorian music hall star and male impersonator Nan Astley, whose lesbian awakening dragged her through the highs and lows of London's bohemian society. It was raucous, camp and surprisingly sexually frank. It may have cast pretty, overtly feminine women in roles that would, today, demand a more butch presence, but in the early 00s, it was a rare case of a period drama, or any drama for that matter, that did not skirt around its queerness. As such, it deserves its place in TV history. Also fun to spot Benedict Cumberbatch and Sally Hawkins and Monica Dolan in early roles, and to wonder how "Monsieur Dildo" ever made it on to the screen in 2002. Rebecca Nicholson
Our Friends in the North
We tend to think of period drama as telling stories from a century or more before and coming from a dead writer's book. Peter Flannery's nine-parter was adapted from his own stage play with a narrative starting 32 years before the transmission date and ending just one year before. Yet all other qualifications of the genre are met: the popularisation of distant history (in this case, civic corruption in the north east), spectacular work by the costume and wig departments, characters of Dickensian richness that four young actors - Gina McKee, Daniel Craig, Mark Strong, Christopher Eccleston - made glorious calling cards: the show's alumnae include a James Bond (Craig) and a Doctor Who (Eccleston) and the four share a heavy shelf of Bafta, Emmy, Olivier and Tony wins and nominations. Mark Lawson
The Red Riding trilogy
"To the North. Where we do what we want!" In period terms, this three-part adaptation of David Peace's titular trilogy is riveted tightly to Britain in the 1970s. The original novels were loosely inspired (if that's the right word for the ambient horror they conjure) by the hunt for the Yorkshire Ripper. These dramas pick up that deadly trail and run with it, into purgatory and beyond. The police station resembles the inside of a smoker's lung. Straight-dealers end up in metaphorical and sometimes literal ditches. And around every corner, there's a moral dead end waiting. The cast - which includes Andrew Garfield, Rebecca Hall, Sean Harris, Daniel Mays, Maxine Peake and Paddy Considine - is extraordinary and taken as a whole, Red Riding represents a relentlessly claustrophobic suite of desolation and corruption. Bodices and frilly frock coats are conspicuous by their absence. Phil Harrison
Parade's End
Parade's End, a sumptuous adaptation of Ford Madox Ford's quartet of novels of the same name, aired on BBC Two when I was 16, just at the point when my TV tastes were becoming refined enough for meaty, sexy fare. I was blown away by Tom Stoppard's expert take on Ford's twisty story, which follows righteous aristocrat Christopher Tietjens as he serves in the first world war. And what a cracking cast: Benedict Cumberbatch plays Tietjens, Adelaide Clemens is excellent as Valentine Wannop, the young suffragette for whom he develops feelings, while Anne-Marie Duff and Stephen Graham appear in supporting roles. It's Rebecca Hall, though, as Sylvia, Tietjens's mesmerising and manipulative - but ultimately miserable - wife, who steals the show. Being closeted at the time, I put my teenage obsession with the show down to a crush on Cumberbatch, but I knew deep down it was actually Hall I fancied, with her long tailored coats and ability to express five emotions with the smallest raise of an eyebrow. I defy anyone watching Parade's End not to fall in love with her. Lucy Knight
Life on Mars
"It's 1973, almost dinner time. I'm havin' 'oops!" growls DCI Gene Hunt (Philip Glenister) as he pins bewildered noughties metrosexual DI Sam Tyler (John Simm) against a filing cabinet in the first episode of the BBC's still mindblowing police procedural Life on Mars. I generally find costume drama a bit too much like homework. For Life on Mars, I make an exception. That's mainly because it's a show about suspension of disbelief. Has Simm's detective really time-travelled from 2006 or is he comatose, dreaming? Could his subconscious have cobbled together a vision of the past this credible? This is a show that encourages you to question every little detail of the seductive 1970s setting - a deconstruction of the period drama that reflects on nostalgia, the nature of reality and the strangeness of the past. Now, 20 years on, it's technically a relic itself - but this postmodern masterpiece is far from dusty. Rachel Aroesti
