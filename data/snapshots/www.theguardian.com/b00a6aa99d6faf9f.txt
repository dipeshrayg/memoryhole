All three major US stock indexes slumped on Friday, with the tech-heavy Nasdaq suffering the steepest loss.
The tech rout was sparked by disappointing quarterly results from Intel and Amazon released late on Thursday.

Analysts now expect third-quarter S+P 500 earnings growth of 31.6%, year-on-year, per LSEG.
Oil supermajors Exxon Mobil and Chevron both beat quarterly profit expectations, benefiting from soaring profit margins as a result of energy supply disruptions arising from the US-Israeli war with Iran and Russia's war in Ukraine.
Starbucks dipped after the coffee chain forecast an annual revenue decline due to its divestiture of its Chinese business.

Binance has no cyber insurance and will fund customer reimbursements through company reserves after hackers stole $6.3bn in the world's largest cryptocurrency heist, Reuters is reporting, citing a source close to the exchange.
Binance has said the attack began with a compromised employee laptop and involved multiple breach points. It found no evidence of private-key leakage and said roughly $3bn of affected assets had been frozen.
The exchange holds directors' and officers' (D+O) liability insurance, Reuters's source said, describing crypto cyber insurance as "virtually impossible" for crypto companies to obtain.
The source also said Binance's Secure Asset Fund for Users (SAFU) had no external insurance or reinsurance backing.
D+O cover could respond to claims against executives alleging failings in their oversight, rather than replace stolen assets.
Analysis: How crypto hacking funds isolated North Korea's nuclear programme
Dan Sabbagh
Defence and security editor
North Korea plays by its own rules. The isolated, secretive and autocratic country is largely cut off from the world's economic system - but when it comes to generating foreign earnings it relies on some of the world's most sophisticated hacking.
Experts, such as TRM Labs, quickly attributed the extraordinary $6.3bn Binance cryptocurrency theft to TraderTraitor, an elite North Korean hacking unit that has also been known as the Lazarus Group. Though the link may not be proven yet, the pattern of behaviour is familiar.
North Korea has a history of cryptocurrency theft dating back to 2017, focusing on the sector's relatively lax security controls as a way of stealing money and generating revenues for its military, including its nuclear weapons programme.
Kim Jong-un, its dictatorial ruler, controls a stockpile of about 50 nuclear warheads, and has been supplying troops and weapons to help Russia in its war in Ukraine. But North Korea is also extremely poor: 40% of the population are undernourished according to the UN and nearly one in five of its children are stunted.
A UN panel of experts, reporting to the security council, said in the spring of 2024 that it was reviewing "58 suspected cyber-attacks" by North Korea "on cryptocurrency-related companies between 2017 and 2023" worth $3bn.
The panel said the cash was estimated to generate half of North Korea's foreign currency income. One unnamed country went further with its estimate: the stolen billions fund "40% of the weapons of mass destruction programmes".
The suspected crypto hacks amounted to as many as 17 in 2023 alone: thefts valued at $750m, and disturbingly, the sums involved are only increasing. The world's largest previous crypto hack took place in February 2025, when $1.5bn was stolen from the Bybit exchange - the work, the FBI said, of TraderTraitor.
Ciaran Martin, a former head of the UK's National Cyber Security Centre, argues that North Korea appears to have targeted crypto because traditional banks have developed sophisticated defences that the fast-growing and lightly regulated sector has yet to match. He said:
Over the last decade, hackers have walked off with nine- or 10-figure sums from crypto hacks on more than 30 occasions. The number of traditional banks who have suffered a loss of more than $100m as a result of a cyber-attack in the same period is zero or one.
The irony is that the internet in North Korea is severely restricted, with access denied to the general population. Maths and science are pushed at school, however, and at university some of the best students are recruited into hacker groups, such as Lazarus, operating under the auspices of the country's Reconnaissance General Bureau spy agency.
The $6.3bn Binance hack could mark an "inflection point" for digital asset insurance buying, with firms needing to look beyond large specie limits and examine the breadth of their protection, Relm CEO Joseph Ziolkowski told Reuters.
Exchanges and custodians had historically prioritised relatively inexpensive specie cover, he said, describing an approach of "form over function". More than $1bn of specie limits was available globally, with less available for cyber cover including digital asset theft.
Ziolkowski described specie as largely protecting private keys. But a malicious intrusion elsewhere in a transaction approval process could enable theft while those keys remained intact.
Binance has said it found no evidence of private-key leakage and identified multiple breach points.
Cyber policies could respond to such intrusions where their wording expressly included digital asset theft, Ziolkowski said. He argued buyers should examine protection across their operations and transaction signing arrangements.
Even holding cyber insurance would not establish that a particular loss was covered, Ed Lewis, global lead for cyber and technology risks at RPC, told the news agency.
"A cyberattack tells you how the trouble started. It does not tell you which policy pays for the missing money," he said.
A precautionary suspension of withdrawals might not satisfy the policy trigger for business interruption cover, Lewis said. Stolen assets, response costs, lost income and customer claims each required analysis against the facts and wording.
Binance has pledged to meet customer losses through its SAFU reserve and company resources, announcing more than $6.34bn of company funds separate from roughly $3bn of frozen assets.
Lewis said frozen assets had not necessarily been returned, while customer reimbursement did not establish that an insurer was paying. "A record theft does not automatically become a record insurance loss," he said.
Tech stocks sell-off deepens, dragging Wall Street toward worst day since June
A sell-off for tech stocks is getting worse Friday on worries that Wall Street's frenzy around AI sent prices too high, the Associated Press reported.
The S+P 500 dropped 1.9% and was on track to erase its gain for October, even though it set its all-time high during the month. The Nasdaq composite was likewise heading toward its worst day since June, as the weakness for technology stocks dragged it down 3.1%, as of 1:20 pm ET. The Dow Jones Industrial Average was down 511 points, or 1%.
The U.S. stock market remains only 3.1% below its record, which has boosted the value of workers' 401(k) accounts, but the shakiness for stocks in the AI industry is coming just days before Tuesday's upcoming US elections. Worries about AI in general have already become a general and bipartisan concern for voters, and Donald Trump has historically used the stock market's performance as a measure of his own personal success.
In the cryptocurrency industry, meanwhile, stocks tumbled following a hack of Binance, the world's biggest crypto exchange. The price of bitcoin fell toward $77,000 from more than $86,000 earlier this month, and a US-based blockchain intelligence firm said a North Korean unit was likely behind the attack that impacted more than $6 billion worth of crypto at Binance.
Explainer: How big a deal is the $6.3bn Binance heist?
By Dan Milmo and Kalyeena Makortoff
The world's largest cryptocurrency exchange has been hit by a $6.3bn heist that, according to experts, is likely to be the work of North Korea.
This is not the first time a crypto market has been targeted by hackers, and the rogue state has a track record of this type of theft. Here we answer some key questions about what's happened:
Just how big a deal is this?
It could be the biggest heist of any kind in history, according to Elliptic, a consultancy specialising in crypto-related crime. The incident, which took place late on Thursday, is more than four times bigger than the previous largest crypto hack, the $1.5bn one targeting the Bybit exchange last year. Elliptic says there does not appear to be anything comparable in the pre-crypto era.
How was Binance hacked?
It does not appear that private keys were hacked. Instead, a Binance engineer's laptop was infiltrated, which in turn allowed the attackers to drain "hot" wallets. Binance later added that there were "additional points of breach" in its systems as well as the laptop.
Do we know who did it?
This attack has been attributed to a North Korean hacking unit called TraderTraitor, a name assigned to it by the US government. The communist state, short of cash and hamstrung by sanctions, has a strong track record of crypto hacking. TraderTraitor emerged from Lazarus, a group working from North Korea's Reconnaissance General Bureau, a military intelligence agency.
