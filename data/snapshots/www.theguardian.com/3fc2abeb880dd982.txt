20. The Healing Room (2000)
This suitably uncompromising opener to the lengthy Faith and Courage offers a collage of radio static, Indian-inspired beats, dub bassline and whispered vocals. More importantly, it's fantastic: you might call it trip-hop were Sinéad O'Connor's vocal not so intense as to negate any potential for relaxation on the part of the listener.
19. The Voice of My Doctor (2014)
From O'Connor's final album, I'm Not Bossy, I'm the Boss - she abandoned an almost-complete follow-up with producer David Holmes after the death of her son - The Voice of My Doctor is grinding, distorted blues rock. Its lyric, a saga of infidelity delivered with real fury, references Nothing Compares 2 U.
18. Success Has Made a Failure of Our Home (1992)
Am I Not Your Girl? now seems wildly ahead of its time; a collection of covers of American Broadway, jazz and film standards (before such things became reliable pop staples), all fabulously orchestrated. Here, the blasting John Barry-style brass presages the 90s easy-listening revival, but the real drama lies in O'Connor's astonishing vocal.
17. 4th and Vine (2012)
An explosion of life-affirming joy in a catalogue not much given to that kind of thing, the ramshackle but wildly catchy 4th and Vine is an untrammelled delight. Extra points for the simple-but-great video, featuring a beaming O'Connor and band belting it out in a packed Irish pub.
16. All Apologies (1994)
O'Connor was a friend of Kurt Cobain: after his death, she stripped back Nirvana's All Apologies to almost nothing beyond an acoustic guitar and a vocal that replaces the original's pained yowl with a kind of fierce, pure whisper. It is utterly haunting.
15. I Want Your (Hands on Me) (1987)
As close to a straightforward pop track as O'Connor ever recorded, the lissom funk of I Want Your (Hands on Me) bears the faint influence of Jam and Lewis in its production, and of their former employee Prince in its forthright lyrical approach to seduction. It's sonically of its time, but a total banger nonetheless.
14. Black Boys on Mopeds (1990)
A stark, potent depiction of institutionalised racism and poverty in Thatcher-era Britain ("not the mythical land of Madame George and roses") that still hits hard nearly 40 years on. "These are dangerous days - to say what you feel is to dig your own grave," she notes, two years before her infamous SNL appearance.
13. Emma's Song (2000)
The soaring melody and warm arrangement (it was co-produced by Brian Eno) can't quite mask the sense of unease engendered by the lyrics. A saga of birth, love, hatred and regret, they're oblique enough to be open to interpretation - is it about mother-daughter relations? - but powerful enough to knock you sideways.
12. I Am Stretched on Your Grave (1990)
An Irish folk standard with roots in a 17th-century poem, O'Connor's take on I Am Stretched on Your Grave welds a stark, largely a cappella reading of the song to a baggy-era breakbeat. It's an improbable idea that somehow succeeds in amping up its stark grief: you couldn't imagine anyone dancing to it.
11. Thank You for Hearing Me (1994)
An entirely original break-up song, apparently inspired by O'Connor's relationship with Peter Gabriel, the gently trip-hop influenced Thank You for Hearing Me occupies a complex but believable emotional space. It's all gratitude and fond recollection until the final verse, when a note of darkness, a faint hint of screw-you, clouds the picture.
10. Troy (1987)
O'Connor's debut single arrived in the autumn of 1987 - not, by any metric, a vintage year for pop music. But Troy would have attracted attention at any point in history: a lyric in which WB Yeats gets bound up with the singer's childhood trauma, a vocal so powerful it's almost alarming, a high-drama string arrangement.
9. The Wolf Is Getting Married (2012)
There are fine moments on O'Connor's 00s albums Sean-Nós Nua, Throw Down Your Arms and Theology, but 2012's How About I Be Me (and You Be You)? still felt like an all-guns-blazing return to form. The Wolf Is Getting Married packs a mighty chorus, its lyrics brimming with fresh optimism, and her voice sounds magnificent.
8. No Man's Woman (2000)
This is O'Connor at her don't-mess fiercest, dismissing "all this man-trolling" - no to another husband, or indeed another boyfriend - over a churning alt-rock backing. There's a faint hint of the Beatles about the tune; the hook is absolutely immense. How on earth was this not a huge hit?
7. Fire on Babylon (1994)
Co-producer John Reynolds said O'Connor's vocal on Fire on Babylon "possessed an energy that was almost impossible to contain". Certainly, there's a harrowing, red-raw quality to her singing on a track that revisits her abusive childhood and unflinchingly excoriates her mother. The reggae-infused backing sweetens the pill a little.
6. This Is to Mother You (1997)
From the largely acoustic EP Gospel Oak - a very different beast indeed to its fraught predecessor, 1994's Universal Mother - This Is to Mother You is just an extraordinarily beautiful, tender song. Subsequently covered by Emmylou Harris and Linda Ronstadt, and reworked in 2009 as a charity duet with Mary J Blige, the original is still the keeper.
5. My Special Child (1991)
A one-off single that stalled just outside the UK Top 40, My Special Child was, perhaps, just too raw and personal in its treatment of the collapse of O'Connor's marriage - and her decision to have an abortion - for mass consumption. There's certainly nothing wrong with its stunning melody, nor its majestic and folky arrangement, though.
4. You Made Me the Thief of Your Heart (1994)
Co-written by Bono and produced by Bomb the Bass's Tim Simenon, O'Connor's contribution to the soundtrack for In the Name of the Father sets a folky melody and murder ballad-inspired lyric to a backdrop of pounding drums, ambient synths and distorted fiddle. Her voice sounds alternately flinty and agonised; the overall effect is stunning.
3. Mandinka (1987)
Despite its none-more-80s drums, O'Connor's first hit sounded like nothing else in the charts on arrival. There's a swaggering confidence about its faintly Clash-like guitar riff, its astonishing swooping vocal and its strange but intriguing words, complete with early statement of her unbiddable approach: "I have refused to take part."
2. The Last Day of Our Acquaintance (1990)
At first, it's an exercise in restraint: nothing but an acoustic strum and O'Connor pouring out her heart about her divorce (the way she sings "I'll talk but you won't listen to me" is shattering). Then, a minute before the end, the strumming gets more frantic and a sense of catharsis kicks in. An awesome song.
1. Nothing Compares 2 U (1990)
In a sense, it feels unfair putting Nothing Compares 2 U at the top of this list. It's a cover, lording it over umpteen examples of O'Connor's songwriting skill; moreover, it enjoyed the kind of vast, unrepeatable success that overshadows the rest of her oeuvre in the eyes of casual listeners. But then again ... just listen to it. More than 35 years of familiarity haven't done anything to dull its emotional impact: her voice whispers and soars, shifting from sorrow to anger to despair, completely inhabiting a lyric in which every suggestion to forget heartbreak and move forward falls on deaf ears. "It's my song now," she once remarked - tough talk when the author was Prince, but absolutely correct.
