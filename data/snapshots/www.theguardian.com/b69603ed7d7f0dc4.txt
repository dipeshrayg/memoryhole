"I feel Gordon Brothers punched us in the guts and took us down," says Kelvin Blake, one of thousands of Poundland workers at risk of redundancy after the budget retailer's US-based owners rejected at least two solvent bids for it.
While administrators at AlixPartners, who were appointed on Wednesday, continue to consider bids for the whole group, which has 600 stores in the UK and Ireland including the Dealz chain, more than 10,000 staff and thousands of suppliers face a grim Christmas of uncertainty.
The impact is being felt at Poundland stores across the country, as well at its head office and its distribution centres, including one in Andy Burnham's Makerfield constituency.
Gordon Brothers, which bought Poundland last year for £1, has already collected close to £20m in interest payments and various fees, including those relating to store closures, according to documents provided to MPs. As a secured creditor on the majority of a £30m shareholder loan, Gordon Brothers is near the front of the queue to receive the proceeds of the administration including the sale of stock and store leases.
All stores currently remain open with about six main interested parties given until Sunday to put themselves in the bidding for Poundland now it is in administration. Those six potential bidders include: the Northern Irish company Martin Property Group; Zachert Private Equity, a German fund founded by a former Bloomberg journalist; little-known British fund Xyra Group Holdings; and Dubai's Premier Quality Foods.
However, many industry watchers now expect the chain to be broken up, with significant job losses and store closures. Stocks are running low as the retailer is short of funds and many of its suppliers have stopped sending goods, fearing they will not be paid.
While trading had improved in recent months, it is understood that Poundland was not generating enough cash to cover its requirements and would need considerable funds to support its continued operation.
Blake, who has worked on the shop floor of one of Poundland's Birmingham stores for 25 years, says the news has "put a downer on Christmas" but adds that he is "more worried about the future". Now in his early 60s, he reckons it will be impossible to find another full-time contract with another retailer if his store does not remain open.
He says the administration - after Gordon Brothers rejected at least two solvent offers for the retailer and said it would not provide further funding to keep Poundland afloat - came despite a massive staff effort to turn the business around.
"The number of people coming through the doors was increasing day by day," he said. "My store is a strong store."
However, several other workers said that they felt the collapse of Poundland was inevitable.
"We knew at some point this was probably going to happen," said one, who added the prospect of the store closing was "kind of worrying" but he hoped a potential buyer such as Tesco or the owner of Poundstretcher might snap up stores.
Referring to Gordon Brothers, he said: "They are doing everything legally right but not ethically right. Nobody can stop them."
Reports this week that Poundstretcher's US owners are lining up a sale suggest it is unlikely to rescue Poundland, though.
It is not only workers in stores that are affected. Hundreds of people work at Poundland's two distribution centres in Bryn near Wigan and at Harlow in Essex, as well as at the head office in Walsall, near Birmingham.
Nearly 300 people are understood to work at Poundland's eight-hectare (19-acre) site in the former coal mining village of Bryn in Ashton-in-Makerfield, within the prime minister's constituency. It opened in 2018, and is one of the biggest retail employers in the area.
Jo Welbourne, a regional organiser for the Usdaw union, said staff are "absolutely devastated", as many have worked at the site for a long time and live locally. "It's devastating at any time of year but particularly around Christmas."
A spokesperson for Gordon Brothers said: "Our thoughts are with Poundland colleagues. We recognise how difficult the continued uncertainty is for them. Efforts to preserve as many jobs and stores as possible will continue, led by the administrators."
The spokesperson said Poundland had "faced several years of instability" and Gordon Brothers had provided £85m in new finance "stepping in when no one else was willing to finance the business".
Still, about 2,000 suppliers will take a considerable hit as many have supplied stock which they will not be paid for and also have further money tied up in stocks or components to make items that they expected Poundland to buy.
Davey Martens, at independent confectionery supplier Byxa, said he was owed £68,515.20 by Poundland for products already supplied. Byxa's future would be at risk if it cannot recover "a meaningful amount" of the money owed, he added.
"This might not sound like an enormous amount," Martens said "But for a business like ours, it is. We can't simply absorb a loss of this size and carry on as though nothing has happened. The consequences could be devastating.
Another supplier said he was owed more than $1.2m (£915,000) for clothing items already in stores or stuck in port in the UK. He said like many other suppliers, he accepted a switch to longer payment terms of 120 days from 90 days, in the interest of helping Poundland, before Gordon Brothers said it wanted to sell the business.
The supplier is having to pay for storage in the port as Poundland does not have the funds to enable the release of the goods despite them selling well in stores.
"That is the part that is frustrating and upsetting - the small guys end up losing while big corporations make money," he said.
The prospect of Poundland disappearing from high streets was bad news for hard-up shoppers, too.
In Woking, Surrey, the potential loss of Poundland is clear to see in the local mall which already has a large boarded up space still fronted by signs for a former Debenhams while the former Wilko now houses a charity shop. Several more stores remain empty after the closure of the Goldsmiths jewellers, Topshop and the exit of several other names such as Poundworld and Carphone Warehouse over recent years.
Poundland's tills in Woking were busy but shoppers' views varied from "I love it" to "I never go there."
Several shoppers said they were "very sad" at the prospect of the store closing.
Christy Nucum, 52, says "I feel sad, because we go there because it is affordable. People are struggling nowadays because everything is expensive." She says she already also shops in Home Bargains, B&M and Aldi and loved Woolworths and Wilko before they closed and adds: "So many things are closing I don't know what is happening.
Emma Goodman said she visited before Christmas every year to pick up things she needed. She said if it closes she "would have to go to a big supermarket" but adds: " I do think you end up paying more in those kind of places."
Annice Biggs, who was in town with her husband, Chris, says she pops into Poundland every week for items such as chocolate and toiletries. "It's part of the high street and I feel like we are losing Woolworths again," she says, referring to the budget chain which shut down in 2009.
"All the well-known names are disappearing from the high street and people still want their high street."
Mark Sseba, 45, who is in town with Simon Clifton, 47, for whom he acts as a support workerr, says it is not that surprising that Poundland is closing as "a lot of stuff you get here you can easily get online and have delivered".
But he adds: "Of course it would be missed." Buying in person is often easier and more enjoyable for his clients and "going to a shop within your budget is good for the community and people with challenges. It helps to step out and buy something and walk around."
But Chloe Asher sums up the views of several shoppers in Woking who said they never or hardly ever visited Poundland.
"Poundland is kind of old fashioned. I just go to the supermarket," she says.
