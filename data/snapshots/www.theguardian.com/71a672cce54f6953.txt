Looking back, how do you regard your experience with Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas? And did you watch Terry Gilliam's version? AwapBamBoogers
No, I didn't watch it. I felt very sad about the whole thing because my wife and I were co-screenwriters. We went out to meet Hunter S Thompson and the poor guy was in a terrible state. He was hoovering up cocaine the whole time we were there. He had a great big TV screen, with a Post-it note saying: "Don't watch too much TV." We said we felt like his caseworkers, because all these guys in Hollywood were winding him up: "Dr Thompson, do something crazy, go out in the back garden and shoot a can of propane." That's all very well, but they were treating him like the freak in the county fairground. How many years was it after Fear and Loathing that the poor guy killed himself? He really needed an intervention. Instead, he was being encouraged by these Hollywood bastards.

What does Liverpool mean to you and how has it influenced your career? AmongstTheWaves
I was born just outside [in Bebington], so I'm not really a scouser. When Liverpool was awarded European capital of culture in 2008, it wasn't because of the Three Graces on the Pier Head. It was because of the incredible creativity of the people. Liverpool doesn't really look towards London, it looks across the Atlantic, because of the shipping, so it's always been very independent. It's a very interesting city, but, of course, I'm prejudiced.
How did Michael Nesmith from the Monkees help get Repo Man made? Melquiades
We were looking for somebody to executive produce the film and find the money, so I was introduced to him by another producer called Harry Gittes. He liked the script, partially because it had a four-page cartoon at the beginning, and he was very into comic books. Nesmith said to me: "Cox, I don't think you're going to have punk rock music all the way through. I want to see a variety of music." But one song at a time, we put in all these punk songs, and Nesmith went with it. He was a very cool guy.
Do you still agree with the line from Repo Man: "The more you drive, the less intelligent you are?" rewilder
That's something my wife said when we were still students at UCLA and, at the time, I did believe it. Now I think that if you're not relying on driver-assist technology, driving is an interesting occupation. It enables you to focus on something in the distance rather than staring at a screen in front of you. Right now I'm crowdfunding Repo Man 2, set in a world of driverless cars. Instead of sneaking down people's drives, hotwiring cars and running away, the repo man just sits at a computer, takes control and drives the car to the sheriff's substation or repo yard. It's a different world, but the same kind of malevolence.
Has crowdfunding become the best way to remain genuinely independent? NorthPixel27
For independent film-makers, yes, because the studios and the big streamers are really only interested in franchises and massive IPs. They want to spend a lot of money, but they don't really care what the film is. Often they don't read the script. In the case of Repo Man, it was obvious Universal hadn't read the script. It got greenlit because of some people Nesmith met in a bar. The people who will back a Kickstarter actually want to see the film.
If you were offered an unlimited budget to make a film on any subject, of any genre, would all that money be a hindrance rather than a help? AD2023
This is irrelevant because I'm never going to be given unlimited funds. But look at people who do have unlimited funds. You look at Christopher Nolan, and how boring his films are. I can't watch three-hour films unless they're works of genius. I don't watch those IP franchise films. Life is too short to waste on rubbish. I mean, what do I care about what some Hollywood nepo baby thinks about Batman? Bond and Le Carré are just propaganda against the Russians. The only really good film that I have seen recently is Flow, this Latvian animated film about what a cat and animals do after all the humans have disappeared. I watched it three times in a row.
Is it a coincidence that both Sid and Nancy and Dogs in Space use going away in a car as a metaphor for death? notvNZ
I haven't seen Dogs in Space. I think we were trying to find a happy ending, which is impossible for that story. John Lydon was right. I showed him the script and he said: "You should fictionalise this more. You've got this wonderful actor, Drew Schofield, playing me in your film. But Drew is a scouser, so he should play it as a Liverpudlian. You should take the film and put it at one remove from the Sex Pistols and it will be more interesting."
Lydon invited Drew to New York so Drew could study him and learn his ways. When the film came out, he said it was a nightmarish piece of garbage, which was very helpful because it created a controversy. He knew that, because the Sex Pistols were born from controversy.
I watched it again quite recently. It's a tough film. The ending really is a betrayal because we take the audience into this very dark place, then we release them. If I made it now, I'd have Sid Vicious dying in a pool of blood and vomit.
What are your memories of working with national treasure Kathy Burke? HenleyRegatta
Kathy Burke is fantastic. Everything she says in her book A Mind of My Own is true. She said: "Shane MacGowan used to call me stupid." I said: "He did that because he was intimidated by you." MacGowan used to call me "shit for brains", because he was intimidated by people who were more intelligent than he was.
I was originally asked to direct a video for A Pair of Brown Eyes, one of the Pogues' early singles. I became friends with the band, so they wrote music for Sid and Nancy and acted in Straight to Hell, which was a wonderful experience. I was also responsible for a terrible thing, because one night we were coming out of the pub, and Shane said to me: "Lend me £10 and I'll buy you a drink." I said: "Oh, bugger off," so he turned his back, walked into the street and got hit by a taxi, and was in hospital for months.
But, going back to the question, Kathy Burke is a person of great intelligence, great sensitivity. It's been a magnificent honour to have worked with her because I do agree that she's a national treasure and I love her very much.
What is your favourite murder weapon in movies? Mine's glue. bisqof
Well, the revolver is always the tidiest, isn't it? But when we were making Dead Souls, the producer, Merritt Crocker, said: "That's too easy, because it's too clean. My eldest daughter is studying media production and aiming to study film production at university. What advice do you have for her? IdoLoveaHat
Make friends with your colleagues and work with them after you leave school. The friends I made at Bristol film school and UCLA, and the students I taught at CU Boulder in Colorado, have become my collaborators. So make friends, collaborate. And when you leave school, stick together and collaborate because you're much stronger as a group than when you are alone.
