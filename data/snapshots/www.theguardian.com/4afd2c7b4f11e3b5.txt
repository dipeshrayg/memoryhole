Sainsbury's says households 'finding it tough' in run-up to Christmas
Sarah Butler
Sainsbury's has warned of a "pretty challenging" market in the run-up to Christmas as households are "finding it tough" and food producers face rising costs.
Simon Roberts, the chief executive of the UK's second biggest supermarket, called on the government not to "further aggravate" pressure on costs for business in this month's budget.
He said it was "important this government does not repeat what we have seen in previous budgets" in raising business rates and employers' national insurance contributions. "That's been really hard," he said.
After Sainsbury's and other industry leaders met government ministers last week, Roberts said he believed "this government is really listening to business" especially on helping to create more jobs for young people.
Roberts added that food inflation was not "anything like" some of the high levels forecast earlier in the year but suggested high energy and fertiliser costs could push up food prices next year.
Roberts added that in a more unsettled global environment the UK needed to "improve food resilience and grow and produce more at home". He called on the government to increase tax breaks that support investment in technology - including irrigation and reservoirs - on farms as the climate crisis is expected to lead to more hotter, drier summers such as this year's.
Shares in Sainsbury's slid 3.7% as the retailer revealed slower than expected sales growth of 2.6% in the six months to 12 September, with growth almost halving to 1.9% in the second quarter from 3% in the first. Strong online grocery sales - up 9% - and a 6% rise in sales of the retailer's premium own-label Taste the Difference range helped with growth.
Roberts said sales had slowed as shoppers had kept a tight rein on the number of items they bought amid a "focus on the cost of living" and had reined in spending on clothing and "big-ticket" items, such as furniture, at Argos. Sales at the catalogue shop were down 1.8% and Sainsbury's general merchandise and clothing sales were down 3.4% in the half.

Lorry drivers' blockades bring southern Italy to standstill
Lorenzo Tondo
French president Emmanuel Macron isn't the only European leader to be holding emergency meetings.
As lorry drivers' protests bring much of southern Italy to a standstill amid food and fuel shortages, with businesses warning they are on the brink of collapse, Italian authorities have launched a police crackdown on road blockades, reports our man in Palermo, Lorenzo Tondo.
The interior ministry said on Wednesday that 1,339 protesters had been identified, 71 reported to judicial authorities and 242 fined over what it described as "illegal road blockades". Police escorted hundreds of vehicles carrying goods, including 500 in Campania alone, while the interior minister, Matteo Piantedosi, warned of possible infiltration by criminal groups.
Industry associations in Campania and Puglia warned that businesses were on the brink of collapse, prompting the prime minister, Giorgia Meloni, to convene an emergency cabinet meeting.
Industry associations representing Naples and other parts of Campania and Puglia said in a joint statement:
The truckers' dispute requires an immediate response. Production has ground to a halt due to a lack of supplies. The strike must end; otherwise, the damage to consumers and businesses, which is already significant, will become unsustainable.
The protests began last week, with lorry drivers blocking roads and slowing traffic across the country, particularly in the south, following strikes and sit-ins by farmers and fishers.
The disruption has left supermarket shelves empty in several regions, with photographs showing entire aisles stripped of goods. Stocks at some supermarket chains have reportedly fallen to 50% of normal levels.
Italy's air traffic management agency, ENAV, said on Wednesday that Crotone and Reggio Calabria airports had run out of jet fuel because of supply disruptions.
According to estimates gathered by Confcommercio Sicilia, the five-day disruption to freight transport has caused between €40m and €50m in losses in Sicily alone, affecting supermarkets and the wider fresh food supply chain.
The protests threaten to deepen the crisis facing Meloni's government, which is struggling to contain the unrest. Measures announced so far by Rome appear to have done little to appease lorry drivers, prompting the government to take the controversial step of attempting to quell the protests through a police crackdown.
The ongoing protests in France create a vicious circle, ING's senior economist Charlotte de Montpellier warned.
The yield on 10-year French government bonds OATs has crossed the symbolic 5% threshold, while the spread over the German Bund has widened beyond 160 basis points, from 137bps last Friday.
These moves reflect both growing doubts about the ability of any future government to pass the necessary reforms and savings measures, and mounting concerns about the outlook for the French economy.
At the same time, the renewed deterioration in financial conditions is further weakening an already very poor growth outlook by increasing the cost of credit.
ING is forecasting GDP growth of just 0.3% in 2026 and 0.6% in 2027, but the risks are tilted towards another downward revision. De Montpellier added:
Against this backdrop, the government's objective of reducing the deficit to 5% of GDP in 2027 looks particularly fragile, as it is based on growth of 0.5% in 2026 and 1% in 2027, well above our forecast. Even if the draft budget were adopted in full, it might therefore not be enough, as weaker activity would automatically reduce the expected tax revenues.
French business confidence improves but unrest clouds outlook, with more protests planned
French business confidence unexpectedly improved in October, but this does not capture the impact of the latest social unrest, while higher interest rates, a weak labour market and worsening public finances are clouding the outlook further.
A closely-watched business confidence index edged up by one point to 97, but remains below its long-term average.
The main surprise came from industry. The manufacturing business climate index rose to 105 in October 2026, above market expectations and 101 in September, marking its highest level since July 2022. The improvement was driven mainly by overall order books, although they stayed negative (-11 vs -16 in September) and foreign order books (-1 vs -10), with the latter reaching its highest level since April 2022.
In the services sector, the business climate indicator rose one point to 99.
On prices, Charlotte de Montpellier, senior economist covering France and Switzerland at ING, noted that the survey provided no new inflationary signal. Intentions to raise prices declined in industry and remained stable in both services and construction. The employment climate indicator fell three points to 95, as hiring intentions worsened in the services sector.
The rebound is encouraging, especially after several months of disappointing industrial data. But it does not fundamentally alter the outlook. The indicator of perceived economic uncertainty rose sharply. In services, it even reached its highest level outside the Covid-19 crisis.
The survey was conducted between late September and early October. It captures the widening in French bond spreads, but not the violence and social unrest seen last weekend. "October's rebound is encouraging, but fragile and already partly overtaken by events," de Montpellier said.
The nature of the risk has changed. It no longer relates only to weak activity and the impact of higher interest rates, but also to the economic, political and financial consequences of social unrest.
The economic cost of the violence is not limited to broken shop windows and looting. Material damage represents only the most visible part of the bill. Closures, lower footfall and permanently lost sales could weigh far more heavily on activity.
But the real issue is persistence. A brief and localised episode would probably have a limited macroeconomic impact, while prolonged unrest could undermine confidence by encouraging households to avoid city centres, tourists to postpone their trips and businesses to delay investment. This could turn a temporary disruption into a more lasting economic shock.
The unrest also makes it even more difficult to secure parliamentary approval for the budget, at a time when the government has virtually no room to respond through additional spending or tax cuts. France therefore has neither the fiscal room to buy social peace nor the political room to impose the necessary adjustment easily.
More protests are planned this weekend, and the French president Emmanuel Macron - back from his tour of the Gulf - is hosting a security council meeting to try and put out the fires at home.
