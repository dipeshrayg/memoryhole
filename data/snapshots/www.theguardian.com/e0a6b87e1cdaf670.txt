Manchester United chief executive officer Omar Berrada has defended the club's managerial turnover and transfer strategy, insisting the Premier League side remains committed to long-term stability despite going through three permanent managers in less than three seasons.
Michael Carrick became the seventh permanent manager sacked by United since Alex Ferguson retired after delivering the club's 20th league title in 2013. He is also the third manager dismissed since Jim Ratcliffe became ‌a minority owner in ‌2024.
"If you think about the fact that we've had three permanent managers in ‌less than three seasons, clearly we haven't achieved the stability that we want to," Berrada told Sky Sports. "There are different circumstances as to why it didn't work out with Erik [ten Hag'], Ruben [Amorim'] and now Michael. And we're always learning. We're always trying to improve and understand what has failed."
Berrada defended the club's approach, saying United would continue to invest when suitable opportunities emerged but would not be pressured into overspending.
"We're actually happy with the players that we bought," Berrada said. "We need to stay disciplined. We need to buy the right players for ‌the right value at the right time. There's no point in us spending £40m or £50m for players that we're not convinced are going to add value to the team." Reuters

St Mirren's independent supports' association, Smisa, has been given a chance to cement its control of the club after the charity Kibble, founded in the 19th century and which defines its core purposes as "supporting at-risk children and young people (aged five to 26) across the UK", decided to sell its 27.5% stake in the club. Smisa, who already own 51%, are being given first option to buy their shares. This from the club's website:
Kibble became part-owner in March 2020 alongside SMiSA. The arrangement, a top-flight club owned by its fans and run in partnership with one of the country's biggest charities, is believed to be unique in European football.
Its aim was to use the club as a platform for Kibble's young workforce development programme. Since then, more than 200 young people have gained training, employment, education and support through the partnership. Shared use of the Marbill Coaches Stadium and Ralston training ground has given the charity's work a home at the centre of the Paisley community.
The club has stabilised over the same period, with turnover rising year-on-year. Its latest set of accounts for the year ending May 2026 is due to be published soon. Kibble's board believes its part in the club's story is complete and with St Mirren on a stable footing, the change in shareholding gives the club and SMiSA members the opportunity to consider how it grows from here.
Mark Macmillan, chief executive of Kibble, said: "When we joined SMiSA as part-owners in 2020, we wanted to show what a football club and a children's charity could achieve together. More than 200 young people have trained, worked, learned and found support through this partnership, and Paisley is better connected for it.
"We set out to do a job and we've done it. Kibble came in to make a difference for young people, not to be a long-term investor in a football club. With the club in a good place, now is the right moment to step back and give St Mirren the freedom to shape its next chapter."
St Mirren are currently eighth in the Scottish Premiership.

Overlap, underlap or sit back? How Premier League teams use their full-backs
Overlapping. Underlapping. Inverted. Traditional. The role of full-back has evolved dramatically. Once a defensive-first position that provided some auxiliary attacking support, the role has developed into something far more multifaceted.
Just ask right-back Ben White, who - with Riccardo Calafiori on the left - has been an important figure in Arsenal's superb start to the campaign. Talking a few years ago, he said: "Playing full-back the way [Mikel Arteta] wants is completely different to any other manager. To play full-back for him, you've got to be a centre-midfielder, a centre-back, a winger and a No 10."
Between them, White and Calafiori have contributed to six goals for Arsenal already in the Premier League this season (White: three assists; Calafiori: one goal, two assists). No other defender has registered more than three goal involvements so far. The Arsenal duo are a great illustration of the impact full-backs can have. But, with the position more demanding than ever before, how does each Premier League team deploy theirs?

Pochettino will have element of surprise an Anfield, says Iraola
Liverpool boss Andoni Iraola fears Mauricio Pochettino will have a tactical advantage over him on Sunday because the new Manchester United manager is coming in fresh.
Pochettino will be taking charge of United for the first time since leaving his role as USA national team coach to replace the sacked Michael Carrick.
With little to go on as to how Pochettino will setup his United side, Iraola is guessing.
"We've faced each other. Obviously he has a lot of Premier League games that we can look at, and we can look at games in the US, but they still have this small advantage," said Iraola. "We don't have references to prepare because they can go different ways that we don't actually know.
"The staff have been working with them, so they are going to arrive well prepared. We don't have the references that we have against other teams."
One advantage Iraola could have looked to exploit was the drone footage posted on YouTube of Pochettino's first training session at Carrington, but the Liverpool manager was quick to criticise the YouTube channel that posted the video.
"They were sending me the footage yesterday, but I think the important thing about this is that it shouldn't be legal. It happened to Manchester United but it could happen to me in a training session in the future," he said.
"It isn't about the advantage, it is the fact that there is a reason we train behind closed doors because there are some things that you have to respect."
United not planning legal action against drone operator
Manchester United do not intend to take legal action against a drone operator who filmed Mauricio Pochettino's first training session, but warned that anyone responsible for further incidents would face significant legal and financial risk.
The operator, who identifies himself as Colin but has not disclosed his full name, apologised earlier today for what he called a "total error of judgement" and removed the footage from his YouTube channel.
A club source told Reuters there ‌was no security breach at the Carrington site and that, while it was unclear whether the incident breached drone laws, United believed it had breached a number of other laws.

Fabian Hürzeler: 'I've never had the feeling of leaving. I'm not done'
Fabian Hürzeler, the Brighton coach, has given his press conference ahead of the game against Hull, and explained how, when and why he punishes players for not being sufficiently committed to their counter-pressing:
Counter-pressing is a law for us. Counter-pressing is not a decision you can do. It's something you can do when you have a good day, when you have a bad day, when you're in a bad mood, when you're ready for a game or training, you can do it every day. It's a non-negotiable for us. If you have the feeling the players are not on it then yeah, in some moments the players have to run. But we use it more in pre-season. As long as I'm happy with the counter-pressing we don't need it.
Hürzeler says he's not worried about drones taking footage of his training sessions. "No, because we have an airport next to us.
