Jensen Huang has thrown his hat into the ring to return to his role running Nvidia, less than a week after being ousted amid an alleged spy scandal.
Huang has issued a statement admitting making "serious mistakes" in his personal life - a nod to his previous admission of cheating on his wife, allegedly with a suspected Chinese spy.
He says:
I created problems for the Board that I deeply regret. I own my mistakes completely, and I am committed to cooperating with the Board's independent investigation.
But Huang also indicated he would be happy to return to his role, saying:
Since my departure, I've seen an outpouring of calls from Nvidia's employees, investors, business partners, and customers for me to return as CEO. Many continue to reach out to me directly as the company navigates complex technology, market, competitive, and geopolitical dynamics. NVIDIA is a once-in-a-generation company.
If the Board of Directors decides that my return is in the best interest of our shareholders, our employees, the company, and America, I would be honored to answer these calls and return as CEO.

Jensen Huang should not be reinstated as Nvidia's CEO, argues Jeffrey Sonnenfeld, the Lester Crown Professor of Leadership Practice at the Yale School of Management.
Writing in Time Magazine today, Sonnenfeld argues that Nvidia's board were wise to closed the door on Huang's return, and shouldn't let it be forced open again.
Sonnenfeld writes:
That door should stay shut. Over 33 years, Huang built the company at the center of the AI era, but in our view, his conduct nevertheless made his position untenable. He misled directors about undisclosed relationships and oversaw a firm no one could check-a breakdown in trust that was magnified in China, where he advocated loosening export controls even as restricted chips reached Chinese military units and a Shanghai facility tied to Alice Huang's network.
The political push to restore him rests on the billions his most prominent advocates have riding on his customer-financing web and on misleading geopolitical spin, at the expense of genuine shareholder concerns and national defense.
Sonnenfeld also argues that a board that can be overruled by a petition has "no function", concluding:
Nvidia's directors deferred to Huang for years and intervened late, and reversing course under outside pressure now would merely extend a habit of deference. Instead, the board should let its investigation finish, restore standalone Singapore revenue disclosures, and select a successor on the merits. Huang spent 33 years building an enterprise the AI era cannot do without. Now comes the defining test of a great company-whether it can outlast the person who built it.

Jay Aldebert, chief growth officer atInternational Services,says Nvidia is confronting several crises at once:
An employee rebellion: Growing internal pressure to bring Huang back, with warnings that critical AI talent could walk away.
A leadership vacuum: Can Kress reassure investors, customers and employees while the board searches for a permanent CEO?
A Wall Street confidence test: Will strong earnings be enough to stabilize the stock, or has the leadership crisis fundamentally changed Nvidia's valuation?
Washington and China: New scrutiny of Nvidia's China-related business and national security questions adds another layer of uncertainty.
The future of AI: Could a prolonged crisis at the world's leading AI chipmaker disrupt investment, innovation and the companies dependent on Nvidia's technology?
