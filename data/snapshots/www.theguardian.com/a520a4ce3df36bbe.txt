The FCA CEO has admitted that new rules meant to stamp out bullying and sexual harassment won't be enough to change working culture across the City.
The TreasuryCommittee pushed NikhilRathi for his views on how regulation would influence behaviour of bad actors still operating in the financial services industry.
Labour MP JulieMinns referred to the committee's 2024 Sexism in the City inquiry, which discovered there was "a very stark gender pay gap, harassment and widespread sexual harassment, bullying, misogyny persisted across the industry. It was a broken system of whistle blowing and widespread misuse of NDAs."
She asked whether FCA rules and guidance, including a recent decision to expand its it's so-called non-financial misconduct rules beyond banks, to hedge funds, asset managers and insurers was "ever going to be sufficient to change the working culture in this sector".
Rathi responded, saying: "No, not on their own."
He explained some cases would remain matters for the police and other authorities.
Rathi added:
We can't do it on our own. There's been other legislation that's come through and that's come into force, including for all employers around prevention of, of harassment. And at the end, the tone on this comes from the leadership of organisations."
Under the expanded financial misconduct rules, companies are expected to report any serious cases of non-financial misconduct to the regulator.
They're also required to pass on reports of bad behaviour - including racism, sexual harassment, violence and intimidation - to that person's potential future employer, to help prevent cases of "rolling bad apples", where rogue bosses move to new firms without consequence.

Rogue AI models may 'self-replicate' to hijack household devices, Anthropic says
Claude developer Anthropic has warned that rogue "AI worms" will soon be able to hijack household smart devices such as ovens and TVs.
The company said in a blog post on Monday that AI agents could be "months away" from "self-replicating" across "internet-of-things" devices, such as smart ovens and off-brand Android TVs.
It is the latest in a series of warnings by AI researchers and companies, as developments in the field continue at pace.
Anthropic said in a research post that it is "seeing signs of life for AI worms."
We don't yet have a full rogue replicator, but agents are becoming increasingly capable of completing the individual steps in the replication chain (and passing on their adaptations to future copies of themselves).
...It's uncertain exactly what form autonomous AI replication will take. It could be a slow, distributed "digital slime mold," living on relatively insecure compute. It could also be a powerful cyber model that breaches datacenters at a high pace, or a model that scams people for compute credits.
Smart devices in homes could be the first target for attacks as they are connected to the internet and historically have had minimal security measures in place, Anthropic said.

Court date set for legal challenge to FCA's £9.1bn motor finance compensation scheme
Kalyeena Makortoff
A court date has finally been set for a legal challenge to FCA's £9.1bn motor finance compensation scheme.
The Upper Tribunal has confirmed it will hold a hearing from 16 to 26 February 2027, which means consumers will continue waiting for compensation.
The scheme is being challenged by lenders - Volkswagen Financial Services, Mercedes Benz Financial Services, and Crédit Agricole Auto Finance - as well as a consumer group, ConsumerVoice.
The lenders and ConsumerVoice are coming at this from different angles (lenders arguing it is unfairly punitive, and ConsumerVoice arguing that it short changes victims), adding to the complexity of the legal challenge.
The FCA continues to insist that its scheme is "the quickest, fairest and most efficient way to compensate consumers" and has pledged to "defend it robustly."
However, the FCA is also warning lenders to prepare for the possibility that this could all fall apart, leaving them to handle individual claims through the court system, which could prove to be far more expensive and draw out the process even further:
Firms should plan for the possibility that, if the scheme or parts of it are quashed, there may be no motor finance compensation scheme and no complaint handling pause. In that scenario, lenders would need to be operationally and financially ready to resolve complaints through the usual complaint-handling process and within the usual statutory timescales.
We expect lenders to plan rigorously for this scenario, make the necessary financial provisions and engage with their auditors."
AI ranked as biggest global risk by insurers - ahead of climate change
Artificial intelligence is now ranked as the biggest global risk by insurance experts, according to the insurer AXA.
Climate change is still considered the biggest risk by the general public, with AI & big data ranked second, up from seventh last year.
But among insurance experts, AXA found that AI is considered the top risk, bumping climate change to second place.
AXA chief executive ThomasBuberl said that "living in a world of 'polycrises' has become the new normal".
For the first time, experts worldwide rank risks related to artificial intelligence and big data as their top concern, ahead of climate change. This does not mean that climate is less worrying - it remains the number one risk for the general population, with unprecedented levels of perceived vulnerability - but it shows that AI is now seen as a structural risk, on a par with major environmental and geopolitical threats.
Among both experts and the general public, 34% of AXA's survey respondents said they identified AI as the top risk because of issues related to human rights, bias and the misuse of data. AI's impact on employment was also a frequently cited reason, as well as the "black box effect" - the inability to understand or explain decisions made by AI systems.
AXA's findings echo a warning today from the IMF that the threat of an AI sell-off in stock markets is affecting the world's financial stability.
Athanasios Vamvakidis, the deputy director of the IMF's monetary and capital department, said:
The exposure of the rest of the world to US assets, particularly AI, is the highest it has ever been. So this is supporting the global economy through wealth effects today; but if we get a market correction it could have broad implications."
An AI crash was one of three "salient risks" to stability identified by the IMF, alongside rising government borrowing costs and the threat of a big cyber-attack.
It called this trio of factors "plausible near-term catalysts for a sharp tightening of financial conditions and market turmoil".
