The giant white dome that hangs over Phoebe Bridgers' stage looks like Disney World's Epcot, Walt's "experimental prototype community of tomorrow" envisioning how we might live better. Bridgers' tour is its own utopia: everyone's phones are locked away, an edict that caused some controversy among younger fans who have never experienced a gig any other way. Down our aisle, none of us know the time and two teenage fans wearing skeleton outfits (Bridgers' old stagewear staple) faux-complain that they've actually had to talk to each other.
It's so unusual not to see a speckle of screens at an arena show that, from the seats looking down on the standing area, it feels as if there's been some kind of power cut, or we're in a protected natural dark reserve. The decision seemed made to protect the sanctity of the pain in Bridgers' third album. Lost Weekend swims in her conflicted feelings over her sometimes-estranged father's death, given his abuse of her mother, and the end of Bridgers' fairytale engagement, after she realised she wanted something different.
The dome lifts to reveal a black-clad Bridgers and her nine-piece band, led by Melina Duterte of Jay Som, who play beautifully orchestrated, faithful versions of these songs. They tap into the Sufjan/National school of ornate crescendo, but nothing is being unduly dressed up here, or inflated to mirror Bridgers' stature as a generational, Grammy-winning songwriter and conspicuous influence on pop stars from Taylor Swift to Gracie Abrams.
What's miraculous is how Bridgers has transformed melancholy, silvery alt-rock into something that hits on the level of pop music, yielding feral screams from the crowd for Elliott Smith-worthy interiority. The production is the only pop thing about the show: later tilted high above the band's heads, the dome's concave insides turn into a sort of crystal ball, showing hazy visions of Bridgers, planets and black holes - maybe the alien, cosmic hugeness of a complicated and irreconcilable grief.
Bridgers' restraint in not trying to fill the 23,000-capacity room means that when she and the band really lock in, it takes your hair clean off. Kill Me goes from a stunned wisp to furious, slamming drums, prompting the dome to rise up, beaming spaceship-style lights from its rim. She moves to the B-stage for Lost Weekend's title track, sitting cross-legged on the floor at a small organ. When the bass floods and then breaks, the lights strobe Bridgers in white as she howls to the rafters: one minute she's there, then she's not, a faltering ghost. Glitter rains down for the belting climax of As Above, which feels something like being flayed.
With the exception of new-love anthem Bobby, these are not particularly cheerful songs (in case you couldn't tell by the pearlescent tears painted on Bridgers' face). You can imagine how she might have anguished about reliving their griefs and disappointments night after night. She's talked about her discomfort at being reduced to a sad-girl meme; some fans thought it was OK to harass her in the airport en route to her dad's funeral. She would have been well within her rights to put her guard up for life. Notably, she's given no interviews about this album.
But aside from the lasers and dry ice, the most striking element of the show is how purely happy Bridgers looks, more so than on any previous tour. She looks genuinely pumped at the huge singalong to Motion Sickness: given recent events, hearing 23,000 young people roaring along to a song denouncing emotional abuse by an older man, is worth getting pumped over. She legs it across the main stage, beaming at the nosebleeds. The phone ban seems as much about preserving the sanctity of her happiness from commodification.
Part of Bridgers' USP is that, for all her emotionally annihilating songs, she's extremely funny. When she asks who has conservative parents, she gets a fairly muted response. "That's much more universal in the US," she says, then nods to the gig's Manchester soil. "You guys have Morrissey. He's everyone's conservative parent." She says she's happy her UK tour means she can dodge Thanksgiving - "celebrating the genocide of people we stole the country from" - and says that for all her difference with her dad, she's grateful they shared those values. "I never had to teach myself critical thinking."
Alone on acoustic guitar, she plays Still Standing, her complex memorial to him, and the widespread sobbing is audible. It's manifestly a devastating song, but something about the lack of phones seems to heighten the response. Taking away fans' ability to turn away from hard feelings feels like a gift that forces a quality of attention: maybe the space theme hints at how disquietingly alien it feels to have nowhere to run, to see what comes up when you stare discomfort in the face. Bridgers has made her career doing it.
After the communal scream-fest I Know the End, she returns to the B-stage for an encore. Now dressed in white, she sings Scott Street sitting on the floor, and the house lights come on as everyone sways, arms in the air, looking giddily at one another after two hours of being locked in. The lack of video emerging from these gigs protects Bridgers' privacy, but if you're down to submit to the terms of her magic kingdom, you can see everything.
