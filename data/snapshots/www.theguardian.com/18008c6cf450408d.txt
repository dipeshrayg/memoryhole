At home, Amna is the modest and dutiful Muslim daughter of her parents' dreams. Online, armed with a webcam and an OnlyFans account, she is PersianBaby - an exoticised version of herself complete with fake accent, designed to entice men willing to part with their cash.
Pink Rabbit, writer-performer Farah Ashraf's provocative and darkly funny solo play, is a nuanced account of being caught between the private life of desire and the cultural expectations that shape how she is perceived. "I loved this idea of push and pull, and two different worlds," says Ashraf who began work on the play during her final year at the Oxford School of Drama, before which she had trained with the National Youth Theatre.
"At the time, I was really struggling and had an inner frustration with how I thought I was going to be perceived when I entered the industry and the roles that I might be limited to playing." Since graduating, however, Ashraf has appeared in roles across film, TV and stage, including in Netflix's The Witcher, Riz Ahmed's series Bait and in a lead role in the coming-of-age play Santi & Naz.
Her initial idea for Pink Rabbit centred on a young woman who appeared comfortable with herself but there was something clearly missing. A breakthrough came when she imagined that character in her bedroom, engaged in a scandalous online encounter, only for her mum to arrive unexpectedly at the door. Ashraf takes on the challenge of portraying both Amna and her ammi (Urdu for mother). Although the play is not autobiographical, Ashraf acknowledges some deeply personal elements. "A lot of Amna's soul is very much a mirror of me and my life," she says. As Ashraf herself has grown, so too has the production.
At its core, Pink Rabbit works to challenge the limited representations of south Asian and Muslim women that have historically dominated cultural narratives. Rather than presenting a single, uncomplicated experience of identity, however, Ashraf allows Amna to be contradictory, vulnerable and largely unpredictable. She is particularly conscious of the importance of sharing this perspective with audiences who may not have encountered it before. Reflecting on performing at the Edinburgh fringe this summer, where she was aware that she would be met with a predominantly white audience, Ashraf says: "There's some people sitting in there, and this might be their first experience of hearing this kind of perspective from a brown woman."
Ashraf also recalls feeling nervous about how older south Asian audience members might respond, particularly to the scenes involving Amna's online alter ego and her explicit interactions with her customers, as well as the prominence of her bright pink sex toy. "But overall, they were so open to the experience and the story," she says. By playing both Amna and Ammi, she brings the tensions between generations into sharp focus, resisting the temptation to position either as entirely right or wrong. The show's distinctive theatrical language further enriches the complexity of dual identity. Directed by Alessandro Babalola, Pink Rabbit draws on Ashraf's longstanding relationship with poetry, as well as her experiences growing up between London and Pakistan. Urdu and Punjabi feature alongside English, creating a rich linguistic landscape.
Gradually introducing prose allowed her to discover new ways of expressing Amna's emotional journey, retaining the rhythm and lyricism of poetry while giving other moments room to breathe. "There's something beautiful about theatre and how it allows for that."
During the play's Edinburgh run, Ashraf performed in a small, tightly packed space. It created a real intensity as she switched seamlessly from Amna to Ammi to PersianBaby's subscribers, Ashraf's physicality bringing to life the story both online and in the bedroom without the use of props, complex lighting or rigorous sound design. As Pink Rabbit prepares for its forthcoming Soho theatre run, Ashraf and her team are keen to experiment with lighting, the use of silence and "how to take everything up a notch, and be even braver with our choices. I always love to grow as an artist and challenge myself. The story is not changing, but we wanted to know where in that story we could add layers, or even take away layers."
She wants audiences to laugh, cry, question and perhaps recognise something of themselves in the characters. "Brown women are not monoliths," Ashraf says, articulating one of the play's most important messages. She hopes that anyone who has a complicated relationship with family or their wider communities will feel seen and heard, while others might leave with a new understanding of experiences beyond their own. "I want them to feel very much alive," Ashraf says, when asked about her audiences. "More than anything, I'd really love people to feel inspired to tell their own stories."
