When Nigella Lawson helps crown the new champion of The Great British Bake Off on Tuesday night, two other winners will also be celebrating: the show itself and broadcaster Channel 4. The fabled "Nigella effect" has freshened up a franchise that was beginning to taste distinctly stale.
Having replaced Prue Leith as judge for this 17th edition, alongside the ever-present Paul Hollywood, the domestic goddess has made the cake-making contest essential viewing again. Prompting a public outpouring of love, magisterial Lawson has proved the missing ingredient in the show's resurgence.
She has certainly been a raising agent in ratings terms. Terrestrial TV is in a state of managed decline nowadays but Bake Off has bucked the trend. Viewing figures overtook Strictly Come Dancing this series, with the first episode drawing 7.1 million viewers compared with 6.4 million for the BBC's Saturday night flagship's launch show. This might ring alarm bells in the ballroom. Perhaps the BBC needs to find a hoofing equivalent of Lawson to put some pep in its dance step.
Ahead of Tuesday's all-male, all-25-or-under grand final - doing doughy battle are law student Mo Mahir, political economy graduate Moyin Odeniran and hairdresser Tom Whittaker, a three-time Star Baker and the bookies' favourite - the new judge's debut run can be deemed a resounding hit.
After nine weeks in the tent, Lawson feels like a fixture. It's strange to think that she had to be persuaded to accept the job in the first place. The 66-year-old food writer was understandably nervous about joining such a beloved franchise, uncertain whether she had sufficient baking expertise.
In fact, media coverage has been overwhelmingly positive. Critics have hailed her as the "icing on the cake". As producers reassured her, Lawson was hired to talk about taste rather than technicalities. After she went viral for taking large forkfuls, she unrepentantly explained: "I like to be able to taste things properly. I'm an eater. Eating is my superpower." When Hollywood described a chocolate cookie as "indulgent and naughty", she shot back: "I don't approve of talking about food as naughty. We need to take our pleasures where we can in life."
She's passionate about flavour and conveys that sensory experience to audiences. Hollywood would happily admit that he's a master baker, not a natural wordsmith. By contrast, Lawson is a bestselling author and former literary editor with the vocabulary to match. She relishes wrapping her mouth around tasty descriptions almost as much as the treats themselves. When she purred the word "sumptuous" in her debut episode, co-presenter Noel Fielding said: "I think I might be pregnant."
Cakes have been "bejewelled with embellishments". Stout in a sponge added "a ferrous tang ... a savage bite". Spiced gingerbread showed "elegant restraint". Pasties were "plump little pillows". A fortnight ago, Lawson compared Mo's nougatine showstopper to "a Renaissance sculpture". Remember when her pronunciation of microwave as "mee-cro-wah-vay" was nominated for Bafta's TV moment of the year? Bake Off viewers went almost as wild when she rolled her Rs while pronouncing "Parmigiano Reggiano" and renamed Worcestershire sauce "Worcester shushter" ("we always call it that at home").
When she's not rhapsodising, Lawson imbues proceedings with a certain regal campness. She glides around the tent with an impish twinkle in her eye, greeting any innuendo with an arched eyebrow or "Boys, behave". She has serious star presence, bringing glamour, wit and poise to the home-spun contest. Her arrival has been accompanied by format tweaks, including an Audience Choice week, pantry-raiding challenges and baking from illustrations rather than recipes. After 16 years, it's helped rejuvenate the show.
Lawson's quotable utterances, hearty mouthfuls and reaction faces make her highly meme-able. For all 86-year-old Leith's qualities, being a social media darling wasn't one of them. The only time she went viral is when she accidentally tweeted her congratulations to the 2017 winner, six hours before the final was broadcast. Talk about a spoiler.
Lawson's relationship with the bakers is sweetly supportive. If Hollywood is their stern teacher, Lawson is their worshipped big sister. He issues firm handshakes; she dispenses warm cuddles. Yet she's no soft touch as a judge. She gives constructive criticism, generously sharing tips on how to improve. As has become a running joke, this often involves adding salt.
When a challenge involves one of Lawson's own recipes, she vibrates with pleasure and the bakers are desperate to impress. Several have hailed her as "an inspiration". They light up with pride when she praises them, gazing awestruck across their workbenches. Semi-finalist Gabe called her "iconic". Tom said he wanted a Nigella air kiss more than a Hollywood handshake.
Before the series aired, tabloids stoked rumours of tension between the judging duo. On the contrary, Lawson and Hollywood have fizzing chemistry. More than her predecessors Leith and Mary Berry - as he says, he's gone "Dame, Dame, Queen" - Nigella gives ol' blue eyes a run for his money. Their back-and-forth veers from mildly flirtatious to spiky differences of opinion, often within the same conversation. She teasingly calls him "The Professor" or "Mr Precision", and promised to beat him in an arm wrestle. If this was a romcom, they'd be a textbook will-they-or-won't they couple. (Rest assured, they won't.)
When Lawson's casting was announced, she correctly called Bake Off a "national treasure". Well, she should know. With her first-name recognition factor and dazzling full-beam charm, Queen Nigella has single-handedly given the show a new lease of life. Applications to enter next year's contest are said to be "booming". Channel 4 bosses are so happy, they've signed her up for a second series, reportedly for £500,000. Long may she reign.
