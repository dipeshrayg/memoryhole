Great Britain's oldest renewable energy projects are likely to rake in a near £9bn windfall next year because of soaring market prices, according to independent analysis.
The research has revealed that almost a third of the UK's wind and solar farms that rely on the government's legacy renewables subsidy scheme are in line for soaring subsidy payments as officials grapple with plans to lower the country's energy costs.
Renewable energy projects built between 2003 and 2014 are expected to claim £4.5bn in subsidies over the next financial year, on top of £4.2bn in wholesale market revenue. The analysis showed developers will receive almost 50% more than before Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine sent gas prices soaring in 2022.
The analysis by the Energy Competitiveness Unit (ECU), a non-partisan research and policy project, said the legacy support scheme - which is paid via taxes and energy bills - must be reformed "if we are serious about getting costs of the electricity system down in the short and medium term".
Unlike newer renewable energy projects that earn fixed-price subsidies for their generation, older projects access support via the renewables obligation (RO) scheme, which provides payments on top of what developers earn via the wholesale market, meaning that higher gas market prices lead to higher subsidy costs for renewables.
The scheme was credited with kickstarting the UK's renewable energy industry when it was put in place almost 25 years ago, but the ECU said it "has been allowed to persist unsustainably".
Andrew O'Brien, the ECU director, told the Guardian that the rise in wholesale prices over recent years has meant that many renewable energy developers are earning "well above what they could reasonably have expected" when first taking the decision to invest in the projects.
The government announced last year that it would remove three-quarters of the RO scheme's costs from home energy bills, and move these levies into general taxation from April 2026 to help temper the steep rise in energy bills.
The government later warned that legacy generators could face higher windfall taxes unless they give up the existing RO scheme in favour of a new partially fixed subsidy contract as part of its plans to protect bill payers.
The ECU believes that the government's consultation on plans to fix the generators' wholesale revenues to help to weaken the link between volatile global gas markets and the UK's electricity costs "falls short". It argues ministers should go further to fix their subsidy payments and create a new scheme.
Since 2013, renewable energy developers have been required to compete in an auction to secure a contract for a fixed price for each megawatt-hour of electricity generated, known as a contract for difference (CfD), which means costs remain fixed regardless of global markets.
Under an alternative proposal set out in the ECU's research, the government could cut electricity costs by £3.8bn next year, and £9.2bn over 10 years, if all generators moved on to an entirely fixed-price contract, which it calls a fair value CfD.
The new private law contracts could also offer a degree of legal protection against the political risk that any potential future net zero sceptic government could dismantle renewable energy support through the government's RO scheme, a threat signalled by the rightwing populist Reform UK party.
"It is in the interests of everyone - investors, generators, businesses, households and government - to get a credible long-term plan in place that can withstand shocks in this increasingly uncertain world," the ECU said.
In his budget speech last month, the chancellor, John Healey, said: "We will further break the grip of international gas prices on our bills by moving more of our old renewables on to fixed-price contracts."
Tara Singh, the chief executive of RenewableUK, said the renewable energy projects were still helping to keep a lid on bills by "pushing expensive gas off the system".
Singh said: "UCL found wind has saved consumers £100bn since 2010 even allowing for support schemes like the RO by reducing the need for expensive imports."
She added that the government's windfall tax, which took effect from July, has meant that renewable energy projects using the RO scheme pay the Treasury up to 80p of every additional pound it earns from market prices above £82.61 per megawatt hour.
"To put this into context, the average wholesale price of electricity over the last 12 months is over £100 per megawatt hour, and frequently higher than that," she said.
"The fastest way to protect consumers from future price shocks is to build more cheap, homegrown renewable power."
