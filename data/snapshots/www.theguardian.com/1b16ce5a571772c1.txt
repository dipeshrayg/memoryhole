Mike Leigh and his partner, the actor Marion Bailey, were knocking around at home last Christmas, looking for a good film to watch and constantly coming up short. Finally, Leigh had a brilliant idea. Why not put on Topsy-Turvy, his 1999 costume romp about the making of The Mikado? Problem solved, perfect choice. "It's not like a habit," he says hurriedly. "I don't sit there watching my films every night like Gloria Swanson in Sunset Boulevard. It's just that Topsy-Turvy happens to be a very enjoyable watch."
The way Leigh sees it, his films are like children. They've outgrown him, moved on, but it's nice to check the family ties are still strong. Some are sunny (Life is Sweet; Happy-Go-Lucky) and some are dour (Naked; Hard Truths), but they all share the same DNA, the same real-world interests and tragicomic mindset. He can't choose between them; he'll defend each one to the death. Then he pauses and says, "But I've got a soft spot for Meantime."
Leigh was 28 years old when he shot his first feature, Bleak Moments. He turned 83 on the set of Tender Loving Care, his 17th. That's a family to be proud of, practically his own dynasty, but his latest, youngest child is very likely his last. Leigh blames his myositis, an inflammatory condition which has weakened his muscles and restricted his movement. He's not retiring, he insists: "I'm going to sit down and write something." The physical demands of directing, however, are beyond him these days.
I can't pick favourites either, his films are all of a piece - full of humdrum magic, broken saints and cracked clowns. Tender Loving Care nods to Career Girls with its focus on female friendship and to Another Year in its portrait of a happy older couple while picking its own course through stricken modern-day London. Kate O'Flynn and Alice Bailey Johnson (Marion Bailey's daughter) star as Amy and Rosie, two social workers with a mountain of casework to tackle. Paul Jesson and Bailey play Amy's aged parents. Tender Loving Care's characters are all coping as best they can, self-medicating with white wine and singing to buoy their flagging spirits. Leigh's film is about the ways people love and help one another; how wonderful that is and how it is never quite enough.
It's a dark November day when I meet Leigh at his film distributor's office in King's Cross, London. He's in a chair by the door, swaddled in a gilet. His snow-white beard is parked on his chest. His walking frame is stowed nearby. He brusquely apologises for not getting up. King's Cross was the setting for his 1988 film, High Hopes, with its gasworks and grungy Victorian estates. Now it's a place of glass-fronted office blocks, shops and restaurants. "We are literally sitting in Cyril and Shirley's old flat," he says. "It was right here, now it's gone. It has all been swept away."
At least some things remain constant. Leigh has been working in the same hand-tooled style since Ted Heath was PM, with each film the result of a rehearsal period that lasts months. He has his actors build characters and sketch scenes and then runs his pictures to ground, like a trained hunting dog chasing a scent on the breeze. "We start with improvisation, distil it down to a precise piece of action and then shoot," he says. "It's like digging the foundations, or mixing the paints."
Film-making is such a rigidly hierarchical affair. Leigh makes the process sound collaborative, inclusive, even borderline democratic. It's as though he's not the director so much as the facilitator, the guide, the conductor of an orchestra.
"Well, yes, all those things," he agrees. "Also the author. The dramatist."
The host of a salon? "Well, I don't know about that," he says, wincing. "You're slightly devaluing it now."
Leigh's method has paid off. He's won the Palme d'Or, three Baftas and the Venice Golden Lion. But it breaks the standard business model, which has made it hard to raise cash. He has no script to show off and refuses to work with big stars. Some investors, he says, might give him a pass on the script. But they all want a big name: it's the A-listers that sell movies.
Why not cast a name? How bad can it be? Leigh loves his troupe of British character actors (Jim Broadbent, Ruth Sheen, Lesley Manville, Timothy Spall). But Hollywood stars on occasion can be good actors as well. Many, I suspect, would have loved to work with him.
"Why not cast a name?" Leigh snorts at this suggestion; he's simultaneously tickled and nettled. "Well, first of all let's clear up what's involved in doing that. You - the name actor - would have to commit yourself to the project for months on end. You may have to get out in the real world and do some research. You may not be playing a character that squares with your idea of how you want to be seen; the idealised version of your own personality. So in the end it's a distraction. It's starting from the position that the film is a vehicle for the actor, which is not a position I like. It's not on. It's dangerous. And yes, it might be all right, but the premise is wrong." He shakes his head. "I mean, if someone were to say, 'The reason you fucked up and failed is because you didn't get star names', then in all humility I'd accept it. But that simply isn't the case."
Making films has grown harder; he's not denying the fact. Tender Loving Care had to be cobbled together at speed on a minuscule budget - and by then he had other hurdles to contend with. He didn't realise at the time that this would be his last film. "That's come this year," he says shortly. "Because this condition I've got, myositis, is just slowly getting worse. The shoot was OK. Everyone was very helpful. I had to be moved around a bit. But since then I've been having more issues with mobility."
Obviously, ideally, he would like to continue. Might that be possible? Leigh is such an unorthodox director that he can surely make a film any way that he chooses. He can be as active and present in the process as he wants. He can direct through a proxy; he can direct from a wheelchair.
I realise I'm haplessly casting about for solutions. It's driving him up the wall; he can't shut me up quick enough. "Yeah, sorry," he says, chipping in. "I'm now very seasoned in listening to everybody in the world - including you - telling me why and how I can keep working. And my answer to that begins with an 'F' and ends with an 'off'."
Probably every decent artist works within limitations. They need money to buy materials, their bodies to stay the course, and their grandest, boldest visions often remain unrealised. In the past Leigh has managed to raise funds for such period productions as Topsy-Turvy, Mr Turner and Peterloo. But he would have loved to have made a big budget modern-day film as well.
"That's the main thing that I will always regret," he tells me. "The thing I wanted to do is now just not going to happen. A broad canvas and a contemporary subject. But no one will buy it. No one will wear it."
A few years ago he had a terrific idea for a film. It was about a group of friends from different ethnic and racial backgrounds. The drama would cover their interactions in the present day, but it would also flash back to show them as their grandparents or ancestors arriving in the UK. He says: "We put it together as a feature. I even wrote a document. And no one was interested. Then a producer friend said that if we repurposed it as a television series the BBC would jump on it. So we took it to the BBC. We had a meeting with the head of content and her team and she turned it down flat. She said: 'We don't know what we're going to get.'"
The memory of the meeting still sticks in his craw. He says: "Well, I'm sorry, but with all due respect, with me you never know what you're going to get."
He was never what was ordered; his parents couldn't work him out. Leigh was brought up in Salford, the son of a GP, listening to the coughing of patients in the downstairs surgery. It was a happy enough childhood, so far as it went, but his father was horrified when he turned towards drama, dismissing his interest as "the moonings of a stage-struck girly". Nobody acted or directed in Salford back then. Ideally, God willing, they became doctors and lawyers instead.
"Well, my old man was respectable," Leigh says. "And he'd just been through the war. I've still got to find out exactly what he went through. He was a medical officer in southern Africa, so he and I didn't see each other until I was a year and eight months old. So there was all that between us." He pauses. "Also, if I'm honest, I think it was a slightly homophobic stance. He didn't want me to be a pansy, so there was all that stuff as well. Anything theatrical automatically meant you were gay."
Salford, he says, felt so small in those days. London was the magnet, the beautiful south, Shangri-La. He had a habit of catching the night train down to Euston. There was a leftwing coffee house that he loved on Old Compton Street, he recalls, while the Gyre and Gimble coffee bar stayed open round the clock. He could sit there for hours and natter with his comrades. He could study each new arrival; eavesdrop on their conversations. "It was fantastic," he says. "It made me feel I belonged."
He likes his films, is that a crime? Topsy-Turvy, Meantime and all the rest of them, too. He says he's met so many directors who want to disown their own work. "They say, 'No way, I won't watch it', because it was interfered with, or they had to cast someone they didn't want to cast, or the producers fucked up the ending, or it went to a test-screening in Hollywood, all that stuff. And frankly I've never had any of that. Every film that I've made has been made without interference. And I have the privilege of not hating any of them."
Our interview is over. The man is not getting up. I tell him that his films have meant a great deal to me. I'm glad that he likes them; it's a good position to be in. It might even be the definition of a life well lived.
Leigh's gaze briefly narrows. It is as though he's inspecting the compliment for a double edge, hidden snares. Then he half-nods and half smiles. He says: "Well then, there you go."
