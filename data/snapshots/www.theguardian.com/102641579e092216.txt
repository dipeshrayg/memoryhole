Sometimes, people who have lied lie about whether or not they've lied. They even, on occasion, fail to tell the truth despite having sworn to do so - in court, for instance, or staring deeply into the eyes of someone who is sitting right there.
Is that a shock? Probably not. We all engage in versions of such behaviour every day. We understand that the current climate of mutually clueless echo chambers helps claptrap gain traction. But we can still recognise the difference between little white lies and big red flags. We know that the moment anyone starts using the phrase "my truth" it's time to cancel the wedding.
You Can See Everything acts as if this isn't the case. It operates in a realm of smart and artful naivety. In order to interrogate whether or not Elizabeth Holmes, founder of magical medical tech company Theranos, is - as she claims - the tragic victim of a miscarriage of justice, Nathan Fielder moves into the annexe of her home for a few weeks before she starts her 11-year prison term for fraud.
"You can see everything," she promises him brightly, juggling babies and her godawful boyfriend. Fab access! A gift to the documentarian! So Fielder duly blinkers up and blinds himself to anything concrete. He does not discuss the court case. He does not examine the reams of actual evidence that resulted in her conviction. Beyond a long chat about the semantics of the word "can", he does not ask Holmes what the hell she was playing at.
Instead, his technique is an extended vibes check. He looks into Holmes's eyes, noses nearly rubbing. He asks her if she was lying. He does this a couple more times, just in case.
This is undoubtedly compelling (for a while, at least). The fine line between profundity and banality means a lot of the movie, with its pore-boring closeups, is pretty gripping.
But it's also perverse and dangerous. You Can See Everything rejects the idea of a generally agreed consensus over what has happened - even as an aim, and even at a time when that concept is under considerable threat.
In its clever, meta way, Fielder's film therefore presents the most pernicious attack on objective truth I've seen - precisely because it's so liberal and slippery.
Rather, the film represents a massive slap in the face for the judicial system, as well as for Tyler Shultz and Erika Cheung, the primary Theranos whistleblowers. Plus all the journalists who meticulously investigated the case over many years, including John Carreyrou and Amy Chozick, whose New York Times reporting is not covered by the film, except to dispute one barely-relevant element of it.
It is also an insult to the victims of the Theranos scandal. Not just the millionaires who lost money but the many, many people who suffered because they were fed false medical results by Holmes's dodgy box.
That background means the film's soapiness can stick in the throat. The more we're encouraged to wonder whether Fielder might fancy Holmes a bit, and maybe it might actually be mutual, the more the real-world consequences of her actions seem to hover unhelpfully over footage of them flirting.
A fascinating and bleak fraud case is digested into a thin vehicle for dopey ponderings. Things like: are you for real? Am I for real? Can you ever really tell if anyone's for real?
And that, I think, is one of the reasons the film has been so successful. Just like the rest of us, Fielder seems uninterested in facts - he's all about the feels. We all appear to believe that the really essential truth now is an emotional one.
You Can See Everything taps the zeitgeist like few movies before it. For me, that's what makes it so frightening.
