The personal scenes in this film from Rachel Fleit have been carefully controlled and curated, the privacy of both parties being important, for different and understandable reasons. But it is an unexpectedly intimate, emotional piece of work, a big-hearted odd-couple story, which is perhaps slightly more complex than at first appears.
Emma Fogarty is a smart, garrulous woman from Abbeyleix in County Laois in the Republic of Ireland who has EB: epidermolysis bullosa, a rare condition which means that her skin is permanently, agonisingly painful and requires a daily calvary of bandaging and re-bandaging. While getting an award at the People of the year event in 2010 for her campaigning work educating the public about this illness, Emma met and struck up a friendship with Colin Farrell, a supporter and patron of the EB charity Debra Ireland.
Their relationship continued for 15 years and in 2025 Farrell agreed to run the Dublin marathon for EB, with the final two miles pushing Emma in her modified wheelchair; a serious physical challenge for any runner. The film shows Emma and Colin in their different worlds preparing in their various ways for the marathon. (They are in fact only shown physically in the same space in the film's final section.) But the film also shows a candidly emotional Farrell talking about his father, Eamon, who was on his deathbed in Ireland at the time, and talking also about his eldest son James, who was born with Angelman syndrome, similar to cerebral palsy.
Clearly, the ebullient Farrell in his middle years is going through something; coming to terms with his recovery from alcohol and substance abuse, with his dad's final weeks and days, with their difficult relationship in his childhood, and with what his own fatherhood means to him. Emma is helping him get through it. (There is a funny, if somewhat alarming sequence in which Colin lays out the huge store of crisps and chocolates he intends to devour after the marathon and grins: "I can't get sugar from alcohol any more!") Perhaps it might have been interesting to put more personal questions to Farrell directly, but Fleit might well have felt that this was too obvious. At all events, this is a very touching and worthwhile double portrait.
