The imminent arrival of Jesse Eisenberg's new film The Debut should be cause for celebration. Not necessarily because it is good - although its star Julianne Moore seems locked into an Oscar nomination at minimum - but because it means that Jesse Eisenberg has to do press for it. This is always a treat.
While the bulk of big magazine profiles about film people involve journalists breaking their spines to make the subject sound funnier or more profound than they really are, that is never particularly necessary with Eisenberg, because he appears to be a genuine oddball.
For instance, the new profile about him in the New Yorker is brimming with endless weird little details that you would never expect to read in a piece about, say, Matt Damon. For example, he says that a Freudian analyst diagnosed him with something called "arousal disgust", a complex where simply liking something brings about waves of self-hatred. He donated a kidney to a stranger. He wrote to the Directors Guild of America to ask that his name be taken off the opening titles of The Debut, not because he disowns it but because it felt needlessly flashy.
Aaron Sorkin, meanwhile - and you have to assume that this was meant as a compliment, compared Eisenberg to Holden Caulfield, the character who inspired Mark Chapman to murder John Lennon.
But by far the most interesting nugget from the profile concerns Eisenberg's relationship with his fans. "People are mostly fine," he says. "It's because there's no sexuality to my work. I think once the public is asked to want to sleep with a person onscreen, that person's life is fundamentally different."
This deserves some interrogation because, on the surface, it's a statement that presents itself like a great unspoken truth. Of course there aren't hordes of lusty fans pulling at his clothes all the time, because he doesn't play the sort of character that attracts those people. If you are a leading man heart-throb like Leonardo DiCaprio, then you probably have to learn how to protect yourself from people who look at you like a randy cartoon wolf or, more specifically, Jeff Bezos' wife.
But Jesse Eisenberg tends to make smaller films, where he plays superficially asexual characters. He's very good at playing a child of divorce. He's undeniably great at playing clever outsiders. And if we're talking characters with no sexual magnetism whatsoever, you need to remember that Eisenberg repeatedly plays a stage magician in film.
However, some wider context is probably necessary. In the profile, Eisenberg was specifically talking about fans interrupting him when he's writing, this is something he does during office hours at a New York public library branch. Immediately this means that we can narrow the focus a little, because it is clear that by "fans" he is describing a very narrow subsection of fans who happen to be visiting public libraries during weekday office hours. Not to generalise too broadly, but this doesn't feel like a particularly sexually incontinent demographic.
And, besides, in terms of roles he has played, Eisenberg is just plain wrong. There is plenty of sexuality to his work. In Richard Ayoade's adaptation of Dostoevsky's The Double, he plays a twin who brims with sexual confidence throughout. In the series Fleishman is in Trouble, he plays a doctor who has so much casual sex post-divorce that it has to be condensed into a montage. And in the play The Spoils - which Eisenberg wrote for himself a decade ago - his character admits to having scatological fantasies about his friend's girlfriend.
Most damningly of all, there is the very famous interview he did with Romina Puga in 2013, which is one of the most sexually charged things you will ever clap eyes on. From start to finish, the interview is a cavalcade of flirtatious bullying in both directions, like they're determined to neg each other into bed. Of course, we should remember that Eisenberg was a bit too relentless in this regard, and in a subsequent post entitled "Jesse Eisenberg isn't very nice", she describes how the encounter left her feeling humiliated.
But still, it blows a hole clean through Eisenberg's argument. People aren't nice to him because he isn't sexual in movies, they're nice to him because he makes small, clever films enjoyed primarily by people who are too shy to barge in on him when he's clearly working in a library on a Thursday. Now, let this be the end of it, because I don't want to convince Jesse Eisenberg that people fancy him any more than I absolutely have to.
