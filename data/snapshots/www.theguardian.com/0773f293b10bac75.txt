HSBC is handing $2.7bn (£2bn) to shareholders after a 32% surge in profits, that will further assure the Treasury that the UK's top banks can shoulder a tax hike in the budget, my colleague KalyeenaMakortoff reports.
The London headquartered bank reported this morning that profits grew nearly a third to $9.6bn in the July to September quarter - slightly below consensus estimates - helped by higher interest rates, lower loan losses and smaller legal provisions compared to last year.
It meant the bank could hand $2.7bn to shareholders. That included another $1bn share buyback, as well as an interim dividend of $0.1 per share, that the bank said would mean distributing another $1.71bn to its investors.
Net interest income - the difference between what banks charge for loans versus pay out for savers' deposits, rose to $9.3bn from $8.8bn year on year. However, that was apparently lifted by its hedging strategy and a growth in deposits in Hong Kong, while UK deposits stayed flat.

The monthly consumer morale gauge produced by statistics body INSEE fell from 85 points to 80 this morning, below forecasts.
INSEE said "household confidence has strongly worsened" this month, with people more pessimistic about their personal financial situation
The proportion of households believing it is a good time, in the current economic situation, to make major purchases fell, as did gouseholds' opinion on their future ability to save.
Accountancy firm EY UK has lifted the pay of its partners by over 10% to £870,000.
The increase is twice as large as the 5.2% average pay rise for EY employees last year.
EY's partners are paid from its distributable profits, which rose to £702m before tax in the last financial year, up from £679m.
The firm has also boosted its staff bonus pot by 25%, and reported reported that its UK fee income rose by 2% to £3.86bn in the financial year ending on 3 July 2026.
Anna Anthony, EY UK & Ireland regional managing partner says:
"These are a solid set of results in a challenging market. We've continued to grow the business, while making significant investments in talent and technology to support our future growth and deliver high quality and innovative services to our clients.
"In FY26 alone, we hired 2,300 people across all parts of the UK, made over 3,700 internal promotions and progressions and announced a significant evolution of our global Alliance with Microsoft.
"We also recognised the contribution and performance of our people with our largest variable pay pool to date, whilst continuing to invest in training and development, with over 1.2m learning hours completed.
"With economic uncertainty, technological transformation and shifting expectations of business reshaping every sector, I'm proud of the steps we've taken to position our firm for the future while continuing to deliver for our clients, people and the communities we operate in."
Britain issues costliest five-year debt since 2008
Britain has successfully sold more government debt this morning, but at the highest price since the financial crisis almost two decades ago.
The Debt Management Office sold £4.5bn of 5-year debt at an average yield of 5.036%, the highest since 2008, Reuters reports.
There was plenty of interest in the gilts - perhaps not surprising, given the lucrative yields the DMO was prepared to pay. Investors bid for 3.51 times as much debt as was available, allowing the DMO to pick the best offers (ie, the investors offering to accept lower yields).
The jump in interst rates which has boosted bank profits is also weighing on the housing market.
Zoopla has reported this morning that 35% of UK homes are in postcode areas where house prices are falling, up from 12% a year ago.
These price falls are "concentrated in London and the South East", it says, where 85% of homes are in local markets with price falls.
This trend is due to higher mortgage rates which have increased the cost of buying and made buyers more selective - the number of sales agreed over the last four weeks is seven per cent lower than a year ago across the UK.
Richard Donnell, executive director at Zoopla, says,
"The extra cost of mortgages has made home buyers more selective while greater choice of homes for sale has increased the scope to negotiate on price. The impact is being felt by sellers in southern England where house price falls are most concentrated as average prices continue to rise across the rest of the country.
"There is still demand to buy homes, and those that are well priced and presented are selling just as quickly as last year. Getting the price right from the start is essential. Overpricing significantly reduces the chances of selling, so sellers should seek advice from local agents on buyer demand and the right asking price for their home."
Record 738,000 UK businesses in 'significant' financial distress ahead of the Budget
Although HSBC has been raking in the profits, smaller businesses are finding life rather tougher.
The number of UK businesses in 'significant' financial distress has hit a record high of 738,144, according to the latest Red Flag Alert research from financial group BTG. That's the highest level since the report began in 2004.
BTG found that the highest volumes of firms strugging operate in support services, construction and real estate and property services.
Julie Palmer, managing partner at BTG, says its a worrying sign ahead of the budget:
"Record levels of 'significant' financial distress underline just how many businesses are approaching the Autumn Budget with limited financial headroom. While firms will naturally be looking for clarity on taxation and economic policy, many of the pressures they face extend well beyond any single Budget announcement.
"My real concern is for consumer-facing sectors such as leisure and hospitality, particularly as we enter the run up to the vital Christmas trading period. Higher employment costs, rising inflation and interest rates will add real-world pressure to many domestic budgets. Add into that a forecast 26% rise in the January 2027 energy price cap and households are likely to concentrate on the essentials.

Kaur suggested some reasons:
"I think in a high interest rate environment, there's sometimes a market expectation that we upgrade guidances or we change our targets, and particularly from a banking NII perspective. So that's all I can think of."
Here's Will Howlett, financials analyst at QuilterCheviot, on the drop in HSBC's shares this morning:
"HSBC's third-quarter results have fallen a little short of market expectations. Revenue was weaker than anticipated, with pressure on net interest income and a slowdown in wealth, and that disappointment has translated into a slide in the share price.
"The numbers themselves are not disastrous. Profitability remains strong, with an 18.2% return on tangible equity, while loan losses were better than expected and HSBC has reiterated its target of delivering returns above 17% over the next three years. The bank is also returning another $1bn to shareholders through a buyback.
"The issue is that HSBC has set a high bar for itself. Following a strong run for the shares, investors have become accustomed to robust results, so even relatively modest misses can prompt a sharper reaction. Today's fall therefore looks more like disappointment against high expectations than a significant deterioration in the underlying business."
The City is not impressed with HSBC's financial results, having expected even higher profits.
Shares in HSBC are down 6.7% in early trading, and appears to be pulling the rest of the banking sector down too.
That's because the 32% jump in profits is slightly below analyst forecasts (although $9.6bn should hardly be sniffed at!).
Chris Beauchamp, chief market analyst at IG, says:
"You don't usually get fireworks from HSBC results, and these are no exception. There are signs of some improvement overall, and a boost to the buyback is welcome, but it's clear why the market wasn't really inspired by these numbers.
HSBC seems to be doing ok, but ok doesn't really cut it in a world where inflation is back on the menu and growth may start to slow. At least it's not exactly rolling in profits this time around, providing some useful air cover ahead of a UK budget that will may include another raid on banks."
How cost-cutting and lower legal bills boosted HSBC's profits
One strong argument for higher bank taxes is that the financial sector has been benefitting from the increase in interest rates in recent years.
That has allowed them to swell their net interest income (NII) by raising the rates charged on borrowers by more than those paid to savers.
However, NII is only a small factor behind the jump in HSBC's profits in the last quarter. NII rose by 6%, or $500m, to $9.3bn - which HSBC attributes to "deposit growth and the benefit of reinvestment of our structural hedge at higher yields".
HSBC also profited from a cost-cutting drive - operating expenses fell by 12%, or $1.3bn, to $8.8bn.
It also benefitted from a $1.4bn drop in legal provisions - this was the sum it set aside last year to cover a claim related to Bernie's Madoff's Ponzi scheme.

TUC: HSBC profits make it "blatantly obvious" banks can afford more tax
The TUC are also urging the Chancellor to increase taxes on banks in tomorrow's Budget, arguing the sector could pay tens of billions of pounds more.
Rather than simply reversing Jeremy Hunt's cut to the bank surcharge from 8% to 3%, the TUC argue that the surcharge - additional corporation tax - should be increased further.
That would provide funding to pay for a social energy tariff that brings down energy bills for those on low and middle incomes.
Hiking the surcharge to 16%, they say, would deliver £24bn over four years.
A 35% surcharge, which would be the same level as the windfall tax the Conservatives imposed on energy companies, would yield £60bn over four years.
TUC general secretary Paul Nowak says:
"Some in the finance lobby have tried every which way to stop tax rises in the Budget. But these results make it blatantly obvious banks can afford to contribute more.
"It's not just HSBC. Barclays announced bumper profits last week too. And Britain's big four banks have made more than £200bn in profit over the last five years.
"With Trump and Putin's illegal wars rumbling on, working people have another cold, hard winter ahead. Households and businesses are going to need more support - and it's time for the Chancellor to make the banks pay a fairer share."
NEF: HSBC profits hard to swallow as households face rising costs
HSBC's jump in profits is prompting fresh calls for new bank taxes in the budget.
As the Guardian reported earlier this month, Healey has decided to tap the financial sector for additional revenue. One way would be to reverse cuts to the bank surcharge made by former chancellor Jeremy Hunt - which would raise upwards of £2bn a year.
Abhijay Sood, economist at the New Economics Foundation, argues that Healey should go further!
"It's not surprising that HSBC have made such significant profits in the last quarter, but it will be difficult news to swallow for households struggling with rising mortgage costs. Like other big banks, its profitability has depended on keeping interest rates on savings accounts low, and a "stealth subsidy" from taxpayers via the Bank of England due to quantitative easing.
"While the rumours of a bank tax change in the Budget are welcome, it is unlikely to go far enough. While banks are making over a billion pounds a week of profit, NEF analysis suggests that the restoration of the bank surcharge to 8% would raise just £1.8bn a year from them.The chancellor should go further by clawing back the stealth subsidy, and use this to fund inflation-busting investment and keep the essentials of life affordable for ordinary people."
On the issue of a bank tax (which John Healey could announce tomorrow), Kaur expresses support for what Andy Burnham's government are doing, saying:
"We only have 24 hours to go. We are very encouraged by the government's growth agenda, particularly how we can lean-in to support not just specific sectors like UK innovation, infrastructure. and the UK trade deals, but also in terms of how we are the connector for the UK with the rest of the world, given the success the government has had with many of these international trade deals."
But Kaur also said HSBC would have to 'respond to customer behaviour':
"Now, obviously, as our customers make their business investment decisions, we will respond accordingly, because that's what we do as banks. When the lending demand goes up, we lend more. We are ready with the right risk appetite to do so. We'll just have to see how businesses and customers react."
