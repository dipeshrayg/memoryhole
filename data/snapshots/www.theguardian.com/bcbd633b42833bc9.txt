Healthcare
The NHS experienced "serious" and "widespread" disruption across the UK on Friday as a result of the Microsoft outage, with hundreds of hospitals, GP surgeries and community health services affected, senior health sources said.
While some doctors, hospital workers and NHS night staff told the Guardian they first noticed problems accessing emails during the early hours of Friday morning, the severity of the problem became apparent to most from about 6am.
The NHS.net Connect system - which more than one million NHS staff use for emails, meetings, calendars, file access and other tasks - is powered by Microsoft. The outage meant many could not log in, perform basic duties, or send and receive patient data needed for the delivery of healthcare.
NHS sources said the outage caused disruption to a range of Microsoft 365 services relied upon by staff, including Outlook, Teams, SharePoint, OneDrive and Copilot. The Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency also said it was experiencing problems sending and receiving emails.

As my colleagues explain, UK politicians and experts have said that the Microsoft outage has laid bare the world's dangerous dependence on cloud services provided by a few massive US tech companies- Amazon, Google and Microsoft.
This reliance has been a growing source of unease through each high-profile outage. It has grown more pronounced because of the hostility of the Trump administration towards Europe, which has prompted some governments to consider alternatives to US software.
As the name Microsoft 365 implies, the world has quickly become accustomed to online services being available 24/7. Given software is no longer solely downloaded to individual PCs but is also stored on a server owned by a company, outages such as this will happen. Most of the time they are brief but sometimes they are not.
There are many different pieces of the puzzle keeping services online - but the effect of one of those services going down is much greater than it ever has been.
Mark Gregory, an associate professor at Australia's RMIT University, said it was important for organisations to thoroughly test updates before rolling them out to the live network. That was in addition to the extra security testing companies must carry out to ensure their services are protected from threat actors armed with AI tools that can find vulnerabilities quicker than ever before.
Gregory said companies that relied on services such as Microsoft 365 should have backup options such as Google's Gmail in the event of an outage. Others have suggested the free and open-source Office alternative LibreOffice.
Around the world, it has become increasingly clear that many companies - and government services - do not have good backups, with businesses resorting to faxes and phones.
The Forrester analyst Dario Maisto said the outage "represents the perfect quasi-natural experiment for stress testing an organisation's - and even an entire continent's - digital sovereignty posture", forcing governments to consider what might happen if mailing services and key tools such as Copilot were interrupted.
Millions of people were unable to access their emails, Teams chats or meetings on Thursday night and Friday after a Microsoft 365 outage brought down services across the globe.
The outage has lasted for more than 21 hours, with meaningful signs of recovery visible beginning shortly before midday GMT on Friday. It affected businesses, government agencies and even Microsoft employees all over the world. Those reporting issues have included the US House of Representatives, an Australian spy agency, airlines in India, European government agencies, and police services, ambulances and hospitals in the UK.
Microsoft is still investigating the cause of the outage, which it has said is not a cyber-attack but a configuration error. While the effects of the incident are global, they are also patchy; some users are still able to access some aspects of Microsoft's service, and service is resuming in some parts of the world faster than in others. Microsoft said it continued to see improvements, with "email queues significantly reduced and approaching normal levels", but some users may still experience issues.
What happened with Microsoft?
At about 6pm GMT on Thursday, the outage website Down Detector showed an increase in users reporting issues with Microsoft 365, the suite of cloud software that includes Teams, Word Excel, PowerPoint and Outlook.
The outage has lasted for more than 21 hours at the time of reporting, with significant recovery beginning at 11:20 GMT on Friday, according to data shared by the research firm Infoblox.
By Friday morning, Microsoft had narrowed down most of the problems to Microsoft Exchange, the email service behind Outlook. Teams meetings and chats had returned to service.
By midday UK time, Microsoft said there was "encouraging recovery progress in Europe", improvements in North America and Asia Pacific, and Japan was "stable".
What caused the Microsoft outage?
Microsoft has said that this was not the result of a cyber-attack, though suspicions were high because of a number of cyber-attacks that have hit organisations in Europe in recent weeks. So far, the problem appeared to be a configuration update that resulted in some servers becoming overloaded and taken offline, Microsoft indicated.
When the company attempted to apply a fix, servers that had come back online again became unresponsive when traffic began returning to them.
As some servers went down, traffic was funnelled on to the remaining servers, causing traffic problems for those remaining online.
To understand what was bringing down the servers, the company began limiting the traffic that could reach affected servers in specific locations, and on some servers rolled back to an earlier software configuration to see if it fixed the problem.
More details are likely to emerge over the coming weeks. "We don't know what actually went wrong, and that will take weeks to discover," said Steven Murdoch, a professor of security engineering at University College London. "The people who are working on it are too busy fixing to understand or report on the cause."
Murdoch said there was likely to be a proper postmortem on an outage of this size, but not for a few weeks.
Why has Microsoft 365 been down for so long?
An outage that runs for so long for a service as critical as Microsoft 365 is unusual. The company has not said why it has lasted this long, but a clue might be in its updates: as servers returned to being unresponsive after being fixed, engineers had to work through alternative solutions, taking longer than expected to get servers back up and running.
Murdoch said an outage of this size was usually "the result of some surprising interaction between systems. Each of the systems is normally quite well understood but interactions between them are complex and frequently the cause of large outages like this.
"It's very hard to build a complex system without there being some risk of some failure."

Small business owners and employees have been telling the Guardian how the Microsoft outage has affected them, with some services now being restored but concerns growing about the knock-on effects in the days ahead, my colleagues Nicola Slawson and Alfie Packham report.
Edward Holliday, a small business owner in West Yorkshire providing fire safety advice, says his emails started pinging again about 2.15pm. Until then, he had been unable to complete reports or respond to requests for quotes from potential clients.
"During the week I picked up a number of requests for quotes and just couldn't get back to them," he says, adding that there is a worry he'll potentially lose out on that business now.
Although he managed to get some other work done during the outage and he didn't lose any emails, he is now facing a backlog that he expects will spill into the weekend as he still needs to send out the quotes to potential clients and finish the reports he was unable to access.
"That's the beauty of working for yourself," he says.
Holliday says he is also concerned about what caused the outage, particularly given the recent spate of cyber-attacks.
"It would worry me if I thought that Microsoft were vulnerable to a cyber-attack," he says. "Obviously my whole business exists within the Microsoft environment."
Meanwhile, Sandra Wright, who works for a family-run cleaning supplies business in Kent, says the disruption could have consequences for supply chains, with the company unable to place orders.
The company has been unable to place orders with suppliers, potentially disrupting its usual delivery schedules.
"Although it is merely a day, a lot of companies, especially ones who operate via outsourced warehousing, have certain order days, be it for their weekly, fortnightly or monthly slots," she says.
"Today alone would have had a ripple effect on these order dates."
The outage has also left colleagues anxious about what might happen next, with some fearing further disruption despite Microsoft saying the problems were not caused by a cyber-attack.
"Today's occurrence with Microsoft shows that nothing is untouchable," Wright says, adding that staff are concerned about the wider repercussions for businesses that rely heavily on email.
