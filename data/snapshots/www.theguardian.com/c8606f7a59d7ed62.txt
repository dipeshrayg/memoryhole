Young people across Europe stage coordinated 'fight for our future' protests
Jon Henley
Students and young workers have taken to the streets in more than 20 European countries in coordinated protests over the surging cost of living and housing, deteriorating learning conditions and rising military spending.
Activists from more than 50 groups in countries including Belgium, France, Germany, Greece, Italy, Portugal, Spain and the UK held rallies on Friday, lighting flares, burning cardboard models of tanks and waving placards, many reading "fight for our future".
Calling it Europe's biggest coordinated youth protest of recent years, the Youth Strike coalition said demonstrations were planned in about 400 cities under the slogan "fight for our future, not their wars".
It made five broad demands, including an end to militarisation and conscription; structural investment in education and welfare; solidarity with Palestine; climate and social justice, and a "radical transformation of the system we live in".
An Italian organiser, Alessandro di Miceli, said the Europe-wide protests were "to claim our right to a better and dignified future". Specific demands differed from country to country, he said, but were all "deeply connected between each other".
A Portuguese organiser, Leonor Chicó, said: "As young people, we know that we must organise and fight for our future, because no one else will do it for us." Governments "show us every day that the needs of our generation are not their priority", she said.
Shabbir Lakha from the UK-based Stop The War coalition said the mobilisation was "a crucial step towards building a lasting international movement capable of bringing together young people, students and workers around common demands".

"Why would anybody turn their back on that?" he said. "I think it's for them to justify now [whether] they won't support the bill that will be presented to parliament next week."

Retailers, manufacturers welcome new deal as 'big win,' but call for 'clear and early' guidance on implementation
Retailers and manufacturers have welcomed Andy Burnham's new trade deal with the European Union as a "big win" but warned they need "clear and early" guidance from government to aid the transition, Press Association reported.
The chief executive of the British Retail Consortium (BRC) called the SPS deal a "big win" for UK retailers but warned that businesses need "at least 18 months" to transition to the new arrangements.
Helen Dickinson said:
"Reducing border friction and simplifying trade will cut costs and support smoother trade in food, drink and plants between the UK and EU, including moving goods to Northern Ireland.
The benefits will be felt across the entire supply chain, from producers and suppliers through to retailers and the millions of consumers they serve."
The priority now must be to get the detail right and give businesses at least 18 months, alongside clear and early guidance, to transition to the new arrangements without unnecessary cost or disruption."
But the BRC also told the Press Association that it does not expect to see prices on supermarket shelves fall in response to this deal, even though this is a welcome intervention.
Upward pressures remain heavy and will not be alleviated by the agreement, it said.
The National Farmers Union said they had worked with the Government to secure the deal which "should help" the industry, but warned that it was "only the beginning".
President of the NFU Tom Bradshaw said that while reducing border friction is welcome, "rebuilding lost exports will take time" as in some cases British products have been replaced by goods from elsewhere.
The managing director of Marks + Spencer Food, Alex Freudmann, said in a blog on Friday that the deal will "remove bureaucracy" from the current regulations.
He wrote:
"How hard can it be to get a spaghetti bolognese from a factory in Lincolnshire onto a shelf in Dublin?
Today, it takes hundreds of extra miles, mountains of paperwork, a vet's signature and the Latin name of a cow (Bos taurus, if you're wondering).
This is what food trade looks like after Brexit. And it's why we need the UK-EU food trade deal announced today."
How to read EU-UK reset declaration on three sectoral deals, youth mobility, and others - analysis
Jakub Krupa
The main document from today's summit is this one: the joint declaration signed by the two sides.
Fancy reading it with me? Let's go.
I won't read it chronologically, but jump a bit, reflecting what I think you are looking for, and not how the document is written.
On "people to people" relations (pages 7-8), the document confirms the Youth Experience Scheme (YES) that will allow "100,000 people from the EU and the UK respectively to reside for up to three years to engage in activities such as work, studies, au-pairing, volunteering or simply travelling."
On timing, it says the sides "aim to apply this agreement no later than 2028," and want it to kick in "no later than January 2030." It will apply to those up to the age of 31, so effectively will help those born in 1999 and later.
The document also confirms the UK's return to the Erasmus+ programme in 2027, which we already knew about.
On the thorny issue of tuition fees, it says the parties will "work towards a supplementing agreement" to be agreed within six months. It's very much unfinished business on that point.
In a win for artists and performers, the document also includes a somewhat vague, but promising, commitment to "explore further mutually beneficial solutions" to help touring artists with ridiculous red tape currently in place.
The statement also covers some of the key sectoral deals we mentioned earlier (pages 9-10), on emissions trading systems, sanitary and phytosanitary measures (SPS), and yes - of course - on fish (noting the decision on full reciprocal access to waters to fish until 30 June 2038).
In similarly practical decisions, there is also a note on "the significant progress achieved in discussions on the mutual recognition of professional qualifications for architects" which are set to continue.
The statement also covers important issues on judicial and security matters, noting plans to enhance cooperation between law enforcement agencies and, particularly, on the removal of foreign national offenders and migrant returns (pages 11-12).
The new language on economic resilience and cooperation is on pages 13-14, including commitment to "counter technology leakage in critical sectors, mitigate risks to critical infrastructure, and [to build] resilient supply chains" and so on.
The document, finally, also covers foreign and defence policy, in generally vague language, but across several pages (1-7).
It includes a long list of things the EU and the UK agree on, including on their support for Ukraine, foreign and defence policy priorities related to Africa, Arctic, Gaza, Iran, Lebanon, Nato, Moldova, and the Western Balkans.
It also includes a line on commitment to "effective multilateralism" through the UN, the international criminal court, which continues to attract attacks from the US, and the global rules-based trade system.
And then we are back to questions on today's deal and the tuition fees disagreement - and whether the two EU leaders agree with Burnham that today's deal marks the high point of what can be done without a broader vision for the EU-UK relationship.
Costa smiles and says that the focus needs to be on building "the closest relationship possible" between the EU and the UK, but how close it can get needs to be defined by the British.
"In the meantime, we need to work very hard to try to solve problems to our companies, to our citizens, to improve the competitiveness of our economies," he says.
But, somewhat echoing Burnham's language earlier (14:37), he focuses on younger generations and says "these young people, a large part of them, was not here ten years ago, but they are here today."
"And this is our duty to open to them new opportunities," he says.
He says today's deal on youth mobility for 100,000 people "is the best way to give future to our shared values, our mutual interest, our historic bond and cultural values that we share together."
Von der Leyen says "we can always work on an incremental improvement to our relationship," but she agrees that there is a much broader question on "the wider picture, the bigger picture" of how Europe should respond to "dramatic and big changes globally."
Burnham, EU leaders condemn 'utterly unacceptable' Russian incursion into Polish airspace
The next question is on something very different - on continuing hybrid attacks about different European countries, asked in the context of airspace violations in Poland.
Two Russian cruise missiles and a jet-powered drone are reported to have crashed in Poland during Russian air attacks on Ukraine that took place amid major western military exercises by allied countries.
All three speakers condemn the incident.
EU's Costa says "it shows that, unfortunately, Russia instead of showing willingness to engage in peace talks continue to spread the threats."
"This is very dangerous and shows Russia is going in the wrong direction," he says.
Von der Leyen strikes a similar line.
Burnham says the incursion into Polish airspace is "utterly unacceptable," as he says he wants to send "a strong message of solidarity to prime minister Tusk and the Polish people who we will support, always, in any way that we can."
He makes a broader point on the back of that, saying "Europe is becoming more coordinated in terms of defence and security," and today's agreement helps with that.
He pivots to Ukraine more broadly, saying "if European security is to come into that question, first we must have a ceasefire, and we must have the basis of a peace deal between Russia and Ukraine."
"We will have to think this through carefully, together, to ensure that we are stronger in the next decade, than we were in the last.
I think some of what we've seen in Europe came at us without us being fully prepared. Not just the aggression, but also the disruption within our democracies and other factors that have sought to weaken Europe.
We will come back stronger in this decade, and we collaborate more deeply on security and defence."
Asked about British people making up their minds, von der Leyen smiles that "I have many British friends and they are very good at making up their minds."
"I know that by experience."
On today's deals, she backs Burnham's line that today's deal matters.
"We have three major agreements where many people thought we would never be able, to come to the finish line."
