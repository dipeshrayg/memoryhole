Is any act ever really too niche for stardom? What could be more leftfield, after all, than Bill Bailey's animated pop-musicology, with demonstrations on the Swedish nyckelharpa and the one-stringed Indian lute? But tonight he has yet another arena crowd hanging on his every strum, drumbeat and twang. Nothing need be niche, perhaps, if communicated with the open-hearted curiosity and passion Bailey brings to his esoteric interests. Add a flair for improbable juxtaposition, and a sense of humour that dusts curmudgeonliness with surrealism, and you've got a concoction that thousands-strong audiences find difficult to resist.
The 61-year-old's current show is called Vaudevillean, and gestures are made towards justifying that title, with reference to the French poet Olivier Basselin and the 1453 battle of Castillon. (You don't get this with Michael McIntyre.) Like vaudeville, the show certainly has variety to recommend it. But really, this offering differs little from many a previous Bailey outing, and finds our host again following his nose from one unlikely musical experiment to another, via nuggets of topical standup and anecdotes from his storied life.
The quality is high this time: Bailey is on fine gadfly form here, hop-skipping from jokes about "the cheesecake Ceaușescu" in the White House to tales of speed awareness courses at the Ramada Inn, Watford. There's musing on the impact of Shakespeare's "To be or not to be" when translated into different languages, and visual gags on an upstage screen about what all those Georgian women in Gainsborough paintings may be concealing beneath their skirts.
There's more: a song with shadow-puppet animation about a big-headed woman and her ferret; all seven deadly sins committed in a kebab shop in 15 seconds flat. In another comic's hands, this might seem frivolous or unfocused - and occasionally here (his bit about animating Chekhov plays with his belly button fluff, say) the dottiness can feel attenuated. But usually Bailey's contagious delight in his off-beam interests, and the comic and musical virtuosity applied to them, keep proceedings buoyant. There's a distinctive pleasure, finally, in enjoying - far from the nearest niche - the popular success of an act that strikes zero compromise with orthodoxy.
