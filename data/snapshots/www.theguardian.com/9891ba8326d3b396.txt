When Jennifer Reid was working in Home Bargains, she used till receipts to write out the folk ballads she was learning by heart, and then tucked them into her waistband, because staff uniforms didn't have pockets. "Or if it was too sweaty in summer, I'd hide them behind the shampoo bottles and then go back for them," she recalls.
Growing up in Middleton, Greater Manchester, Reid discovered local broadside ballads - single-sheet songs sold on the street depicting life and events, which boomed during the Industrial Revolution - when volunteering at Chetham's Library in her late teens. Reid describes her years prior to this as being "a cantankerous young 'un with all this energy that was sometimes misdirected into antisocial stuff" but this new direction was mind-expanding. "I'd never thought about Oldham or Rochdale in a historical cultural context before," she says. "Very soon I realised: I want to make sure this history doesn't die."
When Jeremy Deller swung by the library while preparing an exhibition for the 2013 Venice Biennale, Reid persuaded him to let her deliver a talk and then to perform a ballad at the festival despite having never sung before. "So my first ever gig was at the Venice Biennale," Reid recalls, as we sit in a Manchester pub. "It was one of those opportunities where I thought, if I don't take it, it's not going to happen, and it's back to Home Bargains."
It turns out Reid had a wonderful voice, one with power and range, and a unique career followed. Now 35, she tours the world singing Industrial Revolution workers' songs in the Lancashire dialect; she now has a repertoire of about 300 - covering factory life, mining, strikes, Chartism and child labour - and she knows around 60-70 by heart. She teaches kids from a variety of backgrounds about the history, folklore and oral traditions surrounding them, and is as likely to get booked at a family-friendly harvest festival as she is at an experimental music event collaborating with noiseniks like Gnod. She's been a support act for Pulp and John Cooper Clarke, released an album of traditional songs and original material (2025's The Ballad of the Gatekeeper) and even landed a role in Shane Meadows' TV series The Gallows Pole, about a group of coin forgers in 18th-century Yorkshire.
She's one of the headliners for the next month's New Folk festival in Slaithwaite, near Huddersfield, but folk music is somewhere that Reid has ended up via more of a DIY punk mentality and aesthetic. Her social media handle proudly proclaims: "Disrupting the folk scene since 2015."
"I've entered it from the sides - kicking and screaming," she says. There have been similarly disruptive entrants in recent years, such as Shovel Dance Collective and Broadside Hacks, presenting and sometimes twisting folk traditions. Is Reid doing something similar? "All I seek to do is uphold and preserve the material," she says. "With all the new stuff that's happening, it's great to push boundaries and make different things out of old shapes. But there needs to be a commitment to, or at least an understanding of, what the old shape was before you go changing it."
At home she listens to archival audio recordings of books being read out about towel mills, in order to study the nuances of language and dialect. Since cotton mill workers wore clogs and danced to the rhythm of weaving looms, she practised clog dancing every day for a year so she can incorporate it into her live performance. "It's all-consuming," she says. "But it needs to be done because there's so much material and this thing is bigger than you are - you're just a tiny ant with your little pickaxe, having a go at this huge wall."
Reid is bursting with passion, sometimes breaking into song mid-conversation; occasionally, she's so blown away by what she's speaking about that she's at a loss for words, contorting her face to express silent astonishment, as when she speaks of a trip to Bangladesh, working with weavers. When the lyrics for their songs were translated, they were "almost word-for-word the same as the songs from Lancashire", she says, eyes bulging. "Oh my God, that was insane to me." Those songs also highlighted the ongoing brutalities of the garment trade, with workers in Bangladesh comparing current conditions to a prison, and experiencing dilapidated factories collapsing just as they did in 19th-century Oldham.
Reid is keen not to romanticise or smooth over rough edges. "You have to realise this was a period of tumult and suffering," she says of the Industrial Revolution. "Not only people in England but also abroad via colonialism. People get rosy-eyed about it, especially engineers, but you have to understand the global context. So I always go to the horse's mouth - all the songs I sing are by autodidactic working men and women, like weavers and miners. Those voices are key to understanding history."
Class is crucial to Reid's work. "It's not about being anti-middle class but pro-working class," she says. "It's important to give credence to people that worked all day, these long 15-16 hour shifts, and then came home to write poems and songs at night." She is very much on a mission to spread the word as far as possible. "My plan is to get everywhere, like water," she enthuses. "Singing is a great way to engage audiences but I want to educate. So that people know where to come should they need to know about the Lancashire dialect. It's like being a lamp-post. It's not very glam, but it's vital infrastructure."
Reid even recalls a panic attack she had at the airport when flying to New Zealand to tour. With her brain gripped by fear, as a surge of negative thoughts rushed through her head, worrying about passports, visas and the whole thing collapsing, she had a stark realisation of how much it all means. "At that moment I decided that if they made singing ballads illegal, I would die for it," she says. "I would go down swinging. It's that important to me."
