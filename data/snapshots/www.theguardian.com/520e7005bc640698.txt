The publisher HarperCollins used a leading legal firm and a non-disclosure agreement to "cover up" David Walliams' inappropriate behaviour towards women two years before the children's author was unexpectedly dropped, according to former staff.
Women had complained to the company's management for years about the author's alleged misconduct and harassment. The concerns raised included that Walliams had an "inappropriate" sexual relationship with a woman who was an intern when he first met her, had inappropriate interactions with at least three other women connected to the company including asking two for dates, and approached a girl who appeared to be in her teens at a book signing event.
The concerns were ignored for nearly a decade, according to multiple sources, who said employees would instead warn each other to avoid him. It was not until 2023 when managers took action and brought in the City law firm Allen & Overy to investigate the allegations.
At least five women spoke to the inquiry, some with first-hand experience of the alleged misconduct. Walliams was never interviewed. His lawyers subsequently said he denied all allegations of harassment or inappropriate behaviour in the workplace.
The inquiry quietly wrapped up in early 2024 with no consequences at the time for the author. Walliams, whose bestselling children's books have been compared to those of Roald Dahl, went on to publish a further five books before HarperCollins severed ties with him in December 2025.
Several former employees said they believed the inquiry was a means to shut down complaints. One, who would only speak anonymously, said: "It was not an inquiry, it was a cover-up."
For one woman who spoke to the inquiry, it led to the end of her career at the publishing company. She was reportedly given a five-figure payout.
She told the Guardian and ITV News she wanted to speak out but could not. It is understood she is prevented from doing so because she was gagged by a strict non-disclosure agreement (NDA) as part of her settlement.
The women questioned were told they could not discuss anything related to the inquiry or their concerns about Walliams, according to multiple sources with knowledge of the process, and they still feel unable to speak openly.
The development comes after a joint investigation by the Guardian and ITV News revealed accusations that Walliams used his celebrity status to prey on young women and teenagers for sex. Two were aged 15 when he first contacted them. Walliams' lawyers said he denied being a sexual predator or abusing his power.
Walliams is now the focus of an investigation by the Metropolitan police into separate "serious sexual misconduct" allegations. Walliams earlier told the Guardian and ITV News he had not broken the law.
The new claims about HarperCollins' handling of complaints raise significant questions for the Rupert Murdoch-owned company, one of the biggest publishers in the world.
Leading figures said the law should not be used to silence concerns about inappropriate behaviour in the workplace. Zelda Perkins, who broke an NDA to reveal the sexual misconduct of the Hollywood producer Harvey Weinstein and is currently speaking to the UK government about further changing the law to prevent their misuse, said: "Nobody can ever understand what having your voice taken away means."
Victoria Barnsley, who was the chief executive of HarperCollins in the UK until 2013, said it was "absolutely outrageous" how the company allegedly dealt with its bestselling author. She said it was not right that the women "have to move to other companies or be forced out or paid off. That really shouldn't be happening."
A spokesperson for the publisher said: "We are shocked by the allegations contained in the ITV and Guardian reporting relating to teenage girls.
"HarperCollins UK previously terminated its publishing relationship with David Walliams. We take the safety of our people incredibly seriously."
Walliams' commercial success
When the Guardian and ITV News published the allegations against Walliams, Kate Elton, who took over as CEO of HarperCollins UK in October last year, made a short online address to employees. Apparently reading from a written statement, she explained that she decided to stop publishing Walliams shortly after taking over as chief executive, and that "it was a decision I felt had to be taken".
Elton, who has worked at HarperCollins since 2011, spoke about the "deeply upsetting accounts" in the news reports and said she wished she did not have to speak to staff about the situation. She went on to reassure people that their wellbeing was important to the company: "If you ever have any concerns or issues, you can speak up in confidence and you will be heard."
Elton's address to staff contrasts with accounts from multiple sources who said women tried to raise concerns or blow the whistle on the star author, only to be ignored by managers or shut down by the lawyer-run inquiry.
Walliams was signed up to HarperCollins' children's division and in 2008 published his first book, The Boy in the Dress, which proved an immediate hit. He went on to become the UK's fourth-bestselling author since accurate records began, publishing more than 40 children's titles and generating around £156m of sales in the UK alone.
It was this commercial success that several former employees said they believed protected the author.
"Because he was responsible for HarperCollins hitting [its] targets every year, nothing ever happened," said one woman who shared her misgivings about Walliams' behaviour with managers in 2015.
Another woman, who worked in the children's division in 2017, said that in her opinion: "If he hadn't been making them money, if he hadn't been this big TV guy ... they would have got rid of him."
Several sources said they believed the reluctance to tackle concerns about Walliams came from the top, linking it to Charlie Redmayne, who succeeded Barnsley in 2013 and left suddenly in October 2025, two months before Walliams was dropped. Redmayne did not respond to requests for comment.
Inappropriate encounters, complaints and warnings
In spring 2015, Walliams separated from his wife, Lara Stone. A few months later, a junior member of staff showed a manager emails she had received from him. He had met the young woman at a book promotion event in a primary school, then asked her out over email, and tried again a few months later, according to the manager.
Lawyers for Walliams said he had messaged the woman when he was "heartbroken and lonely after his divorce and was hoping to meet someone to spend time with". It is understood the woman ignored his emails.
"She was just so young," said the employee who saw the emails. "It's just not appropriate for an author to be messaging a member of staff like that."
She said she brought the incident to the attention of the human resources (HR) department, but that she felt she was ignored. It was one of at least six occasions, the Guardian and ITV News were told, when women alerted management to their concerns about what they felt was Walliams' inappropriate conduct.
In 2017, Walliams started a sexual relationship with a junior employee, according to multiple sources. He met the young woman, who was an intern for the company, at a promotional book event at a school. She was later sent to his house alone to deliver paperwork. She was in her mid-20s and Walliams was 45. The woman told colleagues that while it was consensual looking back she considered the relationship to be the result of "an abuse of power".
News that Walliams was having a relationship with a junior employee spread around the company. One former manager said he felt uncomfortable to learn that the young woman had been sent to Walliams' house. A second source said Walliams sent a large bunch of flowers to the woman's desk, and another colleague saw the woman crying about Walliams in the office bathrooms.
Senior management, including Redmayne, were aware of the relationship, according to former employees.
Lawyers for Walliams said it was a "consensual sexual" relationship.
At a book signing event a year later, Walliams was seen approaching a girl who appeared to be in her mid-teens. A former HarperCollins employee who was helping to arrange the signing witnessed the interaction. She said it seemed as if he was asking for her Instagram handle. "I just was like, no, no, no, no, no, no. This is a fucking children's bookstore. Are you out of your mind?" She said she and another colleague reported what they had seen to a manager.
Walliams' lawyers said he did not recall the interaction. They said if he had been asking for Instagram details it would have been to tag the bookshop in his own posts. Walliams did post two photos from the event, but he did not tag the shop.
In the face of apparent inaction from bosses, women took it upon themselves to warn each other about Walliams' behaviour. Two women said colleagues had advised them to take care when they had been asked to deliver paperwork to his house.
A young woman who was in her early 20s when she worked at HarperCollins said she was told to be careful as she was blonde and considered by colleagues to be "his type". She said there was an "unwritten rule" that young women should not be alone around him.
Worried, she said she raised concerns with senior managers and HR at least twice. "I was reassured by them that there was no proof and we would never be put in bad situations," she said. "Now I think maybe we weren't necessarily safe."
Not all former employees spoke negatively about Walliams. Three former staff members said they had not witnessed any inappropriate behaviour themselves, with one saying they found him to be professional and quite introverted.
Beyond the office, Walliams' reputation caused consternation for the publisher. In 2020, he faced criticism online about the content of his books after the anti-poverty campaigner Jack Monroe posted on Twitter (now X) that they were "sneering classist fatshaming grim nonsense". Soon afterwards a young woman started posting about her interactions with him in his role as a judge on the TV show Britain's Got Talent.
A group of authors published by HarperCollins UK sent an email to the company to express their concern about the tweets.
Then, in November 2022 Walliams was dropped by BGT after a hot mic incident, revealed by the Guardian, caught him making derogatory comments about contestants.
HarperCollins continued to publish his books.
Internal inquiry and 'isolation'
In October 2023, Walliams was the guest of honour at a tea party at the HarperCollins office to mark the launch of his latest title, The Blunders.
Days earlier, managers had launched the internal inquiry into his conduct. A new head of the children's department, Cally Poplak, was told of the concerns about Walliams' behaviour. She told one colleague she felt there was a culture within the company of managers looking the other way.
Oversight of the internal investigation was taken up by the publisher's head of global compliance, Isha Mehmood, based in New York.
The inquiry was then passed to Allen & Overy (now known as A&O Shearman) and run by Sarah Henchoz, a lawyer described on the firm's website as having a particular specialism in "business protection and workplace conduct issues".
At least five women were interviewed as part of the process, according to sources. It is understood the women were warned not to discuss the inquiry with colleagues, and that if they did it would be a disciplinary matter.
In early 2024, the law firm handed over the report on its findings. A&O Shearman did not respond to a request for comment.
Several sources told the Guardian they believed the inquiry was just a way to scope the company's legal liability and shut down complaints.
A source with knowledge of the process said the women were never told the outcome of the inquiry but were "left in the dark" and still fear speaking out.
One woman left the company after agreeing to a settlement, which is understood to have included a strict NDA. Some of the other women, according to multiple sources, said they felt "isolated", "overwhelmed" and "shut down". Some were told they could avoid going to the office when Walliams was present.
A spokesperson for HarperCollins said: "To respect the privacy of staff we cannot comment on individual employee matters but can confirm that in 2024 an investigation was concluded by an external law firm and recommendations were implemented."
For nearly two years HarperCollins continued to publish Walliams' books.
Unanswered questions
Walliams' departure from the publisher in December 2025 came just two months after another seismic event. After more than 10 years with the company, Redmayne had left suddenly that October. There was no internal announcement and no big leaving event. Redmayne did not respond to requests for comment.
He was replaced by Elton, who was promoted internally. She had been in post for two months when the company was approached by the Telegraph with questions about the internal inquiry. Days after receiving those questions, Walliams was dropped.
In her address to staff, Elton said she was positive about the company's future.
Industry insiders have said there is much work to be done. Philip Jones, the editor of the Bookseller magazine, called the revelations about HarperCollins the biggest scandal in publishing in his 20 years in the sector. "This is a #MeToo movement moment for the industry," he said. "HarperCollins now need to step up, come clean and don't let it happen again."
He said company executives needed to investigate "what they knew, what they condoned, what they thought was permissible and what they thought they could manage".
"This is a moment where the entire sector needs to stop burying their heads in the sand and do something."
Barnsley, the former CEO of HarperCollins, said: "What's staggering is that everyone is relating it to 90s and 00s culture, but it's not, it's still happening. That's what people have to understand. They have to stop trying to comfort themselves by saying it happened a long time ago."
