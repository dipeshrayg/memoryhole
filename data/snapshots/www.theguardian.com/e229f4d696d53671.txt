Premieres are all well and good, but what about second performances, and third and fourth? We puzzle over the problem of new music, of how to build a living classical repertoire, while too often giving new works just one outing to make their case, audiences just one chance to hear them. How brilliant, then, to see American composer Luke Mombrea's Black Gold - winner of a 2025 Ivor Novello award - at the centre of the Manchester Collective's new tour: another chance to get our ears around this widescreen soundscape with its slippery electro-acoustic vistas.
The fire that raged through the Santa Fe oil fields in 1928 burned for two months, swallowing up the surrounding desert in smoke and ash. Steel plate photographs show derricks dwarfed by pillars of flame and black billowing clouds. They're the starting point for Mombrea's chamber work, whose three movements for flute, cello, double bass and percussion were conceived in tandem with Nate Mohler's monochrome, photo-manipulating video projections.
We open in a prelapsarian landscape, bowed vibraphone and electronics stretching the sonic horizon, while Alex Jakeman's flute, Delia Stevens' hammered dulcimer and a cello's feathery harmonics (Peggy Nolan) paint a nostalgic desert-pastoral of birds and folksongs. Man arrives with thrumming, mechanistic rhythms (ground out by bassist Marianne Schofield) and tight little motifs, before the fire unites both worlds: are those birds or sirens? Is it the raging, destructive energy of flame or nature itself screaming in protest? Mombrea treats his instruments like a Bill Morrison film - acoustic sounds distorted and denatured, decaying in real time. It's utterly compelling, with little need of Mohler's visual anchor.
A first half performed as a continuous sequence saw James Tenney's 1982 trio Glissades and Hildur Guðnadottir's choose-your-own-adventure graphic score From the Other Place interleaved with new arrangements of chorale preludes from Bach's Orgelbüchlein by the Collective's co-artistic director Jasmin Kent Rodgman, climaxing in Sasha Scott's newly commissioned Into the Glare. The effect was of the familiar made strange: Bach's intricate horizontal lines (stripped clean of almost all vibrato by violist Ruth Gibson) suddenly flipped vertical by a vibraphone's percussive impact; sliding string portamenti turned into an endless, impossible Escher staircase by Tenney's acoustic games; grungy city streets blurred and whited-out, now intimately lyrical now hard and urban in Scott's sonic collage. The disorientation was heady, intriguing. Luckily the Collective's ongoing tour gives us all three more chances to get lost in it.
