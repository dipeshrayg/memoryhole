For three decades, film-maker Joe Berlinger has gripped true-crime buffs with his deep dives into small-town murders and infamous serial killers. His Netflix explorations of John Wayne Gacy and Jeffrey Dahmer helped flip documentary storytelling from viewer homework to a welcome distraction.
After dedicating so much time to the life of Jeffrey Epstein, the kidnapping of JonBenét Ramsey and other unsolved mysteries, Berlinger thought Primetime Preacher$, his new Netflix documentary on America's televangelist boom, would offer a break. But it turns out that's a pretty juicy crime story too, about the religious right's bank job on American politics. "The deeper resonance for me came when I realized that the global televangelism rise actually mirrors the rise of the religious right," he says. "Religion and politics, from Reagan on, has really changed politics for the worse in my opinion. This was the birth of that."
In the early 1970s, roughly a quarter of US adults identified as evangelical Christians. As televangelism expanded, so too did the appeal of the prosperity gospel - this notion that personal prosperity was an indicator of divine favor. It encouraged the faithful not only to tithe generously to God's work, but also to the ministers ostensibly chosen to carry out His will.
Primetime Preacher$ focuses on three: Jerry Falwell, Jimmy Swaggart and Jim Bakker - pillars of the movement who paved the way for Joel Osteen, TD Jakes and other modern contemporaries while establishing the comedic archetype of the greedy pulpit huckster, played by everyone from Steve Martin to Druski. Americans who lived through this period will remember these pioneering holy men well - Falwell, the befrocked Lee Iacocca clone who steered the "moral majority" and Liberty University; Swaggart, the fire-and-brimstone Pentecostal who rivaled cousin Jerry Lee Lewis for theatrics; and Bakker, the Mr Rogers type who famously lost his TV megachurch after an extramarital affair and a federal fraud scandal brought down PTL ministry (short for Praise the Lord, obviously).
What my young mind couldn't appreciate at the time from scanning grocery aisle tabloid covers, eavesdropping on the TV news and observing the disgraced PTL first lady on the RuPaul Show was how tightly the three men were intertwined. "It's a fun little Shakespearean tale of ego, greed and ambition," Berlinger says. "A clash of these three men and this bizarre era in history."
Bakker anchors the 114-minute film. Through archival video and interviews with close associates and the Pulitzer prize-winning Charlotte Observer team that uncovered the scandal, Berlinger shows how the genial former puppeteer won followers with folksy charm and Tonight Show-style productions that sold a more compassionate version of Christianity to a satellite audience of more than 13 million people. Behind the scenes, Jim and Tammy Faye carved out a life of luxury on the church's dime, with homes (including an air-conditioned doghouse), a private jet and a pair of facelifts for Jim.
Swaggart took deep offense at Bakker's PTL, however. He delivered over-the-top sermons to steer his flock away from football games and movies, while disparaging Bakker as a false prophet who was leading a double life. On CNN, he called Bakker "a cancer on the body of Christ".
It all came to a head in the late 1980s, when Jessica Hahn, a former church secretary, publicly accused Bakker of rape and it emerged that she had received $265,000 in exchange for her silence. (Hahn, who endured a level of public scorn that seems unthinkable in the current post-#MeToo era, declined Berlinger's sit-down invitation.) IRS auditors later concluded that $1.3m in ministry funds had been spent for the Bakkers' personal benefit.
Bakker resigned from PTL and brought in Falwell to keep donations flowing and manage the crisis, thinking his friend would hold down the fort long enough for him to make a triumphant comeback. "I think he was genuinely interested in not having a massive embarrassment for Christianity," Berlinger says. "There was a lot of good that PTL was doing that he thought was worth preserving. But did he think he could also expand his reach? Yes, but I don't think he wanted to expand his reach to personally enrich himself."
Falwell, the canniest operator, wound up burying his biggest competition. He went public with details of more of PTL's financial improprieties, the Bakkers' handwritten severance wishlist ("$300,000 for life to Jim ... a maid and a secretary for one year") and rumors that Bakker may have engaged in homosexual relationships. Ultimately, Falwell walked away from a business that was more than $65m in the hole. Swaggart, though, was in no position to gloat. After fiercely proclaiming that he had never had sex outside of marriage, much less disrobed for another woman, Swaggart was snapped during a tryst with a sex worker in New Orleans.
The film takes obvious satisfaction in the hypocrisy on display, not least Swaggart's insistence on cutting a hole in his pants to maintain the, um, spirit of the truth. "Swaggart was telling people how to live," Berlinger says. Swaggart's melodramatic apology - "I have sinned against you, my Lord!" - became fodder for Jim Carrey and others keen to send up similar figures. And the laughing only got louder when Swaggart was caught soliciting again three years later.
Primetime Preacher$ shows how trouble beyond the pulpit drove a broader political conversion. When Bakker, who served almost five years in prison on federal fraud and conspiracy charges, ran into questions from Jimmy Carter's Federal Communications Commission over PTL's fundraising practices, he lent his considerable platform to Ronald Reagan, midwifing the California governor's makeover from divorced Hollywood B-lister into the cowboy conservative who spoke of a "city upon a hill" that "hungers for a spiritual revival".
In the nearly half century since, a movement that once warned against worldly entanglements has become a cornerstone of the GOP coalition, helping elect every Republican president since Reagan. That includes Donald Trump. His standing with evangelicals was shaky, to say the least, until a timely blessing from Jerry Falwell Jr - who extends the movement's legacy of scandal (grist for its own documentary as well). Falwell Jr also appears in the film, explicitly invoking his father - who, before his death in 2007, declared the scandals of those bygone decades had "strengthened broadcast evangelism and made Christianity stronger, more mature and more committed". That the Christian right remains a powerful political force, despite the recent blue wave, strengthens his claim.
Only Tammy Faye Bakker emerges from this story as something close to a redeemable character. She at least appeared genuinely committed to meeting faith-seekers where they were. While Jim sought to grow PTL into the Disney of Christianity, Tammy Faye aspired to be more of a Dolly Parton-style entertainer who led with humor - and suffered endless jokes about her caked-on makeup - while giving a platform to gay faith leaders whom other Christians had pushed aside and condemned to hell.
After divorcing Jim, Tammy Faye had a glorious second act as a kitschy B-lister and unabashed LGBTQ+ ally before dying from cancer in 2007. Jay Bakker, who followed his parents into ministry, does his mother proud by wrapping himself in a progressive theology that embraces LGBTQ+ rights - a position that opened a rift between him and his father that did not close with Jim's death last month. "I found him to be extremely sympathetic as a person and as a character," Berlinger says. "It also speaks so loudly to Jim Bakker's character that he rejected Jay Bakker when he became LGBTQ-affirming."
And yet: the biggest irony in Primetime Preacher$ isn't Jim Bakker pivoting from come-to-Jesus telethons to hawking doomsday prepper kits. It's him essentially showing the televangelists who followed him how not to get caught. Today's TV megapastors don't cloak their avarice in the faith and good works of the church. They just come right out, ask for the jet and claim it's God's will. "It's amazing people give under those circumstances," Berlinger says. "But people want to believe, you know?"
