Anthropic's shares slumped at the start of trading on Friday, as its record-breaking IPO failed to stem a sell-off in AI-linked companies.
Shares in the frontier AI company, which floated on Thursday, fell by over 9% at the start of trading on Friday. They dropped to $88.17, towards the $83 level at which the company sold shares in its IPO this week.
Anthropic's first two days as a listed company have been volatile. Its shares jumped by 17% on Thursday, after briefly surging by almost 40% when trading in the frontier AI firm began.
The UK and EU have agreed a reset deal, Downing Street has said, although the level of tuition fees EU students will pay to study at UK universities remains unresolved.
"The deal is done - negotiations have concluded," a No 10 statement said, less than an hour after the prime minister, AndyBurnham, arrived at the summit venue in Brussels, where he was greeted by Ursulavon der Leyen, the EuropeanCommission president, and AntónioCosta, the EuropeanCouncil president.
The reset means there is agreement on three deals - food and drink, emissions trading, and a youth mobility scheme - but EU sources say these deals will not enter into force unless the UK reaches a settlement with the bloc over tuition fee rates for European students.
The statement came shortly after headlines on Friday suggested that the tuition fee issue could cause other parts of the process to collapse.
However, after the deal was announced, UK officials pushed back strongly against the idea that the three plans could be jeopardised by a failure to agree on tuition fees, saying the issues were completely separate. Earlier, EU officials had indicated that progress on the main deals would be contingent on tuition fees.
The UK government borrowed less than expected in October despite a rise in debt interest costs, in a boost for chancellor John Healey amid the pressure on Britain's economy from the Middle East war.
The Office for National Statistics (ONS) said public sector net borrowing - the difference between government spending and income - was £17bn in October, about £800m higher than the same month a year ago. City economists had forecast borrowing would increase by more, to £18bn.
In the financial year so far, public sector net borrowing was £111.3bn, the ONS said - £7.3bn less than in the same period last year, helped by stronger tax receipts.

The price of gold is also continuing to fall today, heading for its biggest weekly decline since March.
The yellow metal is down 1.1% to $3,578.48 per ounce, touching its lowest level since September 4, 2025 at $3,513.54 earlier.
Much of the pressure on gold prices has come from strong economic data and high inflation in the US, which has fed expectations of interest rate hikes. Gold typically becomes less appealing to investors when rates are high as the metal does not pay them a yield.
Here's the view from DominicSchnider, head global FX & commodity, and GiovanniStaunovo of UBS Global Wealth Management:
The sharp price decline warrants revisiting the drivers of last year's gold rally into 1Q26. Rising expectations of Fed rate cuts, tariff concerns, questions about Fed independence, and perceptions that the US administration favoured a structurally weaker currency created a supportive backdrop.
Higher inflation has since shifted rate expectations toward hikes. Concerns about Fed independence have eased as the Fed has raised rates without material intervention from the US administration. Meanwhile, an AI-driven investment boom has supported US economic activity.
Taken together, these developments could be interpreted as driving a full retracement of gold's rally toward USD 3,500/oz. While this interpretation has some merit, we believe the market is overly optimistic about US economic resilience and may overestimate both the level and duration of higher interest rates.
We expect inflation to level off and US activity to moderate slightly in 2027, allowing rate expectations to shift from further hikes to a pause, followed by cuts later in the year. Beyond these cyclical factors, structural headwinds for the USD remain a topic, notably the US fiscal deficit trajectory.

AMD was also the victim of a short seller attack last night.
Shares in the $802bn US chip company fell by as much as 11% yesterday after reports connected to the short seller Hunterbrook raised concerns about its ties with China.
HunterbrookMedia raised concerns around AMD's dealings in China, claiming that it leaves the company more vulnerable to US chip export restrictions and citing its extensive supply chain in the country.
It said:
Most AMD processors face few export restrictions today. The trouble is that AMD's growth story rests on the chips that face tighter restrictions.
Meanwhile its newsletter, TheBearCave, also highlighted that AMD's use of datacentre lease guarantees and backstops for unsold cloud capacity.
It wrote:
On the surface, its financials look solid. But a proverbial AI capex bank run could test just how solid they are."
AMD has said in a statement that the report's claims "misrepresent AMD's business practices and relationships".
It added:
AMD complies with applicable US export control laws and regulations and maintains rigorous processes to ensure compliance across our global operations.
Investors however still seem spooked - AMD shares are down by 5.4% in pre-market trading.
The UK's stock market has a reputation for its rather meagre selection of tech stocks, which typically protects it from AI-driven sell-offs. But some of the pain is being felt here today too - the FTSE 100 is now down by about 0.9%, to its lowest level since June.
Its worst performer is the Polar Capital Technology Trust, a listed investment company that mostly backs tech companies and a range of chip stocks such as Nvidia, AMD and TSMC. Its shares are down 5% this morning.
