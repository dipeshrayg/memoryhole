I would understand, after the week in the news we've had, if you were not feeling quite ready to deal with the new instalment of I Am ... , Dominic Savage's anthology series that examines different aspects of women's lives and tends to do so with merciless precision and in pitiless detail.
The stories are worked out with the female actor central to each: Vicky McClure in 2019's inaugural I Am Nicola, about coercive control, Samantha Morton in one about the vulnerabilities poverty brings, Suranne Jones imploding under the accumulated pressures of midlife and trying to be everything to everyone because nobody else will pick up the slack, and other heavy hitters like Lesley Manville and Kate Winslet starring with younger actors to showcase different generational concerns. The latest was devised with Nicola Coughlan.
I Am Helen is as harrowing in its unerring accuracy as the rest. By all means wait to watch it, but do watch it. Helen (Coughlan, in a superb everywoman performance) is five years into a relationship with Steve (Joe Cole). They are engaged and happy - or, as is generally the case in Savage's determinedly realistic universe, happy enough. We open with them going to bed together and starting to have sex, something they have obviously done many times before. This time, however, the mood shifts and he begins to choke her and doesn't desist as soon as she starts to struggle and say no. It's in those few moments between the starting and the stopping that essentially all of the drama lives. The next 90 minutes (divided into two parts) are an exploration of what they mean - for Helen, for Steve, for the couple, for us, for society and even, perhaps, for the future if we cannot close the gaps between the sexes, between communication and miscommunication, consent and compliance, reality and fantasy, needs and wants, love and service before they become gulfs that separate us for ever.
Helen is distraught. Steve is baffled ("We were having fun! Playing ... trying a few new things") and defensive. But if he is genuinely the former, why would he be the latter? Why not apologetic?
We see Steve at work, watching porn at his desk while his male colleagues - a mixture of amused and very much not - try not to notice. Later, he chats with one of the more amused, Martin (Nick Preston, in a performance so subtly nasty and compelling that it would have been interesting to see what he might have done with the main part) and between the two of them Savage brilliantly makes manifest the entire spirit of the manosphere.
Martin reassures him that he did nothing wrong - the whole a-man-has-needs justification falling on fertile ground - and shows him as proof the much stronger stuff he looks at online. Steve explains that he was only watching the video to give him something to think about after five years of boring sex with his girlfriend. Martin says he and his girlfriend keep things interesting with bondage - it just took "a bit of pushing" to get her there - and he uses sex workers when that's not enough. Later, he adds that there's always the option of dragging her upstairs and "belting her in the mouth" like you were allowed to do in the 60s and 70s.
"I can't tell if you're joking or not," says Steve, laughing but with that awful, hungry glint in his eye. Ah, jokes. Always telling.
We now see more clearly what Helen is unknowingly up against when she makes her personal pleas and puts her rational arguments to Steve, about not "just trying new stuff" that she does not and will never want. And we watch as he shifts the argument to feeling like less of a man due to her independence, lack of desire for children and her ambition to start her own business.
As ever in this series, the atmosphere is everything. The sense of threat and danger builds in layers, the toxicity by degrees until Helen - with the help of a friend and a women's health centre (a particularly acutely rendered scene) - must admit to herself that the situation is untenable and make decisions about who and what she wants the rest of her life to contain.
Some of the domestic scenes are a little repetitive - a lot of Steve shouting about not being heard and having his head done in without moving the drama on - and there are moments when it feels a little ripped-from-the-headlines. But they are fleeting. I Am ... remains a powerful, illuminating force in a world that seems to have more need of it than ever.
