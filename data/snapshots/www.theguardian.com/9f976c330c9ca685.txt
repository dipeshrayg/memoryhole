The UK wwould not have to follow the EU and apply tariffs on Chinese car imports under an Irish proposal that could prove another breakthrough for the prime minister, Andy Burnham, in his reset of relations with the bloc.
The proposal comes as the UK's foreign secretary, Ed Miliband, told a parliamentary committee that there had been "a transformation in the EU view".
Ireland, which holds the rotating EU presidency and plays a central role in steering legislation, has come up with a compromise under which the UK would be included in the bloc's new "Made in Europe" legislation without having to align with the EU's trade policies.
Made in Europe, or the Industrial Accelerator Act (IAA), is designed to protect the bloc's supply chains from cheap competition, mainly from China, particularly in the automotive and clean energy sectors.
It is understood that in a fourth compromise text, due to be considered by EU competition ministers on 3 December, Ireland has proposed giving the European Economic Area, Switzerland and the UK "partner" status for the assembly of cars.
This would allow not only cars such as the Mini, made at BMW's Oxford factory, to qualify as European, but also vehicles made by the Japanese firm Nissan at its vast Sunderland plant.
Under the compromise, a "partner" country would agree to mutual access to any subsidies available to EU-owned assembly plants, and to public procurement contracts for fleet cars.
Miliband said he thought the EU had considerably changed its view on UK inclusion since Burnham became PM.
"My sense is that view has been transformed and that is somewhat reflected in the agreement.
"I think we are heading for a good outcome on Made in Europe and I think there is a real understanding and we should also thank our our allies who argued for us to be in Made in Europe not outside," he said.
Irish negotiators have also suggested giving Japan and South Korea, which lobbied hard against the original Made in Europe proposals, access to public procurement and other public support schemes for car parts and components.
A spokesperson for the Irish presidency said: "Having listened closely to all member states, the presidency has proposed a pragmatic and balanced way forward on the IAA to deliver for European competitiveness."
Burnham last week took the biggest step towards deeper ties with the EU since Brexit, sealing deals to ease trade and create a mobility scheme for young people.
The latest iteration of the IAA text is designed to reflect the car sector's position as a cornerstone of European industry.
France, which championed the legislation, originally opposed the inclusion of the UK at all but dropped its opposition last month on condition that the UK dynamically align with the EU on tariffs on Chinese vehicles.
But this caused further tensions with the UK. The business secretary, Jonathan Reynolds, insisted the British government would not be coerced into imposing tariffs on Chinese vehicles and would only take steps that were in the national interest.
Mike Hawes, the chief executive of the Society of Motor Manufacturers and Traders, said the UK "should not become collateral damage" from measures intended to address concerns arising elsewhere in the global market.
The UK car industry has argued that it is so intertwined with its EU counterparts that both sides would be damaged if the UK was not included in Made in Europe.
Hawes said: "It is essential that the final arrangements recognise the unique UK-EU automotive relationship as allies in adversity, and do not create new barriers to our shared supply chains."
The latest proposal will be discussed by an EU working group involving industry leaders and EU member states on Friday, before being finessed further for consideration by ministers.
European carmakers are attempting to respond to a flood of cheap Chinese electric vehicles into the bloc. Volkswagen began wage talks with labour representatives on Tuesday amid mounting worker anger over deep cost cuts, with protesters carrying black coffins to symbolise what they said was the death of trust and the company's future prospects.
China faces further restrictions on its trade with EU: tariffs were imposed on Chinese EV imports in 2024, and quotas agreed with Beijing in October come into force on 1 December.
