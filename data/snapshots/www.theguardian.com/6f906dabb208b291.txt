UK's top polluter Drax could power country's largest AI datacentre
Jillian Ambrose
Britain's most polluting power plant could soon power the country's largest datacentre under a £20bn deal to build AI infrastructure on the site of the Drax power plant in North Yorkshire.
The power plant's FTSE 250 owner has struck a deal with infrastructure investor Brookfield and and the AI infrastructure platform Radiant to develop a datacentre with an eventual capacity of more than 1 gigawatt which could begin operations by early 2028.
In the initial phase of the project Drax will provide the datacentre project with land and access to the national power grid through existing connections on the site, but it hopes to begin powering the data centre directly by the early 2030s.
Drax said the development could attract more than £20bn ($26.8bn) in investment and represent "the next chapter" for the former power plant after ministers signalled plans to limit its power generation in the future.
Will Gardiner, the chief executive of Drax, said the project would "help secure the long-term future of the site and protect hundreds of jobs" while "supporting the North's reindustrialisation and strengthening sovereign AI capability".
The largest power plant in the UK produces almost 5% of the country's electricity by burning woody biomass pellets, making the site the single largest emitter of carbon dioxide, according to analysts.
These emissions are not accounted for by the UK government because Drax claims that the equivalent carbon emissions are absorbed by growing trees which provide the waste wood used to make the biomass pellets.

Garry White, chief investment commentator at the US investment bank Raymond James, said that Primark will need a disciplined returns policy for its planned new home delivery service.
Home delivery is an obvious gap in Primark's proposition, but it will need to be managed carefully given the retailer's position at the value end of the market.
The experience of other online fashion retailers shows that shoppers often order multiple sizes or styles with the intention of returning unwanted items, resulting in far higher return rates than those seen in physical stores.
Returns can be expensive, generating costs related to shipping, handling, quality checks, repackaging and, in some cases, markdowns on items that can no longer be sold at full price. As a result, Primark will need a disciplined returns policy if it is to keep costs under control and protect margins.
