"James Bond will return," says the closing title on No Time to Die, the most recent James Bond film and the final one to star Daniel Craig. It is just as well: Bond has to all appearances been vaporised by sea-launched missiles, having been infected by nanobots programmed to attack his nearest and dearest at the slightest touch. No one would believe that one of the world's most successful film series would come to such an abrupt halt, but no doubt a little reassurance helped.
Nevertheless, the first order of business for scriptwriter Steven Knight and director Denis Villeneuve will be dealing with it: how to revive and re-energise a character at the heart of the action. Speculation on their best way forward has been rife, and previous examples are not hard to find. Sherlock Holmes was killed off by Arthur Conan Doyle by fighting his arch enemy Professor Moriarty at the Reichenbach Falls in the story The Final Problem, only to resuscitate him a decade later with The Adventure of the Empty House. Superman is apparently killed at the end of Batman v Superman: Dawn of Justice, but resurrected in Justice League for the fight against Steppenwolf. Arguably the most enduring pop-culture return is Bobby Ewing's famous shower scene in the TV show Dallas in 1986, which attempted to ensure continuity by suggesting Ewing's death was his wife's dream. And even Ian Fleming toyed with killing off Bond, with a climactic poison scene at the end of his 1957 novel From Russia with Love, and leaving him amnesiac and vulnerable to Soviet attack at the finish of You Only Live Twice, the last Bond novel published in Fleming's lifetime.
Colin Vaines, producer of Coriolanus and Film Stars Don't Die in Liverpool, says the superhero-type reboot is the most probable way forward. "It's possible that, like Holmes, he could turn up having escaped death and then hidden out to protect his friends from assaults on them by the villains. But I believe it's way more likely that they are starting again as though nothing has happened, like it's a multiverse in a Marvel movie where old or dead or alternate versions of superheroes just turn up and do their thing."
Russell T Davies, who has had to confront similar recasting issues with his work as showrunner and head writer on Doctor Who, agrees that trying to stick to some kind of continuity - ie a plausible development of what has already been depicted as occurring, would be too much of a stretch. Davies, who is also the creator of Queer as Folk and It's a Sin and the writer of A Very English Scandal, says: "I'd never presume to give the great Steven Knight any advice. But if I had to, I'd say: you can't resolve this as a continuity issue, it's gone way beyond that. I'd just recast all the others - M, Moneypenny, Q, everyone - and simply have Robert Pattinson walk in for his next day's work. They've never commented on a new Bond, don't start now!"
Questions had been asked before over the future of the Bond character, particularly after it was revealed in the run-up to No Time to Die in 2019 that the 007 codename was to be given to Lashana Lynch's new MI6 agent Nomi. Was Bond really about to be put out to pasture? The ambiguity of the future of Bond - which was seen by some as a new front in the culture war - was underscored by Craig's open dislike of the role, and his determination that No Time to Die was his last film in the role.
Vaines suggests that the Bond death narrative was Craig's way of making sure he never had to come back to a series that, at that time, was still under the control of its original production company Eon. "I believe Daniel Craig wanted a definitive line drawn under his Bond, which everyone agreed on at the time. The Eon team may well have just planned to effectively start again the way that rather good opening sequence in the first Craig Bond played out, establishing after two kills that he was an 00 agent. That always felt like a excellent origin story."
He adds: "I hope Amazon MGM do something similar with Mr Pattinson, who I personally think is a really great choice: has the charisma and the acting chops, and such an interesting light and shade in his work."
As Bond heads into a new era, it is clear that wrestling over the past and future of this enduring icon is nothing new. Celebrated playwright David Hare, who has also written scripts for films including Damage, The Hours and The Reader, told the Guardian about his experience with the franchise.
"In 1972, Sandy Whitelaw, the head of production at United Artists took me out to lunch at Claridge's. As a decent and progressive person, he said he found it painful and embarrassing that so much of his company's income depended on a character whose treatment of women was deplorable, whose pleasure in violence was disgusting and whose politics were unspeakable. He wanted someone of my generation - I was 24 - to reconceive the character. Could I make James Bond less fascist?
"I told him that was way beyond my powers as a writer. Thirty-five years later, in 2007, the director of Quantum of Solace took me out to lunch at Nobu and outlined the identical brief. Some problems don't change, I guess."
