On the early November day that I met Pitchfork founder Ryan Schreiber in New York City, the website he created is all that anyone on my timeline can talk about, again. Today, it's thanks to a freshly dug-up tweet from Jon Ossoff, the newly re-elected Georgia senator, who sincerely wrote in 2012: "Looking forward to your writeup of the new @imaginedragons album."
It's an all-timer, up there with Phoebe Bridgers saying that she thinks about the site's review of Jet's second album (a video of a monkey pissing in its own mouth) once a day, and Halsey wishing that the Pitchfork office, which is on the site of the 9/11 attacks in New York, would "collapse already". The joke in Ossoff's case is that Pitchfork would not be caught dead praising the purveyors of stomp clap radio rock; a 2021 review characterized the band's music as "emotions fired out of a T-shirt cannon".
Since Schreiber founded the site in 1995, Pitchfork has become as synonymous with a roving sense of new music discovery as it has with sharp and often barbed criticism. While Spin and Rolling Stone were still focused on monthly print issues, the site met the speed of music consumption in the Napster age, publishing reviews when albums leaked online rather than waiting for CD drop dates, and helped to break a wave of, broadly, left-field white artists that included Arcade Fire, Fleet Foxes, Interpol, Sufjan Stevens and Grimes. By the time Schreiber sold the website to Condé Nast in 2015, the site was the most influential publication in music. He resigned three years later. (A disclosure: I have written for Pitchfork since 2018.)
Schreiber is in a jocular mood when I meet him, which might have something to do with the "blue tsunami" sweeping the country in the aftermath of the US midterm elections. If he's nervous about the release of his dishy upcoming memoir, he doesn't show it. Titled Weird Era: How Pitchfork Changed Music Forever, the book finds Schreiber opening up about the site's rapid rise and its sale to Condé Nast for the first time since he quit in 2018, as well as his own story of coming out as gay in his 40s. He is often unsparing when describing what he sees as his failings. "One of the most rewarding things has been able to be able to go on record and take accountability," he tells me, sitting on a cafe terrace in the last of the fall sun. "To be like, 'This is why it happened the way it did', or 'This was a mistake.'"
Growing up in a blue-collar Minneapolis suburb, Schreiber was a loner kid who was relentlessly bullied. Music was his escape: first Prince and Janet Jackson, and later Nirvana and riot grrrl, the feminist punk subculture that was born from Olympia, Washington. He clicked with a sentiment espoused by Kurt Cobain on a T-shirt he wore on the cover of Rolling Stone that read "Corporate magazines still suck." Schreiber was instead drawn to the DIY spirit and humor of Cake, a pop culture zine, as well as the righteous snark of British publications like Melody Maker and NME. "Zine culture made me feel that I could do this in a way that doesn't have to be from a formally educated, journalism degree background," he says. "When I got online, I was like, 'I could literally just start doing this by myself.'"
Launched in 1995 as Turntable and renamed Pitchfork a year later, the early site's reviews were scrappy and irreverent, but full of distinctive voice. Schreiber didn't know anything about how the music industry worked but was ballsy enough to cold-call record labels, and ended up scoring interviews with alt-rockers like Low, Kelley Deal and Modest Mouse. "I'd grown enough of a thick skin in school, being bullied," he says. "But my mom and dad were very nurturing. So despite the fact that I was up against it at school, and there was no way for me to be liked as a person, I didn't give a shit what anybody thought of me."
Early Pitchfork was anti-canon, unafraid to tear down rock legends like the Pixies' Frank Black, Metallica and Sonic Youth, whose 2000 album NYC Ghosts & Flowers was compared to an open mic night in hell. At times, they took it too far. When Schreiber's beloved shit-kicking alt rock hero Liz Phair went pop and teamed up with Avril Lavigne's producers, the album was given a rare 0.0 rating. In Weird Era, Schreiber writes: "Looking back now, it's the single best example ever set for how not to employ the 0.0 rating: as petty personal retribution."
Some of Weird Era's inside baseball may be lost on generalists, but longtime Pitchfork readers will probably pore over the nerdier details, like when Schreiber and his team debate whether Arcade Fire's Funeral was a perfect 10 (it was awarded 9.7) or the now quaint-feeling controversy that greeted the site's first pop-leaning tracks of the year list in 2003, which featured Outkast, Beyoncé and Justin Timberlake in podium positions. "It was a groundbreaking time for pop music and for R&B and hip-hop especially," he tells me. "I'm probably 24 at that time, and I couldn't not just have my mind blown by this incredible sound design. The fact that these songs are dominating pop radio was like, 'Oh, the world is waking up to more experimental and adventurous sounds.'"
I venture that perhaps it was less that the world was waking up to Black pop and hip-hop innovators than that the white critical establishment was finally beginning to take them seriously. "You're right," Schreiber says. "Part of it was me moving away from lily-white Minneapolis to Chicago," he says. "The culture around me changed very much as well." He was also impacted by critic Kelefa Sanneh's argument in his well-known 2004 essay The Rap Against Rockism about the deeper prejudices that might inform a music snob's disdain for pop and R&B. Once I read that, I was like: "Oh, shit. There's really something deeper at play here that I need to re-evaluate my entire stance on."
In the early 2010s, Pitchfork expanded into video, launched a short-lived film vertical called The Dissolve and a blog section called The Pitch. "I think I would have slowed down a little bit," Schreiber says. Moving with more caution may have helped avoid a notorious incident in 2012 when Pitchfork filmed a video interview with Chicago rapper Chief Keef at a shooting range, unaware that the then 17-year-old rapper was on parole and legally banned from handling firearms. After Keef's rival Lil Jojo was shot dead later that year, Pitchfork was suddenly at the center of a police investigation and was subpoenaed by Illinois prosecutors, who used the site's audio and video records as evidence to send Keef to juvenile detention.
Schreiber says he was devastated by the series of events. "It was a learning moment where we started to realize we need more checks and balances here, and maybe a little bit more of a diverse makeup of our team," he says. "This was well overdue, and it was time to put resources into this as company policy." He adds that the diversity of the team "absolutely" changed after that.
After leaving Pitchfork, Schreiber came out as gay. He says that the experiences that followed the end of his two-decade heterosexual marriage were among the most challenging of the book's passages to write about. "I'm quite guarded in some ways," he says. "I'm conscientious of what feels like a TMI moment." The closest that the very tactful Weird Era comes to that is a passage where he talks about a (failed) hookup through an unnamed dating app which is almost certainly Grindr. Did he have to write it through gritted teeth? "Yes," he says, laughing. "Oh god, yeah."
It took him back to the painful memory of being discovered fooling around with another boy at age 13 and the grilling from his parents that followed. It sounds horrifying - any closeted teenager's nightmare. "It buried that side of my sexuality," he says. After his mother read an advance copy of Weird Era this summer, he talked to her about the episode for the first time in decades. "She was very regretful about how the situation was handled," Schreiber says. "But I also understood it from her standpoint as I was writing it, and I was trying to step into her shoes. In the late 80s, Aids was perceived as such a death sentence in pop culture."
In the book he writes: "It wasn't until I was out of the spotlight and no longer the public face of the company that I began to question how much of my identity I'd engineered to lead the business and how much I'd suppressed because it didn't fit the image I felt I had to project."
I'm curious why Schreiber thought that being Mr Pitchfork was incompatible with being an out gay man. Put simply, he was afraid. "I did feel like, moving to New York, that this personality of who people understood me to be was already established," he says. "There was an element where I was like, 'beginning to experiment with my sexuality in any open way would lead to speculation and questions and confusion. I had worked so hard to build this reputation.'"
The idea that telling the truth could risk losing everything you've built will be familiar to many who have been in the closet. "I had not really ever given myself permission to explore it," Schreiber says.
By the mid-2010s, Pitchfork was in trouble, with dwindling ad revenue and a staff of 50 to support. Condé Nast had been circling for a while, and Schreiber met with executives in 2015 to discuss an acquisition. "What if selling out could save us?" he writes.
Condé promised to get Pitchfork out of its financial hole and put its corporate bigwigs on its business operations. "Like the indie band signing a deal with a major, part of me knew the promises weren't true," he writes. "But I still hoped Pitchfork might be the rare exception - the outlier that conquers the mainstream while retaining total creative control."
His skepticism was not unfounded. The acquisition was announced in a press release in which a Condé executive said: "Pitchfork brings a very passionate audience of millennial males into our roster." The internet had a field day, with Jezebel writing a story titled Bro, Condé Nast Bought Pitchfork! That Is Fucked News For Us Men.
Schreiber soon soured on Pitchfork's new home thanks to an ad team who were laughably ill-suited to manage site partnerships (among their proposed brand deals were with Topgolf complexes and Dave Matthews' wine company), as well as the mean girl energy of Condé HQ. In one memorable passage from Weird Era, Schreiber recounts how, after then-incumbent Vanity Fair editor Radhika Jones showed up to an interview with Anna Wintour wearing tights printed with a fox illustration, Wintour was so tickled by this fashion faux pas that she gifted all editors with fox-patterned stockings and socks for the holidays.
"Seeing it at hand was as ugly, if not maybe a little bit uglier than I anticipated," he says of Wintour's behavior, adding that he believes she has "a callous disregard for any empathy" and sees Condé Nast employees as "just a cog in a broader machine".
"I think she sort of likes the villain reputation," he goes on. "But it was a very harsh reality to be under her thumb, and at the same time disregarded and ignored. There was no warmth whatsoever."
Schreiber remembers his final day at Pitchfork as "one of the worst days. In the time since, I've looked back and gone, 'What if we'd held on? What if we tried to make some of these difficult decisions ourselves that were ultimately made for us?'" Has revisiting the time made him regret any of his decisions? "I wouldn't say that there's anything that writing the book has made me regretful of", he says. "It's just helping to process things."
Many felt that Condé showed their true colors in 2024 when they canned the majority of the site's staff in a move that also laid off Puja Patel, Schreiber's successor as editor-in-chief. (Wintour, one staffer reported, declined to remove her sunglasses while telling employees they had got the chop.) The site is now edited by Mano Sundaresan, founder of the No Bells music blog. "The new guard is killing it," says Schreiber, adding that he is friendly with Sundaresan.
Today, Pitchfork remains one of the few publications whose opinion you can trust about music at all in the wake of the erosion of alt weeklies, the blogosphere and just about every legacy music publication out there (those that are clinging on are either on life support or hailing latter-day Taylor Swift albums as instant classics on release). You might often disagree with Pitchfork scores, ruthlessly graded on a 10-point scale from 0.0 to 10.0, but the site is still the only publication out there that can get an entire friend group riled up over a decimal point.
Since selling Pitchfork, Schreiber has bounced between gigs, dabbled in advisory and management for new artists, DJed at art openings and curated a monthly playlist called What's Good. Recently, he's been experimenting with filming commentaries on front-facing video, TikTok style. "There's a sense of freedom in reinvention," Schreiber says. "I still quite enjoy writing as well. At the core, I'm still a critic, listener and pundit. It's something I can't turn off: I'm obsessed with music."
