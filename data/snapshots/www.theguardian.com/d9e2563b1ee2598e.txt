Arun Pillai-Essex, principal geopolitical analyst at the risk intelligence company Verisk Maplecroft, has looked at the impact on US trade policy.
He argues the US midterms point to a significant shift in the political landscape, with implications extending well beyond Washington. This is particularly the case for key trading partners and emerging markets in Asia, where US trade and technology policy has an outsized impact on investment and economic growth.
With affordability dominating the midterm debate, we expect growing bipartisan pressure for tariff relief, particularly from lawmakers representing agricultural states and areas with strong trade ties to Canada. We've already seen selective exemptions on Canadian imports ahead of the elections.
We expect Congress to become more assertive on trade policy, challenging tariffs through disapproval resolutions and greater oversight. While presidential veto powers limit their direct impact, growing bipartisan opposition could raise the political costs of further escalation.
However, we don't expect a fundamental shift in the White House approach. Tariffs remain an important bargaining tool, and further duties targeting manufacturing overcapacity across 16 major economies remain likely, adding another layer of restrictions following the forced labour tariffs imposed earlier this year.
The proposed overcapacity tariffs have a significant focus on Southeast Asia and the wider APAC region, adding to the trade pressures already facing regional exporters. A divided Washington is unlikely to offer much relief, with protectionist headwinds continuing to complicate trade and investment decisions across the region.
Overall, we expect the broader tariff framework to remain largely intact, but with greater scope for selective exemptions and negotiated concessions, while the White House keeps the threat of further escalation on the table.

Deborah Cunningham, chief investment officer of global liquidity markets at Federated Hermes, says:
Democrats pulled off a double victory in the US midterm elections that few saw coming at the onset of the year. The blue wave means the federal government is sure to be divided on most issues. That typically creates a blockade against new legislation - an environment which broad financial markets traditionally view favorably as variables decrease.
But liquidity investors are different: they primarily look to the US Federal Reserve headquarters further down the National Mall from the Capitol for guidance - and its perspective is unlikely to have changed. The Fed might have wanted to hike rates in October due to stubbornly high inflation, but that policy-setting meeting may have been too close to the election. (Market sentiment has it that Fed officials traditionally have avoided rate moves in those periods to avoid seeming biased to a political party.) That stance, and officials penciling one more increase in 2026 in their September Summary of Economic Projections, could mean a quarter-point hike in December. That would raise the fed funds target range to 4.00-4.25%.
That scenario would be music to the ears of cash investors, as the yields of most liquidity products have the potential to rise in concert with Fed hikes. And additional tightening in 2027 is likely. Why? Due to the lag between policy moves and their impact on the broad economy, inflation might not decline for several quarters. Also, the Fed tends to adjust policy in cycles rather than intermittently. But even if rates stay at present levels, industry liquidity offerings should, we believe, remain attractive.

Steven Chiavarone, chief investment officer for global equities at Federated Hermes, says:
Prior to the US midterm elections, we felt that only two outcomes could meaningfully change monetary and fiscal policy: a GOP sweep creating wider voting margins or a Democratic blue wave large enough to produce filibuster- and veto-proof majorities. While the Democrats have retaken both chambers of Congress, it appears not to be enough to create the latter scenario.
In the coming weeks, the stock market may experience volatility, but we think it should ultimately rise. Historically, the 12 months following midterm elections have been good for US equities. In fact, it has been 88 years since the S&P 500 last posted a negative return in that period, with an average of 14%. Why? One explanation is that the poor result of the president's party motivates it to champion economic policies that improve its chance of victory in the next presidential election. Another is that a divided government hinders the sort of drastic legislation that can unsettle investors, though this election outcome actually raises the odds of modestly stimulative policy passing.
While the present uncertainty and changing of the guard might make investors anxious, no US midterm election has ended the world, and this one will not either. We are looking past the noise, focusing on the real opportunities due to strong economic growth, significant infrastructure buildout and what may be the earnings cycle of our lifetime.
