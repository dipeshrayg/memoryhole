Rachel Winter, partner at Killik & Co, says
The market is experiencing a sizeable post-election bump, thanks to the removal of the pre-election uncertainty and the fact that Donald Trump has not contested the result. Ordinarily, a divided government is preferable for the markets, as the associated gridlock makes big legislative changes less likely. However, while the Democrats have won control of both the House and the Senate, Trump is now referring to himself as 'Mr Veto', which implies that a state of legislative gridlock will likely endure for the remainder of his presidential term.
The past few trading sessions have been characterised by a rotation out of tech and into other sectors as the Democrats advanced in the polls. Several polls have indicated that Democratic voters are more likely to oppose data centres, and new Democratic senators have been elected following campaigns that focused on greater restrictions on data centre construction. This is potential a problem for a variety of companies in this space, most notably CoreWeave, Oracle, SpaceX and Tesla.
Texas had accounted for over a quarter of planned data centre capacity, and some of this could now be at risk, particularly for Elon Musk, who selected Texas as the home of his business empire. With the new Texas Democrat James Talarico now in the driving seat, backed by a campaign platform supporting greater data centre regulation, all eyes will be on his next move.
Democratic control of Congress will make further conflict with Iran more difficult, which has likely driven the drop in Brent crude, as the world hopes for an easing of oil supply concerns. This should also be helpful in terms of bringing down inflation. With a prolonged conflict now harder to maintain, defence spending is less likely to rise, which has put both BAE and Northrop Grumman in negative territory.
Wall Street opens higher after Democrats secure Congress
Wall Street has surged at the open, after Democrats scored historic victories in midterm elections and secured control of US Congress.
The Dow Jones rose 2%, while the tech-heavy Nasdaq and the benchmark S&P 500 both gained more than 1%. That's quite a reversal: Overnight, stock futures fell sharply as investors worried about the prospect of tougher regulation of the AI sector and the impact on economic growth.
However, Michael Heydt, senior vice president and sector lead of global sovereign ratings at Morningstar, voices a note of caution.
The central consequence of Democratic control of Congress is likely to be gridlock rather than sweeping policy change. While Democrats may seek to advance healthcare, oversight, and affordability priorities, substantial legislative action will remain constrained by Senate procedures and presidential veto authority.
As a result, investors should focus less on major tax or spending shifts and more on the risks associated with government funding negotiations, debt ceiling debates, congressional investigations, and confirmation battles.
Although targeted bipartisan cooperation remains possible in areas such as permitting reform, energy infrastructure, and AI regulation, the next two years are more likely to be defined by institutional confrontation and political positioning ahead of the 2028 election cycle.

George Saravelos, global head of currency research at Deutsche Bank, has sent us his thoughts on the midterm elections.
1. Risk positive. We agree with the rebound in global equities and risky currencies overnight. The combination of a large Democratic majority in Congress and president Trump's statement that he is willing to work with the opposition should act as a break on extreme policy outcomes and bring down risk premia. The market is increasingly likely to interpret surprising policy shifts from the executive as time-bound by the next presidential election or the new domestic political configuration.
2. No news is good news on Iran. Over the last 48 hours, president Trump stated that no attack on Iran is imminent and has acknowledged that the Iran war drove part of the election outcome. We have been writing that with oil flows through the Middle East having materially recovered, the market is pricing a large imminent risk premium of re-escalation and energy supply disruption. There is therefore negative time decay on the oil price, the more escalation is delayed, the bigger the downward pressure on oil.
3. All about the debt ceiling (and AI) now. The single biggest source of domestic policy uncertainty remaining in the US is the debt ceiling that will become binding by Q2 of next year. It is reported that Republicans aim to pass a debt ceiling increase via reconciliation over the next few weeks. If this succeeds, it will remove one additional layer of uncertainty on fiscal policy, while also preventing AI and other policy matters from getting bundled up in a protracted negotiation with a Democratic Congress. Irrespectively, question marks around the build-out of AI will likely persist.
On balance, we see political developments overnight as supportive of a continued bottoming out in euro-dollar: lower energy and global policy risk premium but higher AI implementation risks should on balance weaken the dollar. If Congress fails passing a debt ceiling increase this year, we would consider it an added dollar negative.

Arun Pillai-Essex, principal geopolitical analyst at the risk intelligence company Verisk Maplecroft, has looked at the impact on US trade policy.
He argues the US midterms point to a significant shift in the political landscape, with implications extending well beyond Washington. This is particularly the case for key trading partners and emerging markets in Asia, where US trade and technology policy has an outsized impact on investment and economic growth.
With affordability dominating the midterm debate, we expect growing bipartisan pressure for tariff relief, particularly from lawmakers representing agricultural states and areas with strong trade ties to Canada. We've already seen selective exemptions on Canadian imports ahead of the elections.
We expect Congress to become more assertive on trade policy, challenging tariffs through disapproval resolutions and greater oversight. While presidential veto powers limit their direct impact, growing bipartisan opposition could raise the political costs of further escalation.
However, we don't expect a fundamental shift in the White House approach. Tariffs remain an important bargaining tool, and further duties targeting manufacturing overcapacity across 16 major economies remain likely, adding another layer of restrictions following the forced labour tariffs imposed earlier this year.
The proposed overcapacity tariffs have a significant focus on Southeast Asia and the wider APAC region, adding to the trade pressures already facing regional exporters. A divided Washington is unlikely to offer much relief, with protectionist headwinds continuing to complicate trade and investment decisions across the region.
Overall, we expect the broader tariff framework to remain largely intact, but with greater scope for selective exemptions and negotiated concessions, while the White House keeps the threat of further escalation on the table.
Turning to AI policy, he says:
Datacentre opposition cuts across party lines, driven by concerns over electricity, water and other local impacts. We're already seeing this translate into state-level action, with New York and Texas moving to restrict new developments and growing scrutiny over who ultimately foots the bill.
In a divided Washington, these concerns are likely to gain further political traction, particularly as both parties look to capitalise on public unease around AI. The debate over datacentres is increasingly feeding into a wider debate about the pace of AI adoption and its economic and social costs.
However, we don't expect the growing political backlash to significantly slow AI investment. Instead, developers are likely to face greater pressure to shoulder infrastructure costs, alongside tighter conditions on new projects.
But the underlying tension isn't going away. Washington wants to accelerate AI investment to maintain its technological lead, while state and local governments face growing pressure to protect households from high costs.
At the federal level, a divided government will sharpen tensions with Congress and the White House over US tech policy. Two areas to watch closely are advanced semiconductor export controls and restrictions on outbound US investment into sensitive technologies.
The central tension over semiconductor exports is between Congress pushing for tighter, legally binding restrictions and a White House seeking to preserve flexibility to negotiate, selectively ease controls and protect US commercial interests.
