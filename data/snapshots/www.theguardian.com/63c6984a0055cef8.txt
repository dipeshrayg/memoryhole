Sydney Tripoli has collected Jeff Buckley memorabilia since 2019. She keeps her collection of T-shirts, posters, vinyl and other merch celebrating the late singer-songwriter in what she affectionately calls her "Buckley box". The 27-year-old scours eBay and resale sites for these pieces; lately she's noticed the prices "skyrocket".
"During the pandemic, it was impossible to find Jeff Buckley shirts. Now they're everywhere on Depop," said Tripoli, who works as an actor in Los Angeles. "Now [my collection] is worth thousands of dollars."
Tripoli was born in 1999, two years after Buckley's death at age 30 from an accidental drowning in the Mississippi River. She first heard his moody, blues-tinged music as a child. In college she wrote a paper on 1994's Grace, the only album released during the artist's lifetime. She felt it was the perfect soundtrack for that time in her life: equal parts horny and romantic, ecstatic one moment and hopeless the next.
Though Buckley was solidly a product of generation X, with slacker posturing and a distaste for the music industry's corporate trappings, his songs seem to speak to members of gen Z like Tripoli.
Buckley's impassioned, unguarded lyrics made him an instant critical darling, but initial sales of Grace were slow, perhaps because such earnestness seemed out of step with the era's more grungier tendencies. But a spate of posthumous releases helped find him new fans. In 2004, the teen drama The OC used his cover of Leonard Cohen's Hallelujah as an emotional needledrop at the end of its first season. The same song hit the top of the iTunes charts in 2008 after American Idol judges Simon Cowell and Randy Jackson called it their favorite version.
David Bowie once called Grace his "desert island" album; Bob Dylan said Buckley was the greatest songwriter of the 90s. Brad Pitt tried and failed to materialize a Buckley biopic he wanted to star in, going so far as to invite the musician's mother to his wedding with Jennifer Aniston.
Now Buckley is once again finding new fans. Streams of his songs have steadily ticked upwards since 2022, just as the oldest of gen Z reached their mid-20s. It's a fitting renaissance for a nostalgia-soaked generation who have also propelled romance novels to the top of bestseller lists. It's a group that came of age during Covid lockdowns, that is less likely to date or enter relationships. They fetishize the act of wanting and unrequited desire.
Chuchu Zulu, a 19-year-old political science student in Orlando, attributes this popularity to "the yearning Jeff Buckley craze". Yearning is a slow burn, an antidote to on-demand culture. Buckley's ornate, louche music is fundamentally anti-algorithm. "We don't really have a Jeff Buckley for our time," Zulu added.
Data from the music analytics company found that Buckley's belting breakup anthem Lover, You Should Have Come Over has been used in more than 500,000 TikToks this year. It has been streamed more than 25m times in the past month. A TikTok representative shared that searches for "Buckley" have increased more than 30% in the last two weeks.
Three of his tracks are on the melancholic - and popular, with more than half a million followers - Spotify playlist "yearner final boss", alongside adult contemporary staples such as The Goo Goo Dolls' Iris and Prince's Purple Rain.
Yaw Appiah, 19, studies journalism at Rutgers University, where he writes music reviews for the school paper and works on its college radio station. He said Buckley epitomizes "the yearning epidemic". "It's such a coveted emotion," he said. "It's so prevalent all around us that it leads us to gravitate towards pieces of media that feature yearning."
Renewed interest in Buckley has been buoyed by the documentary It's Never Over, Jeff Buckley, which premiered at last year's Sundance film festival and is now on HBO. Experimental musician Rebecca Moore, who dated Buckley and inspired much of Grace, spoke at length for the first time in the film, humanizing her oft-mythologized ex. She described Buckley as an animal lover who would "throw himself over the counter at a bodega to hug a cute cat", but said that he struggled with mental health issues. In their last phone conversation just days before his death, he told her he might be bipolar.
Erika Freiman, a 22-year-old from Vancouver, works part-time in a record store. She said Grace is a bestseller, particularly among people her age. "It always sells, especially in the fall because it's super cozy and emotionally vulnerable," Freiman said. "It's super romantic, too. I think if someone buys you Grace for your birthday, it says a lot."
The unbridled candor of Buckley's lyrics speaks to a generation that's literate in therapy speak but disillusioned by its platitudes. "A lot of people are yearners, but they're too scared to show it," Freiman said. "You can hide behind a screen and comment on TikTok saying, 'This is so me,' but people have a hard time doing it in real life. People want that connection, and this generation is just so lacking of it. It doesn't really happen, so it's kind of like a fantasy."
Tripoli, from Los Angeles, added that Buckley's songs are the antithesis of the misogynistic drivel that comes out of the manosphere, which young women are all too familiar with in 2026. "The concept of a man in his 20s describing a woman with such beauty and respect is so rare," she said. "Even when it has some of his songs with more sexual undertones, I feel like it's still so pure and whole." (Joan Wasser, a musician who performs as Joan As Police Woman, dated Buckley and described him in the documentary as "a really staunch defender of woman".)
Sammi Katzmann and Jenna Pascale are a cello-saxophone duo based in New York who go by KatzPascale. The pair have covered Buckley's songs in concert. Both are gen Z, but they think the music resonates with people of all ages.
"I feel that it's timeless, and it just happens to be having a revival moment right now because it's so good," Katzmann said. "I was in Paris the other day, and they were playing Jeff Buckley the entire time, everywhere."
