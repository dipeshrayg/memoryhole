Usman Khawaja has outlined a proposed joint statement by the entire Australia team of 2018 in which they would accept collective responsibility for ball tampering in South Africa to help them move on from the sandpaper crisis.
Speaking to the Grade Cricketer podcast on Wednesday, the former Test opener shared his suggested wording for this collective mea culpa and said it has been put to Todd Greenberg, the chief executive of Cricket Australia (CA), for his consideration.
Khawaja's proposed statement, as dictated to the hosts Sam Perry and Ian Higgins, reads: "We accept knowledge as a team that we knew we were ball tampering by artificially altering the condition of the ball in South Africa in 2018.
"Whether it was sandpaper, or by any other means is irrelevant. We knew what we were doing and we take full responsibility as a team and squad for our actions, something we should have done eight years ago.
"Furthermore, we would like to acknowledge that Steven [Smith], Cameron [Bancroft] and David [Warner] paid an enormous price for the sake of the team for which we are sorry and eternally grateful."
While it appears a possible route out of the current mess - amid which conflicting accounts have emerged and CA has set up an "independent forum" for players and staff to privately share their recollections on that ill-fated tour - it also appears optimistic given how entrenched positions have become.
Pat Cummins, the current Test captain, gave a 68-minute press conference in Sydney a fortnight ago that included a straight "no" when asked if he was aware of ball tampering at any point during that South Africa series. His fellow seamer Josh Hazlewood has since said he backs "everything" Cummins said that day.
Along with Mitchell Starc and Nathan Lyon, the pair also released a carefully worded bowlers' statement in 2021 that denied knowing that sandpaper was going to be used on the field in Cape Town. Darren Lehmann, the former head coach, has also denied knowledge of sandpaper use during a recent interview with ABC.
Even though Khawaja's proposed statement offers some wriggle room here - the line about the method of ball tampering in South Africa being "irrelevant" - it would still represent a significant climbdown by those individuals.
Khawaja said that Greenberg's response to the suggested peace deal was the following: "[Greenberg] was like: 'Yeah, look, I'm not sure. I'll get in touch with the other parties and I'll let you know. But I'm not sure if this is going to fly.'
"He said to me: 'Look, no matter what at least we can look each other in the eye and say that we are trying everything possible.'"
In the Grade Cricketer interview - originally set up to discuss his autobiography, Sabr, but twice postponed due to the public firestorm the chapter about ball tampering triggered - Khawaja insisted he was not trying to destabilise the current side or see Cummins removed as captain.
Still, Khawaja was taken aback by the Cummins press conference. The pair had been in contact that week, only for Cummins to go well beyond a public denial of his former teammate's account of events in South Africa and repeatedly question his character and integrity.
Khawaja said: "That was a really difficult time for me too, because it kind of felt like everyone thought that I just made this up and I was lying about this."
He continued: "I've always loved Pat Cummins, the person and the leader. I just was never expecting him to come out and attack me personally like that.
"Personally, the way he did it for so long and just kept going and kept going, I've never seen that side of Pat before. And that's what just really disappointed me. I always wanted Pat to remain captain."
Khawaja expanded on his disappointment with Cummins later, telling the Sky Sports Cricket Podcast: "I thought Pat had this really great opportunity to come out and bring everyone together. Then, obviously, the personal attacks were sad. I'm not going to lie about that, they hurt from a friend, someone you really respect. That wasn't very Pat Cummins-like, very different to the Pat Cummins I know, and it's hard for me to judge him on that.
"Right now, are we talking? No, not really, but I've still got a lot of hope in my heart that at some stage if there's anyone that can bring everyone together, it's Pat. He's got the opportunity of power, whether it's now or later."
Asked during the Grade Cricketer interview if he could now confirm that knowledge of the sandpaper method included the bowlers, Khawaja again stopped short but added: "You're smart individuals. Like you can make up your own mind. I'm not here to convince anyone.
"I'll share the burden with Cameron Bancroft, Steven Smith and Dave Warner. I'm hoping other teammates will eventually have the courage and the confidence to do the same thing."
Despite the fallout, Khawaja is unrepentant about revisiting the sandpaper affair in his autobiography. His intention was to bring the issue back to the surface and allow others to then share their accounts, not least Bancroft as the junior player who was hit with a nine-month ban (to go with 12 months for Warner and Smith).
Bancroft published a detailed 4,000-word statement last week in which he revealed the extent of ball tampering by that team, said he was directed to use sandpaper by the bowling coach David Saker, and included the words: "WE ALL KNEW."
Khawaja said: "I knew the first guy that was going to talk about this - and that was me - was always going to get nailed. But that day I had a message from Cameron Bancroft. He said to me: 'Uzzy, you've done the right thing and I appreciate it more than you can ever imagine.'"
Two days after the Cummins press conference - the trigger for Bancroft to go public - Khawaja then received the following from Bancroft: "[What] you've given to me is an absolute blessing. You've paved the way for me to be able to talk about this openly and honestly. And I'm forever grateful for what you've done."
As well as discussing the issue at length, and acknowledging the personal toll for all involved, Khawaja confirmed a recent report by the broadcaster Gerard Whateley that an unnamed Australia player suggested using a sweet to shine the ball during a series against Pakistan in the United Arab Emirates later that year.
Justin Langer, who had replaced Lehmann as head coach, apparently told the Amazon documentary crew that was following the team to stop filming before issuing a dressing down to the individual and the team at large.
Khawaja said: "This blow-up was one of the all-time great blow-ups. It was what we needed because of what just transpired [in South Africa], literally the first game after there needed to be a line in the sand kind of thing.
"I can't remember if he actually said this [but it was like] 'if any of you guys ever even think about this again, you will not play for Australia again. This is why I loved JL during that time. He was the exact coach we needed."
