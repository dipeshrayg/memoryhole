Ministers have promised to create an entirely new welfare system for young people as they gave their backing to Alan Milburn's 278-page report, promising spending cuts next year to help pay for his proposals and giving him a government role to push them through.
Andy Burnham, the prime minister, told his cabinet colleagues on Tuesday morning the report was a "watershed moment" and offered a "comprehensive vision" as he invited its author to address the meeting.
He then sat in the Commons in a public display of support as his work and pensions secretary, Pat McFadden, backed Milburn's vision of a youth employment system with much more employment support but significantly lower benefit payments.
"We need a new settlement for young people," McFadden said. "This new settlement must give people the best chance of higher living standards for the whole of their lives. It must change the equation that sees us spend £25 on the consequences of inactivity for every pound to be spent on opportunity."
He added that the search for departmental cuts to help pay for the proposals would begin almost immediately.
"I have no doubt it will also be an issue in the spending review, but the first thing we should do is reprioritise spending to get more support to young people," he told MPs. "And I'm happy to do that from my department."
As he was speaking, the government announced Milburn would be given a role as chair of a new youth opportunities unit, monitoring the implementation of his proposals across government.
Milburn's plans would involve a new youth employment system based on major state intervention to help young people into work, to be paid for in the long term by cuts to disability and sickness payments for under-25s.
The job support schemes were likely to prove so expensive, Milburn said, that the entire package would only save the government £200m a year.
"I know that much of the public debate about this issue centres on welfare reform," he told a press conference in London. "For some people, the only exam question they're trying to answer is how to cut the benefits bill. That is important, and it does need to be done. But the primary question for me is not about saving cash, it's about improving lives."
Under Milburn's plan the government would spend nearly £2bn a year on a youth opportunity service, which would provide support and advice to give every young person a guaranteed route into education, training or work.
It would involve spending just over £2bn to create more vocational training courses and apprenticeships, and a further £1bn in direct payments and tax breaks for companies that hire young people.
The tax breaks would include national insurance cuts for businesses that hire young people, starting with the hospitality sector.
This was one proposal McFadden refused to back explicitly on Wednesday, saying: "We know that employment costs matter and we will consider all these things as we move forward."
The new system would be paid for largely by taking disability and sickness benefits away from young people and replacing them with a single youth allowance, which would be considerably below what some people can claim currently.
People aged 18 to 24 would no longer be able to claim personal independence payments, which would be replaced by a new allowance available only to those whose conditions are "well established, significant and ongoing".
The benefit cuts would be expected to save £3.6bn by 2036-37, with an additional £2.4bn being generated by higher tax receipts. That money would be almost entirely swallowed by the £5.8bn cost of the new system, Milburn said.
Milburn began his review last year amid concern at the top of government over the rising numbers of young Neets. That figure reached 1 million earlier this year, equating to 13% of young people - a higher rate than in any European country except Romania.
"Britain is at risk of losing a generation of young people," he warned.
Ministers and Milburn himself have worked hard to avoid the response which greeted Keir Starmer's attempts last year to cut the benefits bill by £5bn by restricting access to disability payments.
The former prime minister backed down in the face of opposition from his own MPs, in a humiliating retreat that arguably began his long departure from office.
As well as addressing the cabinet, Milburn spoke to Labour MPs on Monday night, while Burnham spoke at a second parliamentary meeting on Tuesday morning.
Milburn criticised Starmer's approach during his press conference on Tuesday, calling it a "fiscal grab", adding: "I do not think that's the way to do it."
Early signs suggest the outreach efforts have been successful, with several of the architects of last year's rebellion speaking in favour of the proposals.
Debbie Abrahams, the chair of the work and pensions select committee, called the review "an opportunity of a generation", adding: "We must change the status quo."
Marie Tidball, one of the few visibly disabled MPs in the Commons, said: "I welcome the government's significant shift in approach and the Milburn review's emphasis that there won't be crude cuts to benefits and instead reforms to fundamentally change our welfare system."
However, some Labour MPs and charities have criticised the proposed cuts to disability benefits.
John McDonnell, a former shadow chancellor, said: "The question does remain why is it that young people, if benefit cuts go ahead, are forced to pay for the new system, and we're not taxing wealth?"
Katie Schmuecker, a principal policy adviser at the Joseph Rowntree Foundation, said: "Cutting sick and disabled young people's benefits should not be part of this package.
"Financial support is part of what helps young people take up earning or learning. The wider support offer won't succeed if the young people it is meant to help are also struggling to afford food or the bus to college."
