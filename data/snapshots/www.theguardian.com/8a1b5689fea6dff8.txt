It's the biggest song in the UK, but have you ever heard it? That was the question many were asking this week, after Sam Fender and Olivia Dean's Rein Me In became the longest-running No 1 single in UK chart history, spending an unprecedented 19 weeks at the top.
The British Phonographic Industry said the song had resonated with listeners "in such a profound way", while Fender thanked Dean and fans "for making this song the juggernaut that it is".
Yet the record prompted a wave of bafflement online from people who claimed they had never knowingly heard it. "Crazy how monoculture no longer exists because I don't think I've actually ever heard this song," wrote one user on X.
The disconnect has reignited a broader debate about whether Britain's music charts still reflect a shared national culture. How can a record dominate the charts for nearly five months, and win song of the year at the Brits, while leaving large sections of the public feeling as though it passed them by? Only those living under a rock when the Beatles or Elvis Presley had a No 1 single could have avoided knowing about it.
The answer, according to industry experts, is that dominance no longer means ubiquity.
"In the era of Top of the Pops, Radio 1 and Smash Hits, the weekly charts were a shared cultural event," said Chris Anderton, associate professor in cultural economy at Southampton Solent University. Streaming has replaced that with endless choice and algorithmic recommendation, he said, producing what author Eli Pariser called the "filter bubble" - where listeners are "repeatedly served music similar to what they already like". It's the same mechanism, Anderton noted, that isolates social media users from opposing viewpoints: taste, like opinion, has been commodified and automated.
The record for the longest spell at No 1 was previously held by Frankie Laine, whose I Believe spent 18 weeks at the top in 1953, when the UK singles chart was less than a year old and featured just 12 songs. More recent contenders, including Bryan Adams' (Everything I Do) I Do It For You, Wet Wet Wet's Love Is All Around, Drake's One Dance and Ed Sheeran's Shape of You, all fell short of breaking the record.
Rein Me In was written by Fender for his album People Watching before being re-recorded as a duet and released in June 2025. It took 36 weeks to reach No 1, and has held the top spot on and off since February, seeing off brief challenges from Harry Styles, Olivia Rodrigo, Ariana Grande and Taylor Swift. It also outlasted the "accelerated decline" rules introduced by the Official Charts Company in 2017 to specifically stop streaming hits lingering indefinitely.
The mechanics of a No 1 hit single have changed too. Chart position once meant physical sales; today it blends sales with stream-equivalents from Spotify, Apple Music and YouTube - so 100 premium streams, or 600 ad-funded ones, equal one sale.
But the fragmentation runs deeper than streaming maths, said the music and culture critic Nicolas-Tyrell Scott. Younger listeners now discover music through TikTok, YouTube and personalised algorithms, meaning "it's not as easy as saying a track is No 1, therefore it's resonant and dominating in everyone's sphere of influence".
Gen Z spend just 16% of their average audio day on the radio, Scott noted, against 42% on streaming and 20% on YouTube - and platform algorithms actively reinforce those micro-cultures. "It's easy to see why a song like this could pass clusters of people by, despite dominating commercially," he said.
Chart analyst James Masterton put it starkly: a single can break records "yet it still struggles with any kind of cultural cut-through". Unlike past mega-hits, Masterton argued, this one has no film or event attached to it. "It isn't from a movie soundtrack or connected with any national event. It just ... exists."
Weekly chart consumption has grown from around 20m streams at the start of the decade to more than 30m now, Masterton said, but most No 1s this year have clustered around just 60,000 of those streams - proof that while the audience has grown, the biggest hits command a much smaller slice of attention than they once did. Television exposure barely moves the needle either: this year's Brit awards produced no lasting bump for the acts it celebrated, while radio itself has splintered into narrowly targeted playlists.
"This is merely one symptom of what sociologists are noting as the demise of the monoculture," he said. "With such a vast range of entertainment options available, few creations or productions really need appeal to a large cross section of the public. Which is just as well given how vanishingly few actually do."
Not everyone is convinced the disconnect is genuine. Some online commentators have suggested that claiming never to have heard a massive song says more about performing detachment than actual unfamiliarity.
Anderton offered a caveat. Songs such as Padam Padam, or the Brat summer moment, broke through because they escaped streaming and became wider cultural phenomena across television, social media, news coverage and memes. "The ubiquitous hit is not dead," he said. "It just requires much more than chart success."
