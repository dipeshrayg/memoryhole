The economy has been more resilient to the recent energy cost shock than expected.
Activity has held up better than anticipated, unemployment has risen only gradually, and redundancies remain relatively low. That may sound like good news. In lots of important ways, it is.
But a low-hire, low-fire labour market creates its own problems. It protects those already in work while making it much harder for those trying to get in or move on.
This, Lombardelli explains, creates three challenges for the Bank of England:
First, it affects how the Bank of England interpret the rise in unemployment. "If those looking for work are poorly matched to the jobs available, higher unemployment may do less to ease wage pressures than it has in the past," she says.
Second, it can weaken productivity and growth - it's harder for workers to find job opportunities.
Lombardelli says "The costs are falling particularly heavily on young people, for whom the first step into work has become harder."
Third, it can make the economy less able to adapt.
Newflash: the UK government has launched a leak inquiry into how the media reported details of its bank tax hours before last month's budget.
JamesBowler, Permanent Secretary at the Treasury, has told parliament's Treasury Committee that a probe is underway.
This inquiry will, presumably, examine how Bloomberg reported at 10.45am on budget day that chancellor John Healey would hike bank taxes less steeply than feared, and only increase the bank surcharge by 3%.
That was at least two hours before Healey did indeed announce this in his budget speech.
Bloomberg's report caused bank shares to rise (as we blogged here) - so it is a classic example of a market-moving leak.
Bowler told the Treasury committee in a letter, just released:
I have been assured that no banks, their representatives or their trade bodies were briefed by Treasury on the details of the changes to the Bank Corporation Tax Surcharge before the publication of the Budget and the Chancellor's statement to the House.
However, I can confirm that an investigation, lead by the Government Security Group (GSG) in the Cabinet Office, is underway. The Treasury is supporting that work. I won't prejudge its findings.

Newsflash: US inflation rose faster than expected last month, as Americans continued to suffer from rising food and energy prices.
The US Bureau of Labor Statistics has reported that the Consumer Price Index rose 0.4% on a monthly basis, higher than the 0.3% rise forecast by economists.
The report found that food prices rose by 0.5% in October, while energy prices jumped by 0.7% month-on-month.
The index for shelter (housing) rose 0.3%.
Core US inflation, which strips out food and energy, rose by 0.3% for the third month running.
Indexes that increased over the month include recreation, medical care, motor vehicle insurance, household furnishings and operations, and education.
But used car and truck prices, and communication costs, decreased month-on-month.
Lombardelli: AI will make junior economist jobs much more interesting
Q: Does the Bank of England agree with US economist Jason Furman who gave a provocative estimate last week that AI could displace at least half of analytical economics jobs?
ClareLombardelli agrees that Furman's piece was "very provocative."
She then explains that it's "quite astonishing" what AI can do.
She says it will fundamentally change what economics jobs are, and make them rather more interesting.
It will mean that junior economists doing a lot less chart formatting, and more time thinking about the economy.
Does it mean fewer people? Or does it mean people doing much better work?
Lombardelli says there will be "a balance between the two".
Q: How restrictive is the current Bank rate of 4%, given rising R* around the world and the UK's solid economic growth?
[R* is the neutral rate of interest, where it's neither restricting growth nor stimulating it. Central bankers can be reluctant to estimate it too precisely...]
Asked about the risks of an AI bubble bursting, ClareLombardelli replies that we are "likely to see lots of changes" around the valuations of AI companies.
But while there may be a lot of volatility, that doesn't mean AI won't be transformative.
Q: Why has the UK labour market showed resiliance, despite recent economic shocks?
ClareLombardelli suggests the Bank may be "systematically underestimating" the economy's ability to respond to shocks.
The BoE's economic models may underestimate the ability of companies, and households, to react.
Another possibility is that the economy's resilience is being eroded - with companies eating into their profit margins, and households running down their savings.
