Newsflash: The Trump administration has proposed new tariffs on 14 major trading partners, including China, Japan and the European Union, according to reports.
The US Trade Representative's office announced the duties after completing an eight-month Section 301 unfair trade practices probe into 16 trading partners, including close allies Japan, the European Union and South Korea, Reuters reports.
The move is designed to clamp down on 'structural excess industrial capacity'.
A senior US official said that tariffs were proposed for goods from China, Bangladesh, Cambodia, the European Union, India, Indonesia, Japan, South Korea, Malaysia, Singapore, Switzerland, Taiwan, Thailand and Vietnam.
The specific rates are expected to be published in a US Federal Register notice later today.
The move follows the Supreme Court's striking down of broader tariffs announced by President Trump.
Associated Press have more details of today's move:
The legal basis of the planned tariffs is Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, which enables the U.S. government to investigate and retaliate against foreign trade practices that violate existing deals, are not justified by market forces or actively discriminate against American goods. A senior administration official told reporters that the new tariffs would be consistent with the trade deals that Trump reached last year with dozens of countries after threatening severe tariff hikes .
Brazil was singled out for extra punishment after talks on trade issues stalled, the official said. The U.S. is proposing higher import taxes for raw and refined sugar and a new 10% tariff on aircraft, a move that would hit Embraer, Brazil's largest aircraft maker.
For some Chinese goods, the new 20% tariff will be stacked on top of other import taxes for a total rate of 45%.

Poundland collapses into administration, putting 12,000 jobs at risk
Sarah Butler
Poundland has called in administrators putting 12,000 jobs at risk ahead of Christmas after its owners rejected at least two last-ditch bids for the budget retailer.
Administrators at AlixPartners have been appointed to the retailer, which has 600 stores in the UK and Ireland by Poundland's directors. The chain is now expected to be broken up with significant job losses and store closures.
The retailer's US owner, Gordon Brothers, which bought Poundland for a nominal £1 last year, has been under pressure to accept an offer that would save jobs and protect thousands of the retailer's suppliers.
High inflation and heavy competition from budget stores, online specialists and supermarkets have left the 36-year-old retailer - the UK's first and biggest nationwide single-price retailer - struggling.
The retailer's board filed a notice of intention to appoint administrations last month - which was later extended - protecting Poundland from the demands of creditors until 26 November while a solution to its problems was sought.
Just two bidders interested in the whole company remained in talks right up until this week's deadline.
Martin Property Group, a Northern Irish family-owned company, which has been working with the existing management, led by Barry Williams, for about two months, remained interested.
It was joined by a last-ditch bid from Germany's Zachert Private Equity, a fund founded by a former Bloomberg journalist that specialises in acquiring mid-sized companies such as the German photo studio group Studioline and the US food and drink company Brands Within Reach.
Other bidders for the whole group included the little-known finance group Xyra Group Holdings, and the Irish investment company Innovest, which both fell out of the bidding race over the past week.
Donald Trump's tariffs have contributed to higher prices for US consumers, and a Reuters/Ipsos opinion poll conducted after Republicans' midterm losses found 62% of respondents supported efforts to repeal them.
But Trump has long vowed to ‌restore duties struck down by the US Supreme Court in February. The latest measures follow an eight-month investigation into unfair trade practices under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, Reuters reports.
US trade representative Jamieson Greer said the proposed tariffs would not apply to about $30bn of Chinese imports under consideration for tariff reductions through a bilateral mechanism known as the Board of Trade.
However, the new duties would be added to tariffs imposed on Chinese goods during Trump's first term, which total roughly 25%.
They would not be imposed on top of the 10% and 12.5% tariffs introduced in July on a broader group of 60 trading partners over allegations of lax enforcement of anti-forced-labor laws.
Tariffs have proven unpopular with US voters, who handed Democrats a decisive victory in the midterm elections that will give the party control of Congress. The Yale Budget Lab estimates that tariffs currently in effect have added an additional $900 in costs for each US household.
The rising cost of living was the number one issue for voters ahead of November's midterm elections, according to a Harris poll survey for The Guardian. An earlier Harris poll found seven in 10 Americans blamed tariffs for higher prices.
Donald Trump is keeping true to his threat of "obnoxious" tariffs, after the supreme court ruled that he had overstepped his legal authority in imposing his "liberation day" tariffs last spring, our colleague Lisa O'Carroll explains.
In this explainer we looked at the various backdoors available to him to reimpose tariffs, which he once described as his favourite term:
The 301 tariffs, which he is threatening to impose today, were considered the most "obnoxious" as they allow for unlimited tariffs to be imposed for four years against countries with unfair trade practices. If applied to the EU today - and not challenged or overturned - they would hold until 25 November 2030, two years after Trump's current term of office ends. But the EU has already passed legislation including a sunset clause that would blow up the tariffs on 31 December 2029. MEPs had originally sought a 2028 expiration date but pushed it back in negotiations with member states keen to keep Trump sweet.
"The latest US tariff announcement is not as alarming as it looks, with these new tariffs essentially replacing - rather than stacking on top of - the previously announced Section 301 tariffs related to forced labour concerns," said Stephen Brown, chief North America economist at Capital Economics,
The increase in the effective tariff rate looks set to be a touch lower than we've been assuming for our forecasts, although the new tariffs will still provide a marginal boost to prices and keep core goods inflation firm into next year. Moreover, the US has left open the option of increasing tariffs if trade agreements - particularly with China - fall apart.
The USTR [United States Trade Representative] today confirmed its recommendations for tariffs under the Section 301 investigation relating to "Structural Excess Capacity and Production in Manufacturing Sectors". Recall that the US administration has long suggested that it would use these latest Section 301 tariffs to bring the overall US tariff rate, based on 2024 import shares, up from close to 11% currently back toward the 14% that applied prior to the Supreme Court striking down the IEEPA [International Emergency Economic Powers Act] tariffs.
Yet to avoid overall tariff rates exceeding those already agreed with some countries, the USTR has proposed a framework in which these new tariffs - likely to take effect in early 2027 - will substitute for the prior Section 301 tariffs imposed under forced labour concerns, rather than stack on top of them. For example, China and Vietnam reportedly face the highest new tariff, of 20%, but those will replace the 12.5% Section 301 forced labour tariffs that China and Vietnam already faced. For the EU, a new 15% tariff replaces the prior 10% one. The other surprise is that Mexico will not face a new, higher tariff, perhaps because the USTR is content that its USMCA negotiations with the country are going well. (Canada was not subject to this investigation).
For now, the key message is that the additional tariff burden is likely to be small, especially as various goods are also not subject to these new tariffs, Brown said.
That said, note that the US has left open its ability to hike these tariffs if trade agreements fall apart. For example, the tariff on China could reportedly rise to 40% if negotiations break down, while the USTR's reports give it flexibility to raise tariffs on other countries too. For example, the tariff ceiling on Vietnam is 35%, almost double the proposed tariff.
One final interesting point is that the USTR's framework for substituting between these two different Section 301 tariffs means the US will be able to maintain the broadly agreed tariff rates on various trading partners even if the courts eventually strike one of the two sets of tariffs down. That lends some support to our view that the Trump administration's tariffs will remain in place in some form for years to come.

Britain is being squeezed from three sides: energy, borrowing costs and now trade uncertainty. Fresh US tariff proposals arrive with markets already volatile, inflation rising and Ofgem announcing a 26% increase in the energy price cap from January. The Bank of England has already raised rates to 4% and left further increases on the table. Households risk paying more to heat their homes and service their debts simultaneously, leaving less money flowing into the wider economy.
The UK's absence from Washington's latest proposed tariff list offers some breathing space, although British businesses remain exposed through European supply chains and overseas customers.
A prolonged trade dispute could weaken export demand and delay investment, compounding the squeeze on spending at home. That sharpens the Bank's dilemma, persistent inflation could demand further tightening just as trade tensions threaten growth. For UK investors, diversification and close attention to companies' debt burdens and cash generation remain essential. Britain can escape the latest tariff list and still pay an economic price.
