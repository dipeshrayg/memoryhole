"You're lucky you don't have a father."
So says Danae, 16, to Iris, 11, as the older girl heads home to face her strict and reliably angry dad, the friends' carefree summer day on the Mediterranean island of Cyprus about to come to a crashing halt. For Iris, though, parental absence isn't anything like a plus. She's a drifting, lonely soul in her own home, left to her own devices by a mother wrapped up in her new boyfriend and a teenage brother distracted by his girlfriend.
In "Hold Onto Me," the luminous debut feature from writer-director Myrsini Aristidou, the father Iris never knew is a shadow behind the eyes and a wound in the heart of wonderfully expressive Maria Petrova, the blazingly talented newcomer who plays the youngster. Little wonder, then, that when Aris (Christos Passalis) - not dead, just estranged and feckless - blows back into town, Iris can't help but be drawn to him. Even when her dad reveals his true colors as a grifter who'd rather use his daughter in his scams than display familial love, Iris tries to hold onto him - with perilous results for both. Aristidou displayed similar dogged, impassioned commitment to her movie. To bring "Hold Onto Me" to screen, the Cypriot filmmaker weathered seven years of script development, financing challenges, Covid-wrought delays and the pressures involved in honoring her story and her island home by hiring multiple nonactors, not least to play her lead child characters.
"Coming from Cyprus or Greece, it's hard to find kids that are actors," Aristidou explains. "We don't have big industries. So I knew it was going to be nonactors playing Iris and Danae [Jenny Sallo]." The filmmaker had grappled with that reality on two shorts, 2015's "Semele" and 2017's "Aria." They both also starred young girls. In "Semele," Aristidou worked with a 9-year-old; in "Aria," a 16-year-old. "I guess they've called me the child whisperer," she says, smiling.
Petrova, who has a beautiful naturalism that shines from the screen, was only 10 when Aristidou shot her. "I had my hesitations," she admits, "because initially I wrote the script for a 13-year-old who was feistier and more teenage-y. But when I met Maria, it made sense to rewrite the script around her as a younger character who is not as feisty but has this internal strength and courage that come out. Maria has that in real life. When you're working with kids, with nonactors generally, it's always better if you cast someone who's close to the character."
The same went for her background artists, mostly untested locals from the towns of Nicosia and Limassol, where she shot. "They don't care about their image or what they're supposed to do. They just are. And there's a freedom in that, an authenticity. I love that and I wanted that," continues Aristidou of a cast of dozens - café-goers, bar patrons, racetrack attendees, teenage boys drinking and smoking their way through the school summer holidays - whom she painstakingly names in the closing titles. "It was important that I put them in the credits, that I give them this honor. They were part of this film that, for me, was a collective thing, that was shot in my country."
She was, too, equally rigorous in the casting of Passalis, an established actor and director in Greece. She first spotted him "from a distance" in a bar during the Thessaloniki International Film Festival in 2019 "when I was there doing the work-in-progress [development] for my film. I was like, 'he has a cool face ...' But I didn't make the connection that he was the guy in Dogtooth," she says. After sharing the script and inviting Passalis in for a casting, "I brought him back two or three times. And I saw him with a lot of different kids, to see the dynamic."
Having been shot over the summer of 2023, "Hold Onto Me" film finally bowed in early 2026 at Sundance, where it won the Audience Award: World Cinema Dramatic. And its awards season run continues. The Cyprus-Denmark-Greece international co-production has been selected for 58 festivals, including this year's edition of Thessaloniki. At the European Film Awards, being held in Athens in January 2027, the feature is one of three films nominated for the European Young Audience Award.
Capping all that, "Hold Onto Me" is Cyprus' first-ever official submission for Best International Feature Film, for which Aristidou thanks her country's Deputy Ministry of Culture and Ministry of Foreign Affairs for supporting the campaign's efforts. The film's hard-earned success is a conclusion in another way, too. After her last two shorts, Aristidou views "Hold Onto Me" as the final part of a triptych of father-daughter narratives. "These stories matter to me a lot. Beyond figuring out our parental relationships, which tend to eventually define our adult relationship choices, we need to allow space to understand and accept - and sometimes even forgive the other. Because not everything's intentional. And when you allow for that space, there's a chance for reconciliation or coexistence between us as humans."
Given the deeply empathetic, evocative way she's crafted and framed these stories of generational separation, disconnect and reconnect, it's perhaps unsurprising that the themes have emotional resonance for Aristidou. "My parents divorced when I was 9 months old, and I didn't grow up with my dad. I remember looking for photos with him and not having any. So there's a lot of truth in the film, and many things in it are very personal." She and her father did, though, reunite in her early teens. "We started bonding more, and I started to understand where he was coming from. But he's a writer - he's not a con artist!" she clarifies with a laugh. "Although when you're a writer, we are all kind of con artists. I think all creatives are."
Plus, in the end, for all that pain of separation, in their reunion, her father did make up for lost time in one notable way. "I got into university in New York for painting. And he was said: 'Now that you're going to be in America, maybe you should try directing. Cinema is the seventh art.' Then I just switched my degree and became a filmmaker, pretty much!"
Her family bonds, no matter how frayed, were also helpful in Park City. "When we got into Sundance, it was already a huge achievement, because they only choose 10 films from around the world. But during the ceremony, I knew that we were not going to win anything. So I entirely zoned out. Then, when they announced the award, my mom pushed me: 'Myrsini, it's you!' And I just woke up! Because I just didn't expect that."
Also, Aristidou reframed her thoughts from the past. "I had let go of expectation. My feeling was: I did my job. I served my calling of creating this film, which was so important for me personally. And I felt so relieved when it was done, as if I had lost 100 kilos of weight off my life. Then I felt: now that the film is finally out, it's not up to me. It doesn't belong to me anymore. Now it belongs to the world."
