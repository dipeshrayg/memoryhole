Whether it's Alan Cumming starring in a love story about self-acceptance or a lesson in proudly embracing one's cultural background or projects examining the U.S. from an international perspective, these 10 shorts spotlight true humanity and, at times, harsh realities of today's society on screen.
"Fat"
"People who are often overlooked have entire stories behind who they are, and we just don't think about it," says writer-director Dolores Rice, whose film opens with Maeve (Liz Fitzgibbon), a shy woman in her 40s, hesitantly discussing weight-loss injections with her doctor. When the physician indicates where the jabs need to go on her body, she recoils and retreats. "Up to 46% of women struggling with their weight have been sexually assaulted as children," explains Rice. "I wanted to tell the story of overcoming this shame. Breaking this silence with hope seemed urgent and universal." Teenage Maeve (Willow McCarthy) had a life filled with freedom, dance and friendship. How does trauma derail all that? McCarthy also wrote and performed the song that closes the short, "Storm," a defiant anthem of strength: "Maeve's a dancer, and dancers' relationship to music is inherently in their bodies." Rice adds: "This embodied reclamation is what the film is dealing with."
"¡Que Suene La Banda!"
In U.S. media, "you hear a lot of Mariachi, which I love," says director and co-writer Jorge Parra Jr., "but Banda's something different." The brass-heavy Mexican music was integral to his childhood in El Monte, California, much as it is a formative part of Javier's (Anthony Gonzalez) family life in "¡Que Suene La Banda!," albeit something he's trying to distance himself from while at school, where his father works as a janitor. "Growing up, you try to fit in, right?" says Parra. "I was a first-generation American. My parents were born in Mexico, and being immersed in the culture in the U.S., you try to pick and choose what you want to show people." Gonzalez calls Parra's film a "story that needs to be told" and a lesson in not being "embarrassed or ashamed of your culture or background, or where your family comes from. Embrace it. It's a beautiful story."
"Agnes"
"The catalyst was an actual shooting at my local supermarket," says Nora Arnezeder, the film's French director and co-writer, who felt an urgency to create the short about a child-sitter merely going about her day, with a fateful trip to buy groceries. "For a couple of years, I did really have a hard time leaving my home," Arnezeder says, calling the film a "very important therapy for me to heal myself." During the entire short, the shooter remains off-screen, a specific choice that gives a non-American perspective on mass shootings in the U.S., according to producer Yoann Scherb. "In our society we have a tendency to put the perpetrators in the front," adds Arnezeder. "I really needed to focus on the victims."
"Kiloran Bay"
"Kiloran Bay," starring Alan Cumming and Jack Wolfe, "began as an experiment in the form of musical cinema" for writer-director-composer Michael Bruce, who was "interested in how musicals worked in cinema rather than on the stage." Set in the Scottish Hebrides, the film follows a man returning home for a wedding who confronts a forbidden romance he was forced to hide, in a love story about self-acceptance. "I wanted to make a queer film with a positive ending," says Bruce. "There's so much important gay cinema that is tragic, and while the film doesn't shy away from pain, it was important to me that it lands somewhere hopeful."
"Never Give Up!"
A mother's words. A stranger's voice. Swiss-Hungarian writer-director Tamás Yvan Topolánszky's short, executive produced by Matthew Modine, follows a 9-year-old Mexican boy left alone in the desert after a deadly border crossing. He clings to his mother's words - perhaps the most overused phrase in American culture: "Never give up." When those words return in the voice of one of the most recognizable - and unexpected - figures in American life, a striking contradiction emerges. Inspired by the 2015 refugee crisis in Budapest, Topolánszky explores the "stark contrast between xenophobia as a political product and the human reality of those fleeing their homes." His film poses a universal question: "Who owns a message - the person who delivers it or the person who gives it meaning?" Overcoming his own fear of heights in the process, Chema (José María Gómez ) brings a touching combination of innocence, vulnerability and quiet determination to a child facing the unimaginable.
"Safe Zone"
After 12 years as a humanitarian aid worker who experienced eight war zones from Somalia and Iraq to Syria, writer-director Ahmad Alameldeen still wanted to "contribute to the humanitarian ecosystem, but through storytelling." The short is fiction deeply rooted in fact, as an 8-year-old girl and her mother risk sniper attacks to reach airdropped food, highlighting "the topic of starvation being used in war zones as a tactic." To tell the story effectively and respectfully, Alameldeen adopted a hybrid animation approach, hiring "artists who drew the frame and built the characters" and then using AI to animate two-dimensional while still being "able to control all the movement of all the frames." "I'm seeing, of course, a lot of conversations around AI in general," he says. "For me, it's a tool. The aim is to discuss the message, about the story of the girl."
"Mary Is Missing"
"We run an arts organization in Ireland called Run of the Mill which exists to platform the talents of intellectually disabled artists," explains co-writer and director Aisling Byrne. The mystery thriller deciphers conflicting accounts of a missing careworker, with Byrne disrupting the "culture of perhaps late attachment of learning-disabled talent to projects." The story was co-authored by an ensemble of 13 actors with intellectual disabilities and developed over 12 weeks of inclusive workshops and writers' rooms. "There was something so captivating and truthful and raw and real" about the process, says producer Killian Coyle. "We would like to think that 'Mary Is Missing' is a proof of concept," explains Byrne. "Work that's ambitious, with large ensemble casts of learning-disabled performers, is absolutely possible if you're willing to consider working in a different way."
"Mine for a Moment"
Writer-director Kristian Lever describes his provocative, emotional film as a psychological drama with "a little bit of a heist feeling to it." Lever says, "I heard about this news story that had happened in Italy about a woman who disguised herself as a nurse, went into a maternity ward and took a newborn baby." The filmmaker explores the behavior and "moral ambiguity" of someone "at the brink of desperation." "I wanted to make a film where you could question the audience's morals," he says. "Can you feel compassion for someone who does something unforgivable? That was really compelling for me."
"Rule, Britannia"
"Art should be political. Art isn't just for entertainment," says Ayman Alhussein, co-writer of this powerful short film exploring attitudes towards refugees crossing the English Channel in small boats. Drawing on his own experience fleeing Syria by dinghy from Turkey to Greece before seeking asylum in the U.K., Alhussein wanted to flip the narrative and imagine what happens when everyone is in the same boat. Co-writer and director Max Fisher describes the film as a cautionary tale, conceived before vigilante activity in the English Channel made headlines. Its message now feels increasingly relevant, warning of the consequences of dehumanising refugees and blaming them for wider national problems. Shot entirely at sea, the film captures the danger and isolation of the crossing through the eyes of radicalized right-wing activists attempting to intercept a refugee boat, only to find themselves begging for help from the very people they sought to turn away.
"I Am the Prize"
The character of Anthony (Russell Tovey), a seemingly successful mentor of men, was born from a writer's program, in which writer-director Sai Karan Talwar saw a cultural shift in some of his friends. Another discomforting effect of the pandemic had isolated some, who were "becoming indoctrinated by toxic social media influences, creating this perverse war in gender dynamics." The concept originated from a television pilot script developed during Talwar's time in a writers' room program. Talwar's film skewers that projection by revealing the archetype of a con man. "If you're able to deconstruct these people and try to understand them," says Talwar, "then in doing so, you can take their power away from them."
