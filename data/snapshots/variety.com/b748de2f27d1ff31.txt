Arab women filmmakers are making their mark on the international stage, with their films earning recognition at major festivals and reaching audiences around the world. But as their visibility grows, questions remain about the opportunities available to them beyond the festival circuit, from securing financing and distribution to building sustainable careers.
Those questions are at the center of a discussion on Arab women directors and the mainstream film industry at the 47th Cairo Intl. Film Festival. For Palestinian director Laila Abbas, Egyptian producer Khouloud Sa'd and Egyptian filmmaker Amal Ramsis, the conversation goes beyond representation to examine the structures shaping which films get made, whose stories reach audiences and what it takes to sustain a career in the region.
"As Arab women filmmakers, we exist," Ramsis tells Variety. "But which kind of stories can we tell? What freedom do we have to tell our own stories?"
Ramsis challenges the idea that success should be measured primarily by recognition at major European festivals. While acknowledging their importance, she argues that filmmakers need alternative distribution networks and more direct connections with audiences.
"We don't lack films from women directors in the international festivals. We are there," she says, arguing that the conversation should focus less on numbers and more on creative freedom.
That freedom, she maintains, remains constrained by expectations about the stories Arab women should tell. "As an Arab woman filmmaker, you should talk about women as victims, you should talk about the veil," she says. "Maybe sometimes that's not the way we want to talk about our issues."
Abbas, whose debut feature "Thank You for Banking With Us!" explores gender discrimination in inheritance laws through humor, has encountered similar expectations when seeking financing.
"Sometimes we think that Europeans want to see a certain type of film coming from the Middle East," Abbas explains. "If you're outside of trauma, war, conflict, then they would not be very interested."
Yet she cautions that the reality is more complicated. Films addressing politically sensitive subjects can also deter potential backers, leaving filmmakers to navigate different expectations depending on the project.
Abbas also challenges the assumption that Arab women directors necessarily face the same barriers as their counterparts elsewhere. Pointing to the prominence of female filmmakers in Palestinian cinema, including Cherien Dabis, director of "All That's Left of You," and Annemarie Jacir, director of "Palestine 36," she argues that the region's particular circumstances deserve greater attention.
"When I look at the Arab world and I see all the initiatives revolving around cinema, whether it's labs or festivals, I see a good presence for women," she says. "I've never really felt that I'm discriminated against when it comes to a story that I've written because I'm a woman."
For Egyptian producer Sa'd, whose credits include Mohammed Hammad's independently financed El Gouna winner "Safe Exit," the growing number of women filmmakers is an encouraging development.
"There are many voices from Egyptian and Arab women in the industry now, and they have a variety of subjects and issues they want to address," she says. "They're getting more prominence, and I think it's very useful for the industry."
Yet greater visibility, she cautions, does not necessarily mean greater access to audiences. "We have many women directors. They have the quality, the topics and subjects, but distribution remains a very big problem," she explains.
Sa'd points to "Safe Exit" and "Withered Green" as examples of independent productions that offer "another vision" for an Egyptian industry she says has struggled with production and distribution in recent years. She sees self-financing as one way to create opportunities for emerging filmmakers outside of traditional production structures.
But that independence comes at a cost. "The journey of these films is very, very long," she laments, noting that self-financed projects can take five or six years to complete. "More funding would make the process shorter and easier. There's a direct relationship between money and time it takes to complete projects."
Sa'd also calls for closer cooperation between producers, distributors and sales agents to help films reach wider audiences. "We should have even more cooperation between the producers, distributors and sales agents," she says, emphasizing the importance of trust between industry players. "The producer and distributor are like a marriage."
Financing remains another concern. Abbas welcomes the proliferation of development labs across the region, which give emerging filmmakers opportunities to refine scripts, build networks and meet potential collaborators.
"These places where you actually meet people, where you make connections, where you hear feedback on your script from different people, from different places around the world," she says. "These insights are very, very important."
But she argues that the funding available to turn those projects into films remains woefully insufficient. "We're all relying on three, four funds in the region," she says, pointing to the absence of national film funds in many countries. Without financial backing from home, Arab filmmakers can find themselves at a disadvantage when approaching international co-producers. "That's not sustainable," she adds.
Ramsis identifies another gap in the industry's support structures: the difficulty of maintaining a career beyond the first or second feature. "Once you make your third film and you haven't become famous, very famous, it's very difficult to continue in this industry," she says, noting that many funding initiatives prioritize emerging talent.
Ramsis is a founder of the Between Women Filmmakers' Caravan initiative, which was established in 2008. Through this project, Ramsis has sought to build alternatives, organizing screenings, workshops and collaborative production opportunities for women across the Arab world and Latin America.
"It's not a project on women, it's a project made by women," she explains. "One of our main goals is to create this big networking between women filmmakers, to exchange experiences and to talk about our challenges in the industry."
For Abbas, the way forward also requires established filmmakers to help create opportunities for the next generation. "Reach back to filmmakers, create a fund for new talent. Do something," she urges.
The challenge, the industry veterans agree, is not simply getting Arab women filmmakers into the mainstream, but ensuring they have the creative freedom, resources and industry support to remain there.
