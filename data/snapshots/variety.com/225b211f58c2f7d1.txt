How far have the Country Music Association Awards come from their inception in 1967 to this year's 60th annual telecast? Outgoing CMA CEO Sarah Trahern, who retires on Dec. 31, has a better idea than most. When she first took the reins of the leadership toward the end of 2014, she dug into the organization's archives to find DVDs that had been kept of the 46 CMA Awards shows that had gone down up to that point, and binge-watched them over the holidays. (There was one gap, the 1969 show, which was lost to history.)
"It was fun to watch the really early shows with Johnny Cash hosting, where you would see him take a drag on his cigarette during the commercial breaks," she laughs. Smoking aside, those were more innocent advertorial days. "We were sponsored by Kraft in the first years, and they would toss it at the break to someone to do a macaroni and cheese recipe, or what you can put on your Thanksgiving table. So I was watching that like, 'Man, we've come a long way.'" Watching the old telecast DVDs, she also caught up on some of the shows' more legendary moments. "I had never before seen the famous moment in 1975 when Charlie Rich, who was presenting for Entertainer of the Year, burned the envelope when John Denver won." Rich's open diss of Denver represented a tension that hasn't completely gone away since then. "You know," Trahern muses, "it's appropriate that every now and then things move to pop and the traditionalists get upset."
Where are we in that dynamic in 2026? In kind of a pleasantly strange place where there is a very traditional-sounding country song, Ella Langley's "Choosin' Texas," that happens to be a pop crossover smash, and the biggest all-genre song of the year, by any measure. On top of the record-breaking 25-week run that "Choosin' Texas" has had atop the Billboard Hot 100, the song is inevitably the 500-pound gorilla in this year's CMA nods, which sees Langley going into the telecast with a leading nine nominations.
Fortunately, Langley is no one-woman pack, but just the current leading edge of a wave of women that have dominated the awards in recent years. "what a wonderful year that Ella has had, and we're all proud to be able to be on her coattails as she's had growth all around the world," Trahern says - "in the same way that Lainey Wilson has really stepped out as our host, and we're so excited she's back for the third year. The fact that we have these monster female artists in the format right now is exciting. "So many of our top nominated people, from Ella to Kacey (Musgraves), are marching to their own drummers and writing their own stuff and taking it to different eras. To have Miranda (Lambert) showing up not just as an artist and female vocalist, but as a producer and writer too (for her work on Langley's recordings), is kind of exciting. And Taylor coming back," with Swift, the world's biggest pop artist not only nominated for her return to a country sound with "I Knew It, I Knew You" but promising to not just be in attendance for the first time in a decade but perform on the telecast for the first time in 13 years.
The 60th annual show, which will air live on ABC and Disney+ and have next-day viewing on Hulu, has a lot to live up to. Trahern can think of a lot more highlights from the telecast's 21st century airings that she did not have to go back to the archival DVDs to catch up on.
"The first time I got to go sit in the house for the CMA Awards was 2001 when Alan Jackson did 'Where Were You (When the World Stopped Turning?),' and anytime I hear that song, I get transported back to the Opry House that night and all of our jaws dropping at the power of that performance." That song remains the best-remembered 9/11 memorial in any genre. She adds, "I felt that same emotion when we did the Route 91 tribute with Carrie Underwood" in 2017, following the gun massacre at a festival in Las Vegas. On that occasion, Underwood sang the hymn "Softly and Tenderly," captured in a single steadicam shot, with photos of the country fans who perished in the tragedy on screens behind her.
"Those are certainly those poignant memories, but I also think about rocking, up-tempo moments," Trahern continues. She singles out the collaboration of Beyoncé and the Chicks in 2016, when they did a mashup of "Daddy Issues" and "Long Time Gone." As collabs go, that one might be rivaled only by the 2015 duet between Justin Timberlake and Chris Stapleton of "Tennessee Whiskey" that made the latter up-and-comer an overight sensation, in one of the few instances in which we can use that term literally.
She can cite star-making appearances from the '90s and the present day in a single breath. "I think of Stephen Wilson Jr.'s performance last year, when he did 'Stand By Me' just with his guitar, one of those moments when people kinda paid attention in a different way and went, 'Oh.' Or I think back to Mary Chapin Carpenter in the '90s, when she barely had a couple of songs out when she did 'The Opening Act,' and the parlor game in the industry immediately became: 'Who's it about?' Everyone could laugh and relate to having that story happen to them with some artist. That's the magic of a show like this where someone can come out of the blue."
A strong, spontaneous-seeming speech can historically have almost as much impact as a great performance. Trahern recalls "the time that Taylor won Horizon [aka best new artist], and said it was a highlight of her senior year. Or when Deana Carter jumped into Ricky Skaggs' lap. Or Zach Top taking his beer up on stage last year because he forgot he had it. There are those pop culture moments you can't predict till they're happening in the show. But the musical stuff - Alan; Beyoncé and the Chicks; Stapleton and JT - those are the things that I really hold dear." There's a pause, and then a promise she makes, as a quick, modest aside: "We'll have a couple of those this year."
Nov. 18 will mark the last time Trahern oversees the airing of the CMA Awards, but this year will also mark a significant first. It's the first time the show will be simulcast on a streaming service, as well as its home base on ABC. That might seem overdue, but it's the outgrowth of the association reupping its deal with Disney for 2027 and beyond, and then getting a head start on some of its provisions.
"We had the opportunity this year to talk to a lot of different networks in our negotiation process, and when we set out on that at the beginning of the year, some of my goals were to make sure that our new long-term TV partnership met where the industry is going," Trahern says. That didn't involve anything so extreme as the rival ACM Awards making an exclusive deal with Amazon Prime. But "having a simulcast in a way that we can reach both the traditional viewers and the streaming viewers was number one on our negotiation team's list. We didn't necessarily know where that was gonna lead us, but to be back in the Disney family with ABC for our linear and Disney+ for the simulcast is particularly exciting. That deal that we negotiated starts next year, but we're able to pull that forward so that we can stream, or simulcast, for the first time this year. That unlocks a whole new generation of viewers who don't have linear television at home, and we're really excited with this new partnership and to be able to bring the show to millions of new and ideally younger fans who are consuming music today."
One issue they faced specifically looking ahead to this year's telecast was the Dolly of it all. The CMAs had a potentially difficult dance in front of them, with how to salute her on the Nov. 18 show. Devote anything less than about 45 minutes of the three hours to eulogizing Dolly Parton, and folks would complain. And perhaps they still will. But the org and ABC preempted at least some of that by giving Parton her own separate two-hour tribute special on Oct. 21.
"Certainly we will be honoring Dolly again in the awards show, but I think if we weren't doing the special, we would be giving more time to Dolly in the awards show, and there's so many other great things to celebrate this year that I'm glad it worked out the way it did." But there's no denying it happened in more of a hurry than they might have liked - with what Trahern describes as "a couple of weeks' notice." One of the main motivators was fear that others would get to a Parton paean first and do something less worthy of the memory of country's biggest superstar. "The day before I went to pitch it to the network, I looked our senior staff in the eye and said, 'I know I'm getting ready to throw a lot of extra work on your plate, but I really feel strongly that this is what we should do and that we'd all feel awful if somebody else were doing it.'"
That made Trahern's plate in her final months with the CMA fuller than usual, and it was already pretty full. A couple of weeks prior to our conversation, there was one of the CMA's more altruistic evenings, Teachers of Excellence, in which celebrities show up to honor music teachers from around the country. A few nights before we speak, she was overseeing the taping of the annual CMA Christmas special - less than a week before the live broadcast of the Dolly special. The time crunch left them with a cricual decision on the Dolly special. "We had a discussion with the network as we were working on the creative where they said we could do an hour-long show that was taped or a two-hour show that was live. I'm sure our producer, Robert Deaton, some days might have wanted me to go for the taped show. But I love live-live. I think there's something that really makes it like a sporting event."
Hearing that sentiment, it may not come as a surprise to anyone who didn't already know that Trahern's background prior to joining the CMA in 2014 was in television, including stints with CMT and the late TNN and GAC that had her interfacting with the org she came to lead for many years before she arrived there. "CMA Fest is always fun, and it's music discovery and I can't wait to come do it," she says, speaking of the org's other ulta-busy season, in June, when they produce the biggest country music festival in the world. But when it comes to where her heart really is, "I've always been a sucker for the awards show. I grew up as a little girl watching it on television, and I remember in the '70s tying my hairbrush to my music stand so I could have my microphone to accept my awards."
Her successor, Brittany Schaffer, will take on the head role Jan. 1, but officially came on board at the beginning of September in part to learn the ropes from Trahern. Of course, it's understood that, coming from a very different background, there are things Schaffer could teach her predecessor.
"When I came on board in '14, we didn't have social media. Now we have three people on our staff that that's their full-time job," points out Trahern. "How we reach fans is different than it was. We still have a strong contingency on our board in radio, and still support what they do through our media remotes. But at the media remotes, we also now have Spotify and Amazon and YouTube and others as the consumption channels have changed. ... It's time for me to just wrap the organization up with a bow and give it to Brittany for her chapter, and I'm really excited to see where she's gonna take it and grow over the next few years."
Trahern was not part of the search committee that selected Schaffer, as "it's good governance that the (outgoing) CEO not be in the mix. But I'll tell you, working alongside her, I love that she loves the history of the business. She loves the new artists, and because of her Spotify experience, has an extensive background in digital consumption, which is where the business is going, just like I had my experience with analog television and cable. So, you know, it's the right thing to have somebody from that new generation in the chair, and I'm very excited for her and for the CMA."
Not everything the CMA does is tied into a television experience. Another thing they just were involved in was the annual induction ceremony for the Country Music Hall of Fame, which is not televised. (The Hall of Fame puts on the induction, but the CMA chooses who goes into the hall.)
The night before we spoke, Trahern had been at a party for inductees and their families and guests. Tim McGraw approached her for a hug, and then she watched him walk by himself into the rotunda. "He was reading every plaque, and looking at where his plaque will hang next year. And I think when you get to see that moment when they realize what this means, that they will be on those walls forever, when their children's children are around... We're really fortunate to get to be the shepherds of this process and to be one step along the way in what is a historic career." McGraw's CMHOF induction will be further celebrated on the upcoming telecast with a tribute; Brooks & Dunn will also get a starry salute, as the CMA's lifetime achievement honorees.
Earlier in our talk, Trahern swore she wasn't feeling unduly sentimental about leaving the CMA. By the end, she's changing her tune. "I think the most emotional I'm gonna be this year will be award show night at 10 o'clock (CT)," she says. "Not because I'm sad to go, because it's the right thing to go on and have a rich chapter myself, but because I'm so proud to have been a part of this, and I just love that team that pulls everything together. So thanks a lot. You just put me in a moment. I thought all I had to worry about was my dress. Now I'm gonna have to worry about my dress and my mascara."
