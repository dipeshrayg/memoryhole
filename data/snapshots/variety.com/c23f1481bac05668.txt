In April of this year, the 19th installment of Record Store Day was the event's most successful ever, but Nov. 27 brings another opportunity for music junkies to get their vinyl on: Record Store Day Black Friday.
While the O.G. Record Store Day was conceived as a general celebration of independent record stores, and features limited-edition, in-store only releases, the Black Friday edition exists for a different reason: to give those indie stores a slice of the "biggest shopping day of the year" pie. That's also why the vinyl releases you'll find making their debuts on Black Friday are more likely to remain there for the rest of the holiday season, and why many stores will make them available on their websites starting on Saturday the 28th.
The concept of hyping local independent record stores as a must for holiday shopping may sound rather obvious, but RSD co-founder Michael Kurtz says that, at first, he was opposed to the idea, which his wife came up with.
"I was not on board with it," he admits. "My initial response at the time was, 'Ah, that's for big-box stores and selling cheap toasters and stuff.'" But eventually, he came around, thinking they could do "a little bit smaller version" for the holidays. RSD Black Friday does feature fewer releases overall, but its main goal is to get customers through the door. "Most record stores do about two months' worth of business" that day, Kurtz says.
For Black Friday, Kurtz says what you'll find in the racks is largely thanks to what he calls a "Secret Squirrel group" of about 20 record store owners nationwide, who help winnow down the overwhelming numbers of releases pitched by artists and record labels.
"They've been around forever; they run iconic record stores," Kurtz explains of this crate-digging cabal. "And I go to them and say, 'Hey, do you think record stores would want to have this?' Because Black Friday is not the same as Record Store Day. It's not supposed to be just a limited thing only. It's also supposed to be things that record stores want to have in their bins year-round."
But what's true for both events is that they feature releases from a dizzying array of artists across multiple genres. That big tent approach, Kurtz reveals, was inspired by Metallica bassist Robert Trujillo, who early on convinced Kurtz to do a vinyl release by iconic jazz bassist Jaco Pastorius.
"It went to No. 1 on Billboard's jazz chart," Kurtz recalls. "And that made us rethink: 'Oh, geez, we could do jazz records, we could do bluegrass records, we could do soul records, R&B, funk."
Of course, rock in its various forms is still a huge focus of both Record Store Day and RSD Black Friday, but in recent years, pop artists have climbed on board the vinyl train en masse, resulting in a surprising boom in customers who are too young to even remember a world where vinyl was once the only option.
Kurtz says, "The majority of people who come to Record Store Day now and participate are younger than the age of 35, and slightly more female than male." Of course, you can credit the biggest pop star in the world for that.
While Kurtz says Billie Eilish's 7-inch releases for RSD "sort of cracked the door open" for that demographic, he notes, "It got blown wide open with Taylor Swift. When she did her 'Long Pond Studio Sessions' record with us exclusively for record stores [in 2023], it changed everything." That album became the first Record Store Day exclusive release ever to hit the top 10 of theBillboard200, proving that brand-new releases on vinyl could do just as well as streaming titles.
For RSD Black Friday 2026, Kurtz predicts that it'll be Noah Kahan's vinyls getting the youth through the door. "He's got a special 7-inch that was done just for record stores that's very very cool: It's tied in with the 'Hunger Games' soundtrack," says Kurtz, referring to "Draw You Out," Kahan's contribution to "The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping - The Album." "And then he's also got an additional record that'll be highly sought after." That would be "The Last of the Bugs," which collects the four tracks Kahan added to the expanded edition of his album"The Great Divide."
Kurtz is also excited about a Bruce Springsteen live box set, the American version of The Beatles' Rubber Soul LP on gold vinyl, a live album by Billy Joel, a disc of the Doors' studio sessions and a 2-LP set by Ween "for all the pot smokers out there."
He also predicts the Miles Davis, Jerry Garcia Band and Grateful Dead releases will be popular, as well as the original, previously unreleased version of Joan Jett's "I Love Rock & Roll" album and not one but three albums by Japanese guitar virtuoso Masayoshi Takanaka.
Kurtz says he doesn't even mind that many of those younger customers are buying vinyl not to play, but to display.
"Y'know, I was originally freaked out about that because I'm a music person, but I kind of get it," he tells me. "Like, I will get the Beatles' 'Rubber Soul' reissue this year and I probably will never open it. I just want it because it's so beautiful, and to know that I have one of 15,000 copies that will ever be printed ... I want to keep it pristine."
Despite admitting to being "completely anxiety-ridden" each year over how successful each Record Store Day event will be, Kurtz is looking forward to the event's 20th anniversary next year. He and co-founder Carrie Colliton would love to mark the milestone with live performances and some kind of art installation featuring past Record Store Day releases. (A copy of each of those limited releases is currently stored in a warehouse in Raleigh, North Carolina, in case you might want to pull off the vinyl equivalent of robbing Fort Knox.) But for right now, Kurtz notes, those plans are "dreams right now. They're not reality."
Still, the failure he fears is unlikely. Nearly 20 years into an event which has helped drive pop culture back to the vinyl age, Kurtz says there's no sign of its popularity tapering off. As he notes, "demand for [vinyl] and the excitement around it is completely still there - even more so, I think." But, he acknowledges, "The only thing that's a bit alarming is how expensive it's becoming" - a reflection of the rising cost of shipping due to increases in gas and diesel.
"It's stunning for me as a person who's ridden this tiger called Record Store Day for almost 20 years to see it from the beginning: When we first started we were the only ones doing new vinyl, and in the used bins you could find [high quality copies of] Led Zeppelin or the Beatles or Pink Floyd for $3, $5 - those days are over."
Kurtz says it was interesting working with shops in the early days, and still can be today - because they're independent in all sorts of ways, including what they prefer to emphasize in their stores and sometimes think RSD should focus on as a whole.
"The most difficult thing about putting it together was working with record store owners, because they're a very creative bunch," he says. "They're very different; they're not people who typically play by the rules. They like to do things their way. So to try to get them all together to do this was very difficult." He adds, "I was in London once and there was a record store owner who just just raked me over the coals because we'd done the Taylor Swift record. I was like, 'What records do you like?' He's like, 'Yes, Black Sabbath, Deep Purple ...' and I said, 'OK I get it. That's what you love and what you're passionate about, but there's another generation.' And so it's working with them and not alienating them, because sometimes I do that by accident."
