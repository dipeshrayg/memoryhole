Japan's production committee system, in which a group of companies jointly finance a film and share its rights, has helped spread risk across the industry. But it can also cap budgets at what the industry itself can raise, make it harder for overseas investors to take part and slow decisions when every rights holder must agree.
At a panel at TIFFCOM, the content market of the Tokyo International Film Festival, speakers examined whether film funds can ease those limits while still fitting Japan's market.
"Film Funds in Japan: What Works, What Doesn't, and What Comes Next" featured Kii Muneyuki, CEO and producer of K2 Pictures, which works with filmmakers including Kore-eda Hirokazu, Miike Takashi and Nishikawa Miwa; Ibe Tomonobu, CEO of Questry, a Tokyo startup that uses digital securities to fund anime and other Japanese content; Nakagawa Keita, managing director at MUFG Bank; and Motoyanagi Yusuke, partner at law firm Nishimura & Asahi.
Kii presented K2P Film Fund I as one example of a new structure. The fund closed in February at about JPY5 billion ($32 million) and aims to back around 50 films over seven years. It is a blind pool, meaning investors commit money before the films are chosen. Its investors include MUFG, the Development Bank of Japan and other financial institutions and companies, and a greenlight committee oversees project approvals. Kii said five films had been released in theaters in 2026.
Kii said a central question for film funds is how to return revenue to creators, including income from streaming and other windows after a film's theatrical run. He also said it is hard to rely on overseas box office, and that Japanese companies need to build businesses beyond distribution.
The panel compared financing structures in the U.S. and South Korea. In the U.S., producers combine presales with completion guarantees to help secure bank loans. A completion guarantee covers delivery of a film on budget and on schedule, rather than its commercial performance. South Korea relies on public-private support, with government-backed guarantees helping projects get bank financing.
Nakagawa presented a Japan-specific feasibility study commissioned by the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry. MUFG was selected to carry it out on March 31. Running through February 2027, the study will use real-world data to develop valuation and completion-guarantee models, and test the full process from application and review to contract drafting. The study assesses feasibility and does not guarantee that financing or completion guarantees will become available.
The discussion closed with four priorities: regulation, capital, creators and cross-border business. These include clarifying which regulations should be expanded and when, combining debt with subsidies and completion guarantees, and building creator returns into fund structures. Speakers also pointed to language and tax hurdles to cross-border investment, including "permanent establishment" rules that can make international investors liable for Japanese tax.
