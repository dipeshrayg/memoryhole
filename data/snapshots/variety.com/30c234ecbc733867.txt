A former cardiac surgeon's pursuit of a world record in spearfishing, a series immersing viewers in the centuries-old tradition of falconry ... the second edition of the Doha Film Festival offers a glimpse into the ambitions and range of storytelling emerging from Qatar's burgeoning film and television industry.
Having dedicated its Made in Qatar strand exclusively to short films last year, the festival has retained its shorts competition while expanding the program to include a separate, noncompetitive showcase of four feature films and an original documentary series.
"I believe this is the golden era to begin," said Qatari filmmaker Jamal Al Khanji ("Jodari Meno") during a press conference. Citing the support offered by the Doha Film Institute, Media City Qatar and Al Jazeera Documentary, he added, "You just need to have the courage to take that step."
Filmmaker Jassim Al Romaihi ("Elzam") acknowledged that Gulf filmmakers have access to resources unavailable to many of their counterparts elsewhere, but stressed that such opportunities come with considerable responsibility.
"It is a challenge to be of the first generation of the cinematic sector," he said. "There are massive companies that have decades of experience before you. We have the responsibility of achieving success."
For Al Khanji, financial privilege should not overshadow the diversity of experiences and stories the region has to offer. "Of course, we might be privileged financially, but we do have other challenges," he noted. "We need to have stories that are humane, that are based in our heritage."
Ensuring that filmmaking becomes a sustainable career for subsequent generations is also a priority for Ahmed Al Baker, CEO of Qatar's Katara Studios and producer of "Elzam," who emphasized the responsibility of established industry professionals to pay it forward, supporting emerging talent, even when doing so does not make immediate business sense.
"We always have to extend a hand to up-and-coming filmmakers, by trying to support them to the best of our abilities," he said, citing providing in-kind services and advice as examples of those efforts. "I just hope if this continues, we will be able to create a very special movement of content creation."
Below, Variety takes a closer look at the five projects featured in this year's Made in Qatar Special Screenings lineup.
"Jodari Meno" (Jamal Al Khanji)
A physician and former cardiac surgeon, Al Khanji travels from Qatar to Zanzibar and Italy in pursuit of a world record in spearfishing, attempting to land a dogtooth tuna. What began as a documentary about his sporting ambitions evolved over five years into a deeply personal exploration of fatherhood, requiring him to explore and expose aspects of his private life he had never intended to share at the outset.
"The hardest thing for me was that I was the main character, the protagonist, and so I had to shed light on the familial and personal aspects of my life," he said. "That was difficult, especially since it doesn't pertain to just me. It pertains to my siblings, my wife and my family."
A turning point came when the filmmaker found himself questioning remarks he had made on camera about his late father. "Was I fair? Was this harsh? Was I fair to him, to his memory?" Al Khanji recalls. "Part of me wanted to talk more about this. One part of me wanted to change the whole subject and didn't want to talk about this at all."
Reexamining his relationship with his father ultimately prompted the filmmaker to reconsider his own parenting and how he communicates his love to his children. "It was a point of revelation because it definitely changed me as a human being. I started asking questions that I never asked myself before," reflects Al Khanji. "For me, it was a healing journey. It was a change process. It was a big shock in a very quiet way."
"When the News Breaks You" (Hamad Al Hajri)
Drawing on testimonies from more than 30 journalists, Al Hajri's documentary examines the psychological toll of reporting from conflict zones, focusing on the experiences and sacrifices of Arab journalists documenting events unfolding in their own communities.
"You cannot be a great journalist if you are not a human first," Al Hajri tells Variety. "That's why they fight for the truth, that's why they give their lives - to document this history to the world."
For the filmmaker, the project is also a response to what he sees as a double standard in the international recognition afforded to Arab journalists in comparison to their Western counterparts. "I think it's my responsibility as an Arab filmmaker to present our stories because we are the right voice for our people," Al Hajri says passionately. "We feel their pain, it's our pain. Their loss is our loss."
Ultimately, the director hopes the documentary will reach audiences far beyond the region, allowing them to better understand and empathize with the people behind the headlines. "I want them to understand us and to hear directly from us, not from someone else," he insists. "We are human and we have rights, whether you like it or not."
"Elzam" (Jassim Al Romaihi, Ahmed Al Baker)
A decade after an earlier television iteration aired on Al Jazeera Documentary and Qatar TV, "Elzam" returns in an expanded format as an eight-part documentary series following falconers both on hunting expeditions and in their everyday lives.
Rather than adopting a conventional documentary format, the Katara Studios production immerses viewers in the action, allowing the participants' interactions to reveal the intricacies of a tradition passed down through generations.
"We feel that a lot of our culture is not captured, and we feel that this is our responsibility," Al Baker explains. "We have the means. We have the know-how. We have the tools. Why aren't we capturing these moments?"
For Al Romaihi, raising the production value of the series was a means of bringing a story deeply rooted in Gulf heritage to a wider audience. "This is an aspect of our culture that has been part of our identity for generations," he says. "It's not about really revisiting it. It's about just shedding light on it."
The series has been acquired by UAE-based streaming platform Yango Play in partnership with Qatar's Film Committee and is set to launch in January 2027.
"First to Fly" (Rodrigo Vazquez Salessi)
Argentine filmmaker Rodrigo Vazquez Salessi follows two young rhythmic gymnasts from Syria and Madagascar training under Russian coach Tatiana Yakimova in Qatar as they prepare to become the first gymnasts in their discipline to represent their respective countries at the sport's world championships.
"In a way, they were all pioneers because the Russian coach brought rhythmic gymnastics to Qatar and started the first club ever here," Vazquez Salessi points out. "They were making history. So I thought, well, we must make a film about it."
Beyond the sporting ambitions of its protagonists, the documentary explores the complexities of adolescence, immigration and family relationships while delving into the physical and psychological pressures placed on young female athletes, highlighting issues such as bulimia.
"When you are a teenager and a woman, your body goes through a lot of change," he notes. "And when you have to compete at the professional level, what does this mean in terms of your health?"
"A Very Ordinary Life" (Muhammed Deniz)
Bringing together Qatar and Morocco, Deniz's fiction feature follows a retired Qatari pilot and a Moroccan art teacher who, unbeknownst to one another, share a connection to a buried family secret. Moving between the present and the early 1990s, the film explores the lasting repercussions of a romance interrupted by conflict and distance.
"I do not see the unfinished as failure, or absence as a void to be filled, but as one of the most human and honest forms of life," Deniz says in a statement on the film's official website.
The Qatar-Morocco co-production, produced by Abdulrahim Al-Siddiqi and Bouchra Malek, is the sole fiction feature in the section.
